# Escalation matrix

Agree, before anything goes wrong, who answers each kind of problem the team can't close itself: how severe it is, who responds first and by when, when it goes up a level and to whom, and how the answer gets back. The ladder is checked for you: levels with no name, gaps, times that run backwards, and urgent problems answered more slowly than routine ones.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

An escalation matrix is a set of ladders, one for each kind of problem a team can't always close on its own. Each ladder says who is called first, by when they must be there, and the point at which, if it isn't solved, the next person up is called, and so on to the top. Problems are sorted by severity, defined by what they do (someone could be hurt, bad parts are being made) rather than by how long they've been open, because the severity has to be clear the moment the problem appears. All the times count from when the problem was raised, so the ladder reads like a clock: the booth lead is there by minute 10, it goes up at minute 30, the next level is there by minute 45. And every ladder ends with how the answer gets back to the person who raised it; without that, people stop raising problems. The matrix earns its keep in the minutes after something goes wrong, when nobody should have to work out whom to call, and again when it's written, because writing it shows the posts nobody fills and the times that don't fit together.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Problems sit unsolved because the person who spots them doesn't know whom to call, or calls the wrong person first.
- The same few people get called for everything, while others never hear about problems they should.
- A team is starting an andon, a problem board or tiered huddles and needs to say what happens after the signal.
- A serious problem waited hours before anyone senior knew, and nobody can say where it stalled.
- Shifts, rotas or reporting lines have changed and the old who-to-call list no longer matches the people.

Not when:

- The problem is one the team at the line can fix every time. Put the fix in the work instruction instead of a ladder.
- You need to find why a problem keeps coming back. The matrix gets the right person there; a 5-Whys or an A3 finds the cause.
- You want to decide who owns each task or decision day to day. That's a RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart; the matrix covers what happens when something goes wrong.
- Nobody will answer the calls it lists. A matrix with names but no agreement from the people named is a list, not a plan.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Say which area, shifts and hours the matrix covers. A ladder that works on day shift may have nobody on it at night, and the matrix should show that rather than hide it.
- **severities** — List three or four severities, most severe first, each defined by its impact: on people, on parts, on customers. "Someone could be hurt, or the line has stopped" can be judged in the moment; "open more than four hours" can't, because it describes the response, not the problem.
- **problems** — List the kinds of problem that need more than the team at the line: equipment stopped, bad parts, short of parts for a shipment, safety. Give each its severity and say who raises it and how: the andon, a radio call, a phone call, the board. One block per problem type; five to ten covers most areas.
- **levels** — For each problem, fill its ladder one level at a time: the role, who holds it today (or where the rota is), the minute they must be there by, and the minute it goes up to the next level if it isn't solved. Count every time from when the problem was raised, not from when that level was called. The top level has no time to go up. Two to four levels is usual; name the people, and agree it with them.
- **answerBack** — Say how the answer gets back to whoever raised it, and where it's recorded: "the technician writes cause and fix on the board and the lead reads it out at the next huddle". This is the step most matrices leave out, and the one that keeps people raising problems.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. Each problem is checked for a missing severity or first responder; a level with a role but no name, or a name but no role; a level with no response time or no time to call the next; a time to go up with nobody above; a ladder that stops at level 1; times that run backwards; a first response slower than for a less severe problem; and no way for the answer to get back. Settle each flag with the people named, and write what was agreed against the problem.
- **nextStep** — Write the changes agreed, when the matrix goes up where the problems are raised, and when you'll test it: a practice call on each shift, timed, shows whether the ladder works before a real problem does.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining severity by how long the problem has been open.** — "Critical: no response in four hours" can only be known after four hours of waiting, and it mixes up the problem with the response. Define severity by impact, so it's clear the moment the problem appears.
- **Writing each level's time from when that level was called.** — Then the times can't be compared and the ladder can't be checked; in the example the maintenance lead's 15 minutes came before the call at 20. Count every time from when the problem was raised.
- **Naming a role whose post is empty, or a team instead of a person.** — "Quality engineer" is no help if nobody holds the job. Name who covers it today, or where the rota is, and change it when people change.
- **A ladder with nobody above level 1.** — If the first responder can't fix it, the problem waits while people work out whom to call next, which is the gap the matrix exists to close.
- **No way for the answer to get back.** — If the person who raised a problem never hears what was done, they stop raising problems, and the matrix goes quiet while the problems don't.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Daily management huddle** (`daily-huddle`) — Problems raised at the huddle that the team can't close go up the ladder; the answer comes back at the next huddle.
- **Tiered huddle board** (`tiered-huddle-board`) — Tiered huddles carry problems up on a daily rhythm; the matrix covers the problems that can't wait for tomorrow's meeting.
- **RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart** (`raci-chart`) — The chart says who owns each decision day to day; the matrix's ladders should send a problem to the same owners.

Downstream:

- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Once the problem is contained, the fix that stops it coming back goes in the log with an owner and a date.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — A problem that keeps climbing the same ladder needs its cause found, not a faster response.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding how severe each kind of problem is
- Choosing who answers at each level, and agreeing it with them
- Deciding how fast is fast enough

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting gaps: unnamed levels, missing times, ladders that stop at level 1
- Spotting times that run backwards or urgent problems answered more slowly than routine ones
- Turning notes from a real incident into the ladder it should have followed

Drudgery — automated:

- Checking every ladder's times against each other
- Keeping names in step with rotas and posts
- Laying out the matrix and exporting it as a workbook
