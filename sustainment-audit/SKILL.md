# Sustainment audit

Weeks after a change went in, go back and check it held: each result measured against its baseline and target, and each new practice checked at the work and rated holding, drifting or lapsed, with what was seen. Whatever slipped gets a reason, a countermeasure, an owner and a date, and the next audit is booked. The gain kept, score and slip list are computed.

Family: Kaizen events · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

Most changes look fine on the day they go in. The test is a month or two later, when the people who made them have moved on to the next problem. A sustainment audit is that test, done on purpose. You go back to where the work happens and answer two questions from what you see. First, did the result hold? Measure each result the change was meant to move, the same way its baseline was measured, and see how much of the planned gain is still there. Second, is the new way still how the work is done? Take each practice the change put in place and check it at the work, by watching it or checking the records, then rate it: holding (done the new way every time you checked), drifting (some of the time, or only partly) or lapsed (back to the old way). The two questions explain each other. Practices slipping while the result still looks good is an early warning. A result slipping while every practice holds means the practices weren't what drove it. For everything that slipped you find out why, agree a countermeasure with an owner and a date, and book the next audit before you leave.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A change went in a month or more ago (from an A3, a kaizen event or a project) and nobody has checked since whether it stuck.
- A result that improved is creeping back and you want to know which part of the change has slipped.
- You're about to copy a change to another line, team or site and want to know it held where it started.
- A follow-up check was promised at the report-out (30, 60 or 90 days) and it's due.

Not when:

- The change went in last week. Under about four weeks people are still paying attention to it, so the audit tells you little; wait.
- You want to know whether one person does a job the way the written standard says. Watch them against it step by step with a standard work audit.
- There's no clear list of what the change put in place. Go back to the A3 or the event report and write the practices down first, or the audit will check impressions.
- The aim is to find who to blame for a slip. People hide slips from an audit used that way, and the next one finds nothing.

## How to fill it in

- **change** — Say what was changed and where it came from: the A3, event or project, so anyone can find the detail. Write what it was meant to deliver, the date it went live and today's date; the days since the change are worked out for you. If changes went in on different dates, count from the last one and say so.
- **results** — List the result measures the change was meant to move, usually one to three, each with its baseline (before the change) and target. Measure each one now the same way the baseline was measured, with the same definition and source, and say which period you used. Gain kept is worked out for you: how much of the planned move from baseline to target is still there. 100% or more is held, above 0 is partly held, and 0 or below means it's back where it started or worse.
- **practices** — List what the change put in place, one practice per row, each written so you can check it by looking: "small-batch orders wait for the two changeover slots", not "sequencing improved". Take them from the countermeasures, the updated standard work and the training that came out of the change. Note where each came from. Five to ten practices is usually enough.
- **evidence** — Go to the work and check each practice. Watch it being done or check the records; asking someone is the weakest check, because people describe the standard rather than what they do. Write what you saw: where, when, how many times, and what was different. Then rate it: Holding if it was done the new way every time you checked, Drifting if some of the time or only partly, Lapsed if it's back to the old way.
- **slips** — For each practice drifting or lapsed, ask the people doing the work why, and write the reason as a fact about the process ("rush requests come straight to the booth"), not a judgement about a person. Agree a countermeasure that deals with that reason, then name one owner and a due date. A countermeasure of "remind everyone" usually means the reason hasn't been found yet.
- **score** — Nothing to enter: the score is the share of full marks across the rated practices (holding 2, drifting 1, lapsed 0), with counts of each rating, the slip list, and how many results held. Each practice gets a check that flags no rating, no evidence, checked only by asking, or a slip with no reason, countermeasure, owner or date. The score is for comparing this audit with the next one, not for ranking teams.
- **nextAudit** — Book the next audit before you leave, after the countermeasures are due, and write a short summary: what held, what slipped, what happens now. Keep auditing until the practices have held through two audits in a row, then hand them to the team's normal checks.

## What good looks like

The example below is the 30-day check on the Line 2 paint booth changes from the 10–12 March kaizen event, written up 35 days after it, on 16 April: the two-slot sequencing rule and queue board from the on-time shipment A3, and the routine card, cleaning fixture and changeover log from the event. The plant manager checked seven practices at the booth: two are holding, four are drifting and one has lapsed, a score of 57%. On-time shipments have kept 81% of the planned gain (94% against a baseline of 81% and a target of 97%), partly held. Changeover time has kept none (46 minutes against a 45-minute baseline and a 25-minute target), so it is not held. The audit shows why: the cleaning fixture fits only one of the two gun models, and the two shifts clean in a different order. Each of the five slips has a reason, a countermeasure, an owner and a date, and the next audit is booked for the 90-day check on 10 June, 55 days later, once every countermeasure is due.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking the practices from the office.** — Asking "is the rule still being followed?" gets a yes. Watching the work or reading the log gets the truth. A rating with no evidence behind it is an opinion.
- **Checking the practices but not the result, or the result but not the practices.** — Either one alone misleads. Practices can hold while the result slips because they weren't what drove it; the result can hold for a while after the practices have slipped. You need both to know what to fix.
- **Rating a practice holding because it mostly is.** — "Mostly" is drifting. Calling it holding hides the slip until it becomes a lapse, which is harder to recover.
- **Answering every slip with a reminder.** — If people needed reminding, something about the work made the old way easier. Find that reason and change it, or the next audit finds the same slip.
- **Auditing once and stopping.** — One good audit shows the change held for one more month. Book the next audit every time until the practices hold twice in a row.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — The A3's countermeasures, follow-up actions and target are the practices and results this audit checks.
- **Kaizen event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — The charter's metrics (baseline, target, how measured) are the results to re-measure here, and its follow-up checks say when to audit.
- **Kaizen event report-out** (`kaizen-event-report-out`) — The report-out lists what the event changed, the result it reported and its open actions. Audit those changes, re-measure those results, and check whether the open actions closed; take the readings and new actions back to the report-out.
- **Run chart** (`run-chart`) — A run chart with the change marked shows whether a result has held: still shifted against the old baseline, or drifting back. Bring it to the audit as the evidence for each result.
- **Quick changeover** (`quick-changeover`) — After a changeover event, watch a changeover on each shift against the new card: a change that never reached the card is the usual reason the time slips back.

Downstream:

- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Each slip's countermeasure, owner and date goes on the team's countermeasure log, so it is followed up at the huddle and not only at the next audit.
- **Improvement kata storyboard** (`improvement-kata-storyboard`) — When a result hasn't held and the reason isn't clear, work it in small experiments on a storyboard instead of guessing a fix.
- **Standard work audit** (`standard-work-audit`) — When a practice is written up as a standard, a standard work audit checks it step by step, with the cause of each gap.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Rating each practice from what was seen
- Deciding why a practice slipped, with the people who do the work
- Choosing the countermeasure and who owns it

Analysis — AI helps:

- Turning an A3 or event report into a list of checkable practices
- Spotting when results and practices tell different stories
- Comparing this audit with the last one to see what keeps slipping

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out the gain kept, the score and the slip list
- Counting days since the change and to the next audit
- Laying the audit out and exporting it as a workbook
