# Spaghetti diagram: walking and carrying paths

Follow one person, part or document through a full cycle and draw every move on a floor plan, so the walking and carrying you'd never see from a desk show up as a tangle of lines. Distances, metres a shift, the three costliest legs, backtracks, crossings and the saving from a proposed layout are worked out for you.

Family: Value stream mapping · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A spaghetti diagram is a floor plan with the real path of one piece of work drawn on it: every walk, every carry, every trip back for something forgotten. You pick one person, part or document, follow it through one full cycle, and draw a line each time it moves. When the work is laid out badly the lines pile up and cross each other until the page looks like a plate of spaghetti, which is where the name comes from. The picture does something a list of steps can't: it shows distance and repetition at a glance, so walking and carrying that everyone has stopped noticing become obvious. Walking to fetch things and carrying material around are two of the classic wastes (motion and transport). They add time, effort and handling damage without adding anything the customer pays for. This tool keeps the drawing and adds the numbers: each leg's distance, how often it happens, metres a shift and a year, the three legs that cost the most, and how much a proposed layout would save. The drawing is for seeing the problem; the numbers are for deciding what to move first and proving it worked.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- People spend a lot of their day walking: fetching material, tools, paperwork or answers.
- A part or document travels a long way between steps, or goes back to the same place more than once.
- You're about to change a layout (a new cell, a moved bench, a new office) and want a before-and-after you can measure.
- A value stream map, a Makigami process map or a waste walk has pointed at a step where people or material move around a lot.

Not when:

- The problem is waiting, not moving: work sits in queues for days. A value stream map shows where the time goes better than a floor plan does.
- The work is mostly on screens and moves between systems, not places. A Makigami process map follows handoffs between people and systems.
- You can't watch the real work. A path drawn from memory leaves out exactly the trips you're looking for.
- The walking is a small part of the day. If someone walks for ten minutes in an eight-hour shift, a new layout won't pay for itself, unless that walking stops a machine or a line other people wait on.
- You want to compare many people's routines at once. Draw one person or one part at a time; a diagram with five people on it is unreadable.

## How to fill it in

- **subject** — Say exactly what you followed and for how long: one operator on the day shift, one order from receiving to dispatch, one invoice from arrival to payment. Pick one full cycle: the whole job from start to finish, not a sample of it. Say how you observed it (watched it live, with a floor plan and a measuring wheel) and how you checked the trip counts. A diagram is only as good as the observation behind it.
- **settings** — Choose whether trips are counted per shift or per day, and how many shifts or days a year the work runs, so the annual totals mean something. Set the walking speed: about 1 metre a second for someone carrying a load, a little more empty-handed. If you can, time the real person over a known distance. Enter the working minutes in a shift (or day) after breaks, so the tool can show what share of the time goes on walking. Give the floor plan's size in metres.
- **locations** — List every place the path visits: workstations, stores, cabinets, printers, desks, the coffee machine if that's where the answer lives. Give each one an x and y in metres from the top-left corner of the plan, read off a drawing or paced out. Write a few words on what is there, so the next reader knows why people go. For the proposed layout, fill in a proposed x and y only for the places that would move. Say which places can't move (a fire-rated room, a drain, a crane) in the proposed layout notes.
- **moves** — Follow the work and write one row for every move, in the order it happens: from, to, what is carried and why. Don't tidy it up; a trip back for a forgotten tool is exactly what you're looking for. Count how many times a shift each leg happens, from the observation or a record such as a log. Then walk the legs that matter with a measuring wheel or by pacing, and write the measured distance: a straight line across the plan always comes out short, because people walk around benches, racks and doors. For the proposed layout, set proposed trips to 0 for trips the change removes, add rows for new trips (today's trips 0), and write a planned distance where the route changes. Each walked leg must start where the last one ended, today and proposed; when you remove trips, add the walk that joins what's left, or the saving comes out too big. The Check column marks a gap.
- **map** — The tool draws every walked leg on the plan, today and proposed, with thicker lines for legs walked more often. A leg and its way back run side by side. The three legs that cost the most walking are orange and numbered. On paper, do the same by hand: one pencil line per leg, a tally mark for each repeat. Look for the knots (places everyone keeps going back to), long lines across the floor, and lines that cross.
- **comparison** — Calculated from your rows: metres a shift today and in the proposed layout, minutes walking per shift at your walking speed and the share of the shift that is, kilometres and hours a year, trips and crossings. Look at the share first: under about 5% of the shift, walking isn't the problem to chase, unless it stops a machine or a line people wait on, and then the cost is the stopped time, not the steps. Then read the three costliest legs: they're usually most of the walking, and they're where to start. A backtrack (a leg straight back to where the leg before started) often means something is kept in the wrong place. A crossing means two flows cut across each other, which costs time and can be a safety risk where people and trucks meet.
- **proposedChange** — Describe the layout change in a sentence or two: what moves where, what is kept at the point of use, which trips disappear. Say what can't move and why. Then go and try it, even as a mock-up with tape on the floor, and draw the path again. A proposal on paper is a prediction; the second diagram is the proof.
- **findings** — Write what the paths show in plain words, with the numbers: which legs cost the most and why, where the backtracks and crossings are, what the proposal saves, and what you'd try next. Three findings someone can act on beat a description of every line.

## What good looks like

Three quarters of it is two errands: labels from the dispatch office printer and cartons from the warehouse. ## Common mistakes

- **Drawing the path from memory or from the official process.** — The trips that matter are the ones nobody planned: going back for a tool, looking for a pallet, waiting at the printer. Only following the real work finds them. Draw it as it happened, not as it should happen.
- **Following a short sample instead of a full cycle.** — Twenty minutes of watching misses the trips that happen twice a shift, and those are often the long ones. Follow the whole job from start to finish, then use a log or a count to get the trips per shift right.
- **Quoting straight-line distances as the saving.** — People walk around benches, racks and doors, so a straight line can be a third shorter than the real walk. Measure the costliest legs, and use planned distances for the new layout, or the saving you promise won't be the saving you get.
- **Changing a layout for walking that costs little.** — A tangle of lines can still add up to a few minutes a shift. Check the share of the shift spent walking before you move anything; below about 5% the money is better spent on waiting or rework, unless the walking stops a machine or a line.
- **Selling the saving in kilometres a year.** — Multiplying a short walk by every shift in a year makes a big-sounding number that says nothing about whether it matters. Quote minutes a shift and the share of the shift; that's what the person gets back.
- **Trying to fix every line at once.** — A few legs usually carry most of the walking. Start with the three costliest, the backtracks and the crossings; a layout change aimed at those pays back fastest and is easiest to prove.
- **Treating the walking as the person's fault.** — People walk because that's where things are kept. The diagram is about the layout and the way supplies arrive, not about how efficient the person is. Say so before you start following anyone, and show them the diagram first.
- **Stopping at the proposal.** — Until the new layout has been tried and the path drawn again, the saving is a forecast. Run the change, follow the same cycle again, and compare the two diagrams.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Value stream map — current and future state** (`value-stream-map`) — A value stream map shows which step in the whole flow is slow. When the reason is people or material moving around inside that step, a spaghetti diagram of it shows exactly where the time goes.
- **Eight wastes (DOWNTIME) worksheet** (`eight-wastes-worksheet`) — A waste walk that finds a lot of motion or transport waste is the cue to follow one person or part and measure it with a spaghetti diagram.
- **Makigami process map** (`makigami`) — In office work, a Makigami shows handoffs between people and systems. Where those handoffs mean physically carrying paper or walking to ask someone, a spaghetti diagram of the office floor shows the cost.

Downstream:

- **Kaizen event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — A layout change that needs several people and a few days to try (moving benches, re-racking a store) is a good kaizen event. The diagram gives the event its baseline and its target.
- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — Once a new layout works, write the new sequence into a work instruction, including where everything is kept, so the shorter path becomes the standard and doesn't drift back.
- **Standard work chart** (`standard-work-chart`) — After the layout changes, time the job again on a standard work chart: the walking saved shows up as shorter walk steps, and the chart sets the new standard against takt.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing which person, part or document to follow, and making clear it's the layout being studied, not the person
- Deciding what can move and what can't, and which layout to try first
- Judging whether a trip is really needed or only exists because of where things are kept

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting patterns across the legs: places everyone keeps going back to, trips that always come in pairs
- Suggesting layout options that shorten the costliest legs, for the team to test on the floor
- Turning rough notes from the observation into a clean list of legs in order

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out every leg's distance, metres a shift and the annual totals
- Finding backtracks and crossings and ranking the costliest legs
- Redrawing the paths after every change to the layout
