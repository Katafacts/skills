# Marketing go-see protocol

Go and see whether marketing's numbers are true where they are made: re-run the count yourself, sample the records, sit in on the work and hear the buyer, then give every gap against the agreed definition a follow-up with one owner and a date.

Family: Measurement · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A marketing go-see protocol is a planned visit to the places marketing's numbers are made, to see whether they are true. In marketing the work is mostly in systems and meetings, so the stops are different from a walk on the floor: a report you re-run yourself, a set of records you open in two systems to see whether they agree, a campaign build or a lead review you sit in on. It has two parts. The route lists each stop, what it should match (the agreed metric definition, or the standard if there is one) and the question to ask once you've checked. The observation record is what you write as you go: the time, the stop, the fact you found (the count you got against the count shown, how many sampled records matched, what was done in the room), whether it meets the definition, the gap if it doesn't, the question and the answer, and a follow-up with one owner and a due date. The habit that matters most is checking before asking: run the query yourself before anyone explains it, so you see the number as it is rather than as it is described.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Metrics have agreed definitions and you want to know whether the dashboard actually counts them that way.
- Numbers move between systems, such as customer relationship management (CRM), enterprise resource planning (ERP) and an ad platform, and you want to see that records arrive complete and on time, not just that the sync reports no errors.
- A number on the board looks too good, too flat or suddenly different, and you want to see the work that produces it before anyone acts on it.
- A routine (a campaign launch checklist, a weekly lead review) is meant to keep the data clean and you want to see whether it does.

Not when:

- The definitions themselves aren't agreed yet. Write them first with a metric definition sheet; without them there is nothing to check against.
- You want to know which channel deserves the credit for pipeline. That is a question about the attribution rule, not about whether records are complete: use an attribution reality check.
- You're looking for someone to blame for a bad number. People will tidy the data before you arrive and you'll learn nothing; look at how the work is set up instead.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Write the one thing the walk is to find out, such as whether last month's lead numbers match the agreed definitions. Say what you'll re-run, sample and sit in on, and when: a lead review only happens once a week, so plan around it.
- **route** — List three to six stops in the order you'll take them. A good route covers where a number is counted (re-run the report), where it travels (sample the latest records in both systems: five is enough to find a pattern), where the data is created (sit in on a campaign build) and where it is acted on (the meeting that uses it). For each, write what it should match, in the definition's own words, and the question to ask once you've checked. Include one stop where the buyer is visible: listen to two recorded first calls on leads Sales accepted, or read five rejected leads end to end. Every other stop shows how the number is made; this one shows whether it describes real buyers.
- **record** — Do the checking yourself first, then write one row per thing you found: the time, the stop (spelled as on the route) and the fact. A fact is a number or something done: "Re-ran April's marketing qualified leads (MQLs): 131; the dashboard shows 142." Not "the dashboard is unreliable", and never a judgement of the person who built it. Mark it Meets, Gap or No standard. For a gap, write how it differs from the definition. Write the question you asked and the answer, in their words. Record what matches as well; it tells you which numbers you can stop worrying about.
- **followUp** — Before the walk ends, give every gap and every missing standard a follow-up: what will change, one owner and a due date. Fix the thing that carries the definition into the work (the report filter, the sync schedule, the checklist), not the person whose number was wrong. Where the fix is a decision someone else owns, the follow-up is to get that decision by a date.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the record is checked for you. For each stop it counts what you found there and how much didn't match, and flags a stop nobody checked. For each row it flags a question with no fact, a fact not compared with the definition, a gap not described, a gap with no follow-up, a follow-up with no owner or date, and words that usually mean an opinion ("seems", "should", "I think").
- **takeaway** — Write in two or three sentences what the walk showed: which numbers you can trust, which you can't and by how much, and the pattern behind the gaps. Then write what you'll check next time, usually the place the fixes have to prove themselves.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' demand generation lead going to see, on 11 May 2026, whether the lead and booking numbers marketing reports are true where they are made, ten days after MQL and sales-qualified lead (SQL) got agreed definitions. Four stops, all checked; eight facts recorded: three meet the definition, three are gaps and two have no standard to check against. All five of those have a follow-up with one owner and a due date, and the record has nothing for the checks to flag. Re-running April's counts herself, she found 131 MQLs by the definition against 142 on the dashboard, so April's SQLs per April MQL, the quick ratio the team watches, read 28.9% when they are 31.3%. Of the five latest closed-won deals, four reached the ERP within 24 hours and one, won on a Friday evening, took 56 hours, because the sync only runs on weekday nights, and nothing would have flagged it: the sync alerts on a failed run, not a late booking. Two paid social ads in the June webinar build had no channel tag, and four of six leads rejected at the weekly review had no reason. The pattern: each gap sits where an agreed definition never reached the tool that does the work. None of the four stops saw a buyer, so the next walk adds two recorded first calls on accepted SQLs.

## Common mistakes

- **Re-reading the dashboard instead of re-running the count.** — A dashboard can be consistent with itself and still wrong: its filter may predate the definition. Only a count you run yourself from the source, to the definition, shows the difference.
- **Trusting "no errors" from a sync.** — A sync that runs cleanly can still deliver late or skip a case nobody planned for, such as a deal won on a Friday evening. Sample real records and open each one in both systems.
- **Asking the analyst before checking for yourself.** — You get the explanation of how it should work, and then you check against that instead of against the definition. Check first, then ask about what you found.
- **Fixing the number instead of what produced it.** — Correcting last month's figure by hand leaves next month wrong too. The follow-up belongs on the report filter, the schedule or the checklist that carries the definition into the work.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Marketing metric definition sheet** (`marketing-metric-definition-sheet`) — The definitions are what each stop is checked against. A gap the walk finds either needs the work fixed or the definition clarified, and a missing rule gets written into the sheet.
- **Visibility board** (`visibility-board`) — The board shows the numbers; the walk checks they are true at the source before a red or a green is acted on.
- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — The same method on the shop floor: a route, a record of facts against the standard, and a follow-up for every gap.

Downstream:

- **Marketing attribution reality check** (`marketing-attribution-reality-check`) — Once records arrive with their channel, the attribution check tests how much the credit depends on the rule chosen. Missing channel tags found on the walk come first.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Follow-ups that take more than a few days go on the log and are reviewed until they're closed with evidence, such as the restated month.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing which numbers to check and which records to sample
- Deciding whether a difference is a gap in the work or a gap in the definition
- Agreeing follow-ups with the people who own the reports, systems and meetings

Analysis — AI helps:

- Suggesting stops and what each should match, from the metric definitions
- Spotting the pattern across gaps: where definitions stop reaching the tools
- Rewriting an opinion in the record as a fact to go and check

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting what was found at each stop and which gaps have an owner
- Turning rough notes into a clean record
- Laying out the route and record and exporting them as a workbook
