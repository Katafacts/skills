# Key performance indicator (KPI) tree

Break one outcome measure into the measures that make it, by formula where there is one and by cause where there isn't, each with a baseline, a current value, a target, an owner and a review rhythm. The tree is checked for you: links whose numbers don't add up, branch targets that wouldn't reach the target above them, and measures with no owner or target.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A key performance indicator (KPI) tree starts from one number the business cares about, such as on-time shipments, and asks: what makes this number? The answer is a few measures, and each of those is made by a few more, until you reach measures a team can move this week. Some links are arithmetic: good parts a shift really is run hours times parts per hour times the share that pass inspection, so the branches' numbers must multiply to the parent's. Others are causes without a formula: faster changeovers give more run hours, but so do fewer breakdowns. Every measure carries where it started, where it is now, its target, the one person who owns it and how often it's looked at. The tree earns its keep in two ways. It tells each person which number is theirs and how it connects to the top. And where the links are arithmetic, it shows whether the targets hang together: if every branch hits its target and the top still misses, the plan was a hope, and the tree shows it before the year is spent finding out.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A top-line number is off target and people argue about what would move it.
- You're setting targets for a team and want each one to connect to the outcome above it, not float on its own.
- A huddle or performance board tracks a lot of numbers and nobody can say which ones drive the result.
- You suspect the targets don't add up: everyone could hit theirs and the top number would still miss.
- A new owner is taking over a result and needs to see what drives it and who owns each piece.

Not when:

- You don't yet know which outcome matters. Agree the one top measure first; a tree with three tops is three trees.
- You need to find why one measure went wrong. The tree shows where to look; a 5-Whys or an A3 finds the cause.
- You need the exact definition of each measure, its formula and data source. That belongs on a metric definition sheet; the tree uses those measures.
- You want to track the numbers week by week. The tree is the map; a performance board or grid is where the weekly numbers go.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Say which outcome the tree serves and why now, and when the baselines and current values were measured. Two numbers from different periods look like progress when they aren't.
- **top** — Write one top outcome measure: a number someone already records, with a unit, such as "On-time shipment rate, Line 2 (%)". Not an aim ("delight customers") and not two numbers at once.
- **branches** — Under it, write the few measures that make it: three or four, not ten. Then do the same for each of those. Stop when a measure is something one team can act on this week. Ask "what makes this number?", not "what else could we track?".
- **combine** — For each measure with branches, say how they make it. Add when they add up to it (minutes lost to changeovers plus minutes lost to stops). Multiply when they multiply to it (run hours × parts per hour × first pass yield; a percentage counts as a fraction). Influence when they drive it but there's no formula. Use a formula wherever one is true: it's the only link the tree can check.
- **numbers** — For every measure: unit, which way is better, baseline, current and target. Use numbers you have, not estimates; leave a gap rather than invent one. Keep units consistent down a formula link (hours with hours), so the arithmetic works.
- **owners** — Name one person who answers for each measure, not a team. Say how often it's reviewed: Daily, Weekly, Monthly or Quarterly. A measure that drives another should be looked at at least as often as the one above it, or it warns too late.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For every Add or Multiply link, the tree works out what the branches give for the baseline, current and target, and flags an entered value more than 2% away. A target that doesn't add up is the key finding: the branch targets won't deliver the parent's. Each measure also gets a status (on target, behind target, or worse than baseline) and is flagged when it has no owner, no target, no baseline or current value, no review rhythm, or a review slower than the measure above it.
- **nextStep** — Write what the team agreed about the flags: which targets change, who owns the measures without an owner, and when the tree is next reviewed. Share it with every owner on it.

## What good looks like

The example below is Line 2's tree for on-time shipments, drawn up on 22 June 2026 by the line supervisor with the paint booth lead, scheduling and dispatch. The top measure has recovered from 81% to 94% against a 97% target. Three measures make it: good painted parts per shift, small-batch orders painted in their slot, and orders at the dock before the truck window. Good parts per shift is a formula link: run hours × parts per run hour × first pass yield gives 47 at baseline and 52.25 now, matching the 47 and 52 packing counts. At target it gives 6.0 × 10 × 97% = 58.2, short of the 60 packing needs, so the tree flags that the target doesn't add up: every paint booth target could be hit and packing would still be short. Run hours are driven by minutes per changeover (46 down to 35, target 25) and by unplanned stops, which have risen from 12 to 15 minutes a shift with no owner or target, the other flag. One of the nine measures is on target; the other eight are behind target, or, like the stops, worse than baseline. The team agreed to find out whether conveyor speed can lift parts per run hour to 10.5, which would give 61.1, and to name an owner for the stops.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with a list of metrics instead of one outcome.** — A tree grown from "what do we track?" ends up as every number the team has, joined by guesswork. Start from the one outcome and ask what makes it; the measures that don't connect don't belong.
- **Calling every link a cause when some are arithmetic.** — Where a parent really is the sum or product of its branches, writing that formula down is what lets the tree catch targets that can't all be true. Influence links can't be checked, so use them only where no formula exists.
- **Setting each target on its own.** — Targets set branch by branch rarely multiply to the target above. The tree shows it: in the example, every booth target met still gives 58.2 parts a shift, not 60. Settle it by changing a branch target, adding the missing branch, or changing the parent's.
- **Measures with a team for an owner, or no owner at all.** — "Maintenance" owning a number means nobody does. One named person answers for each measure; they can share the work.
- **Drivers reviewed less often than the outcome they drive.** — A driver is meant to warn early. Looked at monthly under a weekly outcome, it reports what the outcome already told you.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`hoshin-kanri`) — The annual plan names the results that matter; a tree takes one of them and breaks it into the measures each team owns.

Downstream:

- **Metric definition sheet** (`metric-definition-sheet`) — Every measure on the tree needs one agreed definition, formula and data source, or two people will report two numbers under the same name.
- **Performance board** (`performance-board`) — The tree is the map; the board is where its measures are tracked against plan, month by month, with a countermeasure when one misses.
- **Performance grid** (`performance-grid`) — The measures at the tips of the tree, the ones a team can move this week, are the squares on a daily red and green grid.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — When a branch stays behind target, the tree says where the problem is; an A3 works out why and what to change.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing the one outcome the tree serves
- Deciding which targets change when the branch targets don't reach the top
- Naming an owner for each measure

Analysis — AI helps:

- Suggesting the measures that make a given outcome, and whether a link has a true formula
- Spotting links whose numbers don't add up and targets that can't all be met
- Spotting measures that are moving the wrong way

Drudgery — automated:

- Multiplying and adding the branch values at baseline, current and target
- Comparing every current value with its target
- Drawing the tree and laying out the workbook
