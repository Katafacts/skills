# Affinity diagram

Turn a pile of customer notes into the few things customers are telling you: one fact or quote per note, grouped by what belongs together, each group named afterwards in a sentence the customer could have said, with the notes that fit nowhere kept in view and the few groups that matter most picked, with why.

Family: Voice of the customer · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

An affinity diagram sorts a pile of notes into groups without deciding the answer first. Each note holds one thing a customer said or did, with who said it. The team moves notes that belong together next to each other, usually in silence so nobody's favourite theory steers it, and only when the groups have settled does anyone name them. The name goes on a header card above each group, written as one sentence the customer could have said ("I can't find out where my invoice is"), not a topic label ("Status"): a sentence keeps the customer's point, a label loses it. Related groups can sit under a heading of their own. Notes that fit nowhere stay on the wall rather than being squeezed into a group, because the odd one out is sometimes the important one. Then the team picks the few groups that matter most and says why. The result is a short, honest answer to "what are customers telling us?", with every claim traceable to the notes under it.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have a pile of customer notes (interviews, calls, complaints, survey comments, chase emails) and need to know what they add up to.
- Opinions in the room differ about what customers want, and you want the notes to settle it rather than the loudest voice.
- You are about to write customer requirements, a process outline or a problem statement and want them grounded in what customers actually said.
- The notes don't fit categories you already have, or you suspect the categories you have are the wrong ones.

Not when:

- You have fewer than about 20 notes. Read them and talk; there is not enough to group.
- You already know the categories and just need to count how often each comes up. Tally them and draw a Pareto chart.
- You want the causes of one problem. A fishbone diagram or 5-Whys goes after causes; an affinity diagram shows what customers experience.
- You still need to gather the notes. Plan the gathering first with a collection plan or an interview guide.

## How to fill it in

- **question** — Write the one question the notes answer, such as "What makes getting paid by us hard for our vendors?", and where the notes came from and when. A clear question keeps the grouping on track; notes that don't answer it can stay off the wall.
- **notes** — Write every note before you group any. One fact or one quote per note, in the customer's own words where you have them, with who said it. Split a note that says two things into two notes. Keep your interpretation off the notes: "Paid seven weeks late on a $240 invoice" is a note, "Our payment process is broken" is a conclusion. Type each source the same way every time so the counts of different sources come out right. 25 to 60 notes is a good pile.
- **grouping** — Move notes that belong together next to each other, without names, in silence if you can, and let anyone move any note. Group by what the notes mean, not by the words they share. Give each group a letter as it forms and put the letter on each of its notes. A note that fits nowhere stays ungrouped: it shows in its own column on the wall. Stop when notes stop moving; for most piles that is five to ten groups.
- **headers** — Only now name each group. Read its notes and write one sentence that says what they say together, as the customer would say it. "When we chase, nobody gets back to us" is a header; "Communication" is a label and hides the point. If a header doesn't fit every note under it, the group needs splitting, or the header needs rewriting. If several groups belong together, give them a heading above.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter: the diagram counts each group's notes and how many different sources they came from, and flags a group with no header, a header shorter than four words (usually a label), a group of one note, a group whose notes all come from one source (one customer talking, not a pattern yet), and a group that holds over a quarter of all the notes once it has six or more (usually two or three groups in one). It also flags notes with no source and picks with no reason. Each flag is a question to answer, not a rule.
- **picks** — Pick up to three groups that matter most and write why. Size is a starting point, not the reason: say what the group costs the customer or you, and how many different customers it comes from. Then write what happens next, with whom and by when. A wall with no picks is a picture, not a decision.

## What good looks like

The example below is Meridian Business Services' accounts payable team making sense of what vendors say about getting paid, a week before they outline the invoice approval process. They wrote 37 notes from 14 vendors (January calls, chase emails and survey comments), one fact or quote each, then grouped them in silence. Six groups formed under three headings: knowing where we stand, getting paid, and getting an invoice accepted. Three notes fit nowhere and stay on the wall. The checks flag one group: "Changing our bank details took weeks" is 2 notes from one vendor, so the team checks whether it happened to anyone else before calling it a pattern. They picked three: "We get paid weeks after our terms" (9 notes from 9 vendors), "I can't find out where my invoice is" (8 notes from 7 vendors, 13 with the group about chasing), and "Our invoice comes back for a purchase order number nobody gave us" (6 notes from 6 vendors). On the process outline a week later, the first two become the vendor's requirement (paid on time, without having to chase, with the remittance group folded in as "the right amount"), and the purchase order pick becomes an input the process depends on: an invoice with the purchase order number on it.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the groups first.** — Start with categories and every note gets filed under one, so you find what you expected. Group first, name afterwards, and the notes can surprise you.
- **Labels instead of headers.** — "Pricing" or "Communication" tells you where a note was filed, not what customers said. A sentence in the customer's voice keeps the point and is what people remember and act on.
- **Notes that hold a conclusion, or several facts.** — "Process is too slow" is the team's judgement, and it groups with everything. One fact or quote per note, in the customer's words, lets the groups show what customers said rather than what you already thought.
- **Forcing every note into a group.** — A note squeezed into a group it doesn't fit blurs that group's header, and the odd one out can be the early sign of something new. Leave it ungrouped and look at it again.
- **Counting notes and stopping there.** — Nine notes from one customer is one customer. Check how many different sources stand behind a group before you call it a pattern, and pick by what it costs, not by size alone.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Customer interview guide** (`customer-interview-guide`) — Interviews run from a guide give you notes in the customer's own words, the best raw material for the wall.
- **Voice of the customer (VOC) evidence log** (`voc-evidence-log`) — A running log of quotes and observations is a ready pile of notes: pull the ones that answer your question onto the wall.

Downstream:

- **SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers)** (`sipoc`) — The picked groups become the customer's requirements and the inputs the process needs on the process outline, each stated in their terms and traceable to notes.
- **Critical-to-quality (CTQ) tree** (`ctq-tree`) — A picked group says what customers need in their words; a CTQ tree turns it into something you can measure, with a target.
