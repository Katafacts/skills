# Visual board design

Design the board a team stands at: a layout of a few zones, and for each one the question it answers, what normal looks like, how abnormal shows at a glance from where people stand, who updates it and when, and what happens when it shows abnormal. The checks are computed: zones with no question, an abnormal you have to read rather than see, too small to see from the huddle, no owner, or no response.

Family: Visual management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A visual board is the wall a team gathers at to see, in a few seconds, whether today is going to plan and what needs doing about it. Designing one means deciding, before anything goes on the wall, what each part of it is for. The board is laid out as a few zones, each in its own place. For every zone you write the question it answers for the people standing there ("did each shift make the 60 good parts packing needs?"), what normal looks like, and how abnormal shows. The test of a good zone is that someone who doesn't work there can tell normal from abnormal from where the team stands, without reading a number and comparing it with a target in their head: a red square, a bar below a line, a card in the wrong column, an empty slot. Then two things that decide whether the board stays alive: who keeps each zone current and when, and what happens when a zone shows abnormal. A board without those becomes wallpaper within weeks.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're putting up a new board for a team's daily huddle and want to decide what goes on it before anyone starts drawing.
- An existing board is cluttered, stale or ignored, and you want to redesign it zone by zone.
- Two or three boards have grown up in one area and you want to bring them into one place to look.
- People stand at the board every day but problems on it don't lead to anything, and you want to agree what each signal should set off.

Not when:

- The team hasn't agreed what it measures yet. Settle the few measures first with a key performance indicator (KPI) tree or a metric definition sheet, then design where they go.
- You want to track one measure's daily red and green over a month. That's a performance grid, which can then be one zone on this board.
- The board already exists and you want to know whether it still works. Walk it with a visual management audit instead.
- Nobody will stand at it. If the team never gathers there, a board on a wall adds nothing a shared report doesn't.

## How to fill it in

- **board** — Say which team and work the board serves, where it hangs, and who reads it and when: usually a short huddle at the start of a shift, plus a supervisor's walk. Measure how far back people stand when they read it. A board read from 3 metres needs bigger marks than one read from arm's length.
- **layout** — Give each zone a short name, as it will be written on the board, and a place: a row (1 to 3, top to bottom) and a column (1 to 4, left to right). The drawing shows the layout as you go. Put what is read first at the top left: safety, then today's plan. Fewer zones read faster; most huddle boards need between five and nine.
- **question** — Write the question each zone answers for the people standing at it, as they would ask it: "Is anything waiting outside a changeover slot?" If you can't write the question, the zone doesn't belong on the board.
- **signal** — Write what normal looks like and how abnormal shows, then pick the kind of signal: a colour, a line on a chart, a position (a card in a column), a light or flag, or an empty space. "Number or words only" means people have to read it and know the target to spot a problem; change it so abnormal shows by itself, for example by colouring the box red below target.
- **readFrom** — Mock the board up, stand where the huddle stands, and ask someone who doesn't work in the area to tell you what is normal and what isn't. Write the distance from which they could read each zone correctly. A zone that can't be read from where people stand needs bigger marks or a thicker line, not a closer huddle.
- **owner** — Name who updates each zone, as a person or a role on duty, and when: "End of each shift, from the packing count." A zone with no owner is the first to go stale, and one stale zone teaches people to stop looking at the rest.
- **response** — For each zone, agree what happens when it shows abnormal: who does what, and by when. "Red: the line supervisor is radioed at once." "Below the line two shifts running: the reason goes on the problems zone with an owner." A zone that shows abnormal with no response only records problems.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: each zone is checked for you. Check flags a zone with no place or sharing a place, no question, no abnormal signal or one that must be read, not tested from a distance or too small to see from where people stand, and no owner, update time or response. The summary counts the zones ready and how many pass each test.
- **nextStep** — Write what was agreed for each flagged zone in its own row, then the next step for the board: when it goes up, what it replaces, and when you'll test it again with a fresh pair of eyes. Plan an audit a few weeks after it goes up.

## What good looks like

The example below is the Line 2 paint booth's board, designed by the paint booth lead on 3 August 2026 to bring the queue board and the booth's problem board into one place where the 06:45 and 14:30 huddles stand, 3 metres back. Nine zones on a three-row layout: safety, today's changeover slots, good parts per shift and changeover time across the top; first pass yield, unplanned stops, problems and actions and the maintenance technician on shift in the middle; who can run a changeover below. It's the team's first pass, tested from the huddle spot with the second shift lead as the stranger, and the checks find four zones to fix while five are ready. The changeover time chart could only be read from 2 metres; first pass yield was a number you had to read and compare with 97%; unplanned stops showed abnormal but nothing was agreed to happen; and nobody owned the changeover sign-off zone. So abnormal shows at a glance in 8 of the 9 zones, 8 can be read from where people stand, 8 have an owner and update time, and 8 have a response. Each flagged zone carries what was agreed, and the board went up on 10 August with the four changes made.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting the numbers on the board without showing what's abnormal.** — A column of percentages looks informative and works badly: only someone who knows the target can spot the problem, and only by reading. Colour it, draw the target line, or leave a gap, so abnormal jumps out to anyone.
- **Designing at a desk and never standing back.** — Marks that are clear on a screen or at arm's length disappear at 3 metres. Test every zone from where the huddle stands, with someone who doesn't work there.
- **A zone nobody owns.** — Every zone needs one named person or role on duty to update it at a set time. The unowned zone goes stale first, and a board with one stale zone soon gets ignored altogether.
- **Abnormal with no agreed response.** — The point of seeing a problem at a glance is doing something about it the same day. If nobody has agreed who acts when a zone goes red, the red gets looked at and walked past.
- **Too many zones.** — Every zone added slows the huddle and buries the few that matter. Keep the zones that answer a question someone asks at the board every day, and move the rest to a report.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Key performance indicator (KPI) tree** (`kpi-tree`) — The tree says which few measures the team can move and who owns each; the board gives each of them a zone where abnormal shows at a glance.
- **Escalation matrix** (`escalation-matrix`) — When a zone shows a problem the team can't close, the matrix says who is called and how fast. Write that into the zone's response.

Downstream:

- **Performance grid** (`performance-grid`) — A grid of each measure's daily red and green is often one zone on the board, with its reasons and countermeasures beside it.
- **Daily management huddle** (`daily-huddle`) — The huddle is where the board gets read and its abnormal turned into actions. Run it at the board, in the order the zones are laid out.
- **Leader standard work** (`leader-standard-work`) — Checking that the board is current and that red zones have an owner belongs in the team leader's daily routine.
- **Visual management audit** (`visual-management-audit`) — A few weeks after the board goes up, walk it and check each zone is current, readable at a glance and acted on when it shows abnormal.

Judgement — stays with you:

Analysis — AI helps:

Drudgery — automated:
