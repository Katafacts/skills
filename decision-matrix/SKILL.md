# Weighted decision matrix

Choose between a handful of options or competing priorities: screen out any that fail a must-have, score the rest against weighted criteria agreed up front, and see plainly whether the lead is big enough to trust — always computed from what you enter, never asserted.

Family: Problem solving / root cause analysis · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A weighted decision matrix scores a small set of options against criteria that don't all matter equally — cost, ease of adoption, support quality, whatever the real decision actually turns on — each criterion carrying its own weight, each option scored directly against each criterion (1 to 5). Before any scoring, each option is checked against the must-haves: conditions no score can make up for, such as a deadline, a spending limit or a safety rule. An option that fails one is out, however well it would score. The rest get a composite score from 20 (every score a 1) to 100 (every score a 5). When you're choosing one option, read the lead over the runner-up. Under 5 points is too close to call and needs more evidence, not a winner. But 5 points is only a rule of thumb: one point on a criterion weighted 40% moves the composite 8 points. So run the test a master would: change any single score by one point, for or against the leader, and check the lead survives. If one point flips the order, that score is the decision, and it needs evidence. Tiers (priority, consider, deprioritize) are for screening a longer list, where they sort the work; on a choice of one they would turn a one-point gap into a different label. It's different from the impact/effort matrix's quick two-axis sort of many ideas, and from an FMEA, which ranks failure modes by risk, not options by fit.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have a real handful of options (not two obvious extremes) and criteria that genuinely matter differently to the decision, not equally.
- You can state real weights and score each option honestly, even when the highest-weighted criterion doesn't have an obvious winner.
- You want the ranking to survive someone asking 'why did we pick this one' with a specific, defensible answer.

Not when:

- You're ranking failure modes, not comparing options against each other — that's an FMEA's job, not this one's.
- You need a fast impact/effort triage across many small ideas, not a deliberated choice between a few real options — an impact/effort matrix's simpler 2-axis sort fits that better.
- Only one option can meet the hard constraints (budget, deadline, policy). Write them as must-haves and the screen will show it: with one option left there's nothing to weigh.

## How to fill it in

- **decision** — Write the decision in one sentence: what's being chosen, and by when. If the options can't all be scored against the same criteria, you have more than one decision on the page.
- **mustHaves** — Write the conditions every option must meet whatever else it scores: a deadline, a spending limit, a safety or legal rule. Check each option Pass or Fail with the evidence (a quote, a lead time), before anyone scores. An option that fails is screened out, so a high score elsewhere can't buy back a deal-breaker. Keep the list short: a preference is a weighted criterion, not a must-have.
- **criteria** — List what matters in this decision and give each criterion a weight, so the weights add up to 100%. Agree the weights before anyone scores an option, so nobody adjusts them to suit the option they already prefer.
- **options** — Score each option still in play from 1 (weakest) to 5 (strongest) against every criterion, one criterion across all options at a time, using what you know rather than what you hope. The composite score, rank and lead over the next are calculated — don't type them. If the top two are under 5 points apart, or one point on a single score would swap them, the scores can't separate them: name the evidence that would, get it and score again. If the winner looks wrong, find the weight or score that's off rather than overriding the result.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences on the result: what the screen removed and why, which option leads, which criteria put it there, and whether the lead beats the 5-point margin. Then move 10 points of weight between two criteria and see if the order holds; if it flips, name the weight the choice rests on — that's the part people need to discuss.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoring an option that fails a must-have.** — Weighted scoring lets a strength on one criterion make up for a weakness on another. That's right for preferences and wrong for deal-breakers: a high score on cost can't make a 20-week lead time meet a September deadline. Screen first, then score what's left.
- **Reading a one- or two-point lead, or a different tier, as a winner.** — Each 1-to-5 score is a judgement with a point or so of error either way, and one point on a heavily weighted criterion moves the composite several points. Under 5 points apart, or if a one-point change to any single score swaps the order, get the evidence that would separate the options instead of announcing a result.
- **Weights don't sum to 100% and nobody checks.** — A set of weights that doesn't sum to 100% silently distorts every composite score in a way that's hard to spot just by looking at the final ranking — the check is cheap and the failure mode isn't.
- **Scoring options relative to each other from a gut feel about which one 'seems best,' instead of scoring each one independently against each criterion.** — A relative gut-feel ranking just launders the same intuition the tool exists to check — real per-criterion scores are what let the weighting actually change the outcome instead of rubber-stamping a decision already made.
- **Letting the option that wins the single highest-weighted criterion decide the outcome without checking the actual composite.** — The whole point of weighting several criteria is that the strongest option on one axis can still lose on the composite — treating one criterion as decisive defeats the purpose of scoring the others at all.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis** (`swot-analysis`) — When a SWOT produces several competing actions, score them here before committing.
- **Impact/effort matrix: sort ideas fast** (`impact-effort-matrix`) — Sorts a long list fast; the big or close calls it flags come here for a weighted comparison.
- **Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)** (`fmea`) — When an FMEA's recommended actions produce more than one real candidate fix for the same risk, a weighted decision matrix is where you choose between them on more than just risk priority number alone.

Downstream:

- **Business case** (`business-case`) — Once a weighted decision matrix names the option worth pursuing, a business case is where that choice gets justified with real investment and value numbers.
