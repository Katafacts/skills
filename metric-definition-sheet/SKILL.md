# Metric definition sheet

One owned formula, data source and owner per metric, so two teams stop computing the same number two ways and both being right, with each metric's direction, target and reaction rule, and any metric still unresolved named plainly.

Family: Measurement · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A metric definition sheet is a real, checkable answer to 'what does this number actually mean, and who says so' — one formula, one data source, one owner per metric name. The problem it exists to solve is common and well documented: two teams both report 'on-time delivery' or 'active users,' each computing it a different way, and both numbers are defensible on their own terms — until someone tries to reconcile them. This sheet doesn't invent a resolution when none exists; a metric with no agreed formula or no real owner is flagged plainly, not smoothed over.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Two teams have quietly started reporting different numbers for a metric with the same name, and nobody has actually reconciled why.
- A dashboard or huddle board is about to go live and you want every metric on it traceable to a real formula and a real owner, not an assumption.
- You want to know honestly which metrics in active use still have no agreed definition, rather than assuming they're all settled.

Not when:

- You don't actually know how a metric is computed today — go find out from whoever owns the system first; this sheet records real facts, it doesn't invent a formula that sounds plausible.
- The metric is genuinely one-off or exploratory, not something two or more people will reference by name going forward — the overhead isn't worth it yet.

## How to fill it in

- **scopeArea** — Say which part of the business these definitions cover — a team, a process, a site.
- **metrics** — For each metric, write what it means in plain words, the formula, the data source, the owner, how often it's refreshed and the unit, as they're actually used today. Then three things people usually leave in their heads: which way is good (higher, lower, or on a target), the target as the team states it, and what is deliberately left out of the count (trial units, returns, cancelled orders). Get them from whoever builds the report, and leave a field blank if it isn't agreed yet rather than guessing. In "refreshed and reviewed", add the reaction rule: the metric is read on a run chart, what counts as a real change (a point outside the limits, a long run on one side of the centre line), and who responds. Before calling a definition agreed, test it: two people count the same period on their own, from the stated source, and get the same number.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences naming which metrics are fully defined and which still have a gap: no agreed formula, no data source, no owner, no direction or no target, each counted for you. Where two teams disagree, the owner who settles it should not be one of them.

## What good looks like

The example below is a production line's three headline metrics. First pass yield at final inspection and paint-booth changeover time are fully defined: formula, source, owner, direction, target, and what each leaves out (engineering trial units and returns; changeovers interrupted by a breakdown). First pass yield also says how it is read: on a run chart, with a stated rule for a real change and who responds, and a definition two people tested by counting the same shifts. On-time delivery rate is not defined: Scheduling counts an order on time if it ships by the promise date, Quality only if it has also passed final inspection by then, so it has no agreed formula, data source or target. The sheet names that conflict rather than picking a winner, and gives the decision to the plant manager by 31 March, with both teams as inputs, since neither can settle a dispute it is part of.

## Common mistakes

- **Not writing down what is left out of the count** — Two teams can share a formula and still get different numbers because one excludes trial units, returns or cancelled orders and the other does not. Unstated exclusions are the commonest reason a metric means two things; write them next to the formula.
- **Writing a formula that sounds precise but was never actually confirmed against the source system.** — A confident-looking formula with no real basis is worse than an honest gap — it lets two teams both trust their own number right up until the day someone tries to reconcile them.
- **Leaving a metric unowned because 'everyone kind of owns it.'** — A definition nobody owns is a definition everyone quietly forks — a real owner is what keeps the formula from silently drifting as systems change.
- **Calling a definition agreed before anyone has tested it.** — A formula can read clearly and still be counted two ways. Have two people count the same period on their own from the stated source; if they get different numbers, the definition isn't finished.
- **Reacting to every point.** — Every metric goes up and down. Without a rule for what counts as a real change, judged on a run chart, people chase ordinary variation and miss the shifts that matter.
- **Resolving a genuine cross-team conflict unilaterally just to make the sheet look complete.** — Picking a winner without the other team's buy-in doesn't resolve the conflict, it just hides it — naming the conflict plainly is what actually gets it fixed.

## What it connects to

Downstream:

- **Key performance indicator (KPI) tree** (`kpi-tree`) — A driver metric in a KPI tree is only as trustworthy as this sheet's own definition behind it — the tree tracks the number, this sheet is what makes the number mean one specific thing.
- **Performance board** (`performance-board`) — Every measure on the board should have its definition here first, so a red month is a real miss and not two teams counting differently.
- **Performance grid** (`performance-grid`) — The grid's rule for each row (red when…) only works if the number behind it is defined here, with its direction and target.
- **Run chart** (`run-chart`) — Once a metric is defined, a run chart reads its history: whether a change in the number is a real change in the process or the usual up and down.
- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — Count it at the source: go and watch where the number is made, and recount a period by hand against the report.
