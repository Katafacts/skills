# Stakeholder coverage map

For one account or deal, a grid of the parts of the account against the buying roles the deal needs, with the real person in each cell, where they stand and when someone last spoke with them. The checks are computed: required roles with nobody in them, contacts gone quiet, parts of the account hanging on one thread, and no champion who is for us.

Family: Deal execution · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A stakeholder coverage map is a grid for one account or deal. Down the side go the parts of the account the deal touches: departments, sites or business units. Across the top go the buying roles the deal needs: the economic buyer who signs off the money, the champion who sells it inside when you're not there, the technical evaluator, the people who will use it, whoever approves the contract, and anyone who could block it. In each cell goes a real person: their name, where they stand (for us, neutral, against or unknown), the date of the last real two-way conversation and who on your side owns the relationship. Deals rarely die because nobody liked the product. They die because the one person you knew went quiet, because the person who signs had never heard of you, or because a department nobody spoke to said no at the end. The map makes those risks visible weeks earlier: an empty column is a role nobody fills, a row with one name hangs on one thread, and a date that's a month old is a conversation that has stopped.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A deal needs more than one person's yes and you want to see who you really know before the close date, not after.
- Your contact has gone quiet, or has just left, and you need to know who else you can reach.
- A forecast call asks whether a deal is real: the map shows whether anyone who can sign has spoken with you.
- A large account spans several departments or sites and you're planning who to meet next.
- Handing an account to a colleague, so they inherit the people and the history, not only a company name.

Not when:

- A deal one person decides alone in one conversation. There's nothing to map; book the call.
- You're deciding which accounts to go after. That's a question about the market; use a fit-criteria scorecard or a total addressable market (TAM) coverage map.
- You want to plan the steps and dates to signature. The map says who; a mutual action plan says what happens when.
- Nobody has spoken with anyone at the account yet. Find a first contact; an empty map tells you nothing you don't know.

## How to fill it in

- **account** — Name the account and the deal, the date the map is true on, and how many days without a real conversation counts as gone quiet. Three weeks is a sensible start for a deal closing this quarter; a long enterprise cycle might allow six. Days since each contact count to the map's date, so move the date forward each time you update it.
- **units** — List the parts of the account the deal touches, one per row: the department that will use it, the one that pays, IT, legal and procurement, and any site or business unit with its own say. Include the ones you haven't reached yet; an empty row is the finding.
- **roles** — Keep or rename the buying roles across the top and mark each one required or not. Required means the deal can't close without someone in that role: usually the economic buyer, the champion, a technical evaluator, a user and an approver. A blocker column is optional; use it to record anyone who could stop the deal. Mark which column is your champion; the champion check reads that one, whatever you call it.
- **contacts** — Add one row per real person: name, job title, part of the account, role, stance, the date of the last two-way conversation and who owns the relationship on your side. A conversation means a meeting, a call or a reply, not an email you sent. Leave the date empty if there hasn't been one. Put only people you know exist; an honest empty cell is worth more than a guessed name. Note what you know about each person and your next move with them.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. Each cell shows its people and their stance. Each contact gets the days since the last real conversation and a flag for gone quiet, no real conversation yet, or no owner on your side. Each part of the account is flagged when it has no contact, only one (single-threaded), or nobody spoken with recently. Each required role is flagged when nobody fills it or nobody in it has been spoken with recently, and the champion column when nobody in it is for you. Treat each flag as a question for the deal team, not a verdict.
- **nextStep** — Write the next moves for the biggest gaps, each with a person and a date: who you'll ask for which introduction, who you'll call and why it matters to them, how you'll answer the person who is against. Update the map every week the deal is open.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' map of its Northline Freight deal on March 5, the day the mutual action plan was written, with three weeks as the quiet limit. Five parts of the account, six buying roles and seven people. The champion, Northline's vice president of revenue operations, is for us and was spoken with three days earlier, and four of the seven contacts are live. But only 2 of the 5 required roles have a live contact (champion and user). The economic buyer is the chief financial officer (CFO), not the vice president: at 10 seats the deal is above what a vice president can sign alone. The CFO signs off the budget on March 13 and is a name nobody at Beacon has spoken to; the only live thread into Finance is the controller, who is against the purchase; IT hangs on one contact who has gone quiet for 24 days while Beacon's security documents are late; a sales manager has been quiet for 23 days; and nobody in legal and procurement is named, so the approver column is empty, the same gap as the plan's unowned legal review. Three of the five parts of the account have two or more contacts. The next moves name who will reach each gap before the sign-off.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting a name as coverage.** — A person nobody on your side has spoken with is a gap with a name on it. The map keeps the date of the last real conversation for each person, so a name you found on a website doesn't pass for a relationship.
- **Mapping only the people who like you.** — The people who are against the deal, or who don't know it exists, are the ones who stop it at the end. Put them on the map with their stance; you can only answer an objection you know about.
- **Guessing names to fill the grid.** — A guessed name makes the map look covered and hides the gap until the worst moment. Leave the cell empty and ask your champion who it should be.
- **Drawing it once at the start of the deal.** — Coverage decays every week someone doesn't call. A map that isn't updated shows last month's deal. Move the date and the conversation dates forward every week the deal is open.
- **One person owning every relationship.** — If one rep owns everyone, the account depends on one calendar. Spread the owners across your team, the way the deal needs to be spread across theirs.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Ideal customer profile (ICP) canvas** (`icp-canvas`) — The canvas says which buying-committee roles your best deals had. Those roles are the columns of this map; who fills each one can change with the deal, as when a large deal moves the signature from a vice president to finance.
- **Anti-ideal customer profile (anti-ICP)** (`anti-icp`) — One champion and no route to the economic buyer is a red flag there. Here it shows up as one live column and an empty or quiet economic buyer.
- **Total addressable market (TAM) coverage map** (`tam-coverage-map`) — The market map shows which accounts you reach at all. This map goes inside one of them and shows who you reach there.

Downstream:

- **Mutual action plan** (`mutual-action-plan`) — Each gap on the map becomes a dated step on the plan with an owner on each side, such as naming who reviews the contract in legal.
- **Deal aging report** (`deal-aging-report`) — When a deal has sat too long in a stage, the map usually shows why: the people who matter have gone quiet or were never reached.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding which roles the deal really needs and who holds them
- Judging where each person stands, from what they said and did
- Choosing which gap to close first and who on your side should do it

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting required roles nobody fills, single-threaded parts of the account and contacts gone quiet
- Suggesting who to ask for an introduction to a missing role
- Turning call notes into stance and last-conversation updates for each person

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting the days since each conversation and keeping the flags current
- Laying the grid out from the contacts list
- Exporting the map as a workbook
