# Go-see protocol

Plan a walk to see the work where it happens, then record what you saw: the route (each stop, what to look for and the question to ask after watching) and an observation record (time, where, the fact, whether it meets the standard, the gap, the follow-up with an owner and a date). The checks are computed: stops nobody looked at, facts never compared with the standard, gaps with no owner, and opinions in the fact column.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A go-see protocol is a walk to the place where the work is done, planned well enough that it finds something and recorded well enough that something happens. It has two parts. The route lists the stops in the order you'll walk them, what to look for at each (the standard, or what you expect to see if there isn't one) and the question to ask once you've watched. The observation record is what you write while walking: the time, where you were, the fact you saw (what was done, a count, a time on your watch), whether it meets the standard, how it differs if it doesn't, the question you asked and the answer, and the follow-up with one owner and a due date. Three habits make it work. Watch before you ask, because the first question changes what people do. Write facts, not opinions, because a fact can be checked and an opinion starts an argument. And give every gap an owner before you leave, because a gap that is seen and left teaches everyone the walk doesn't matter.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A new standard or countermeasure is in place and you want to know whether it's how the work is really done, not just what the card on the wall says.
- A number on a board or a report looks fine but something doesn't add up, and you want to see the work that produces it.
- You're starting a problem-solving effort and need the current condition from what happens at the work, not from memory or meeting notes.
- A leader's routine includes a regular walk and you want each one to find something and leave a record.
- Work crosses shifts or teams and the trouble seems to be at the handovers nobody watches.

Not when:

- You want to check whether people are following the rules so you can correct them. That turns the walk into an inspection and people will show you what you want to see.
- You need to measure how far people walk or how long each step takes. Follow one person with a spaghetti diagram or time the steps instead.
- You've already seen it and recorded the facts. Analyse the cause with a fishbone diagram or 5-Whys, then walk again to check it.
- Nobody can act on what you find. Settle who owns the area first; a record with no possible owner changes nothing.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Write the one thing the walk is to find out, such as whether the new changeover routine is how changeovers are really done. Say when you'll walk: the shift and the time the work you want to see is running. A walk with no question becomes a tour.
- **route** — List the stops in walking order, three to six of them, each named the way people on the floor name it. For each, write what to look for: the standard if there is one ("flush timed for 90 seconds on the booth timer"), or what you expect to see if there isn't. Then write the question you'll ask after watching. Plan to stand where the work happens during the part that matters, and include the handovers and the place where the evidence ends up (the log, the reject tags), not only the main job.
- **record** — At each stop, watch a full cycle before you speak. Then write one row per thing you saw: the time, the stop (from the route, spelled the same), and the fact. A fact is what was done, a count or a time: "Flush stopped at 41 seconds; the booth timer was not started." Not "operator rushed the flush", and never a judgement of the person. Mark it Meets, Gap or No standard. For a gap, write how the work differs from the standard. Write the question you asked and the answer you got in the same row. Write things that meet the standard too; they show what is working.
- **followUp** — Before you leave the floor, give every gap and every missing standard a follow-up: what will happen, one owner and a due date. Agree it with the person who will do it rather than assigning it from your desk. The follow-up fixes how the work is set up (training, a box for the cards, a check at handover), not the person you happened to watch.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the record is checked for you. For each stop it counts what you saw there and how much wasn't to standard, and flags a stop nobody looked at. For each row it flags a question with no fact, a fact not compared with the standard, a gap not described, a gap with no follow-up, a follow-up with no owner or date, and words that usually mean an opinion ("seems", "careless", "should"). The summary counts how many gaps were followed up with an owner and a date.
- **takeaway** — Write in two or three sentences what the walk showed: the pattern, not a list. Where do the gaps sit? What worked? Then write when and where you'll walk next, usually the shift or the part of the day you didn't see this time.

## What good looks like

The example below is Line 2's line supervisor walking the paint booth on 15 July 2026, during the 10:00 changeover slot, to see whether the changeover routine card put back after the 5-Whys (nozzle off, a timed 90-second flush, a clean white test card before the first part) is how changeovers are really done. Four stops, all observed; nine facts recorded: five meet the standard, three are gaps and one has no standard to see against. All four of those have a follow-up with one owner and a due date. The checks flag one note left as first written, "seems well organised", and the takeaway rewrites it as the fact to count: the flush cart staged before the last part of the old colour. The gaps share a pattern the walk was planned to find: they sit at the edges of the day shift. A cover operator from another line was trained on the old card and stopped the flush at 41 seconds, the last changeover of the night went unsigned, and second shift's test cards had nowhere to go once the bench was locked. The routine card itself held up: the test card caught the short flush and a second flush cleared it.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking before watching.** — The first question changes the work: people explain what should happen and then do it while you're there. Watch a full cycle first, write what you saw, then ask.
- **Writing opinions in the fact column.** — "Operator didn't care about the flush" starts an argument and can't be checked. "Flush stopped at 41 seconds, timer not started" can, and it points at the fix: how that person was trained.
- **Walking without a standard to compare against.** — Without knowing what should happen, everything you see looks normal. Write what to look for at each stop before you go, and where there's no standard, record that as the finding.
- **Leaving gaps without an owner.** — A gap seen and left is worse than one never seen: everyone watched you notice it and nothing happened. Agree a follow-up, one owner and a date before you leave the floor.
- **Turning the walk into an inspection of people.** — If walks end with someone being corrected, people start hiding problems when you appear. Look at how the work is set up, and thank people for showing you what goes wrong.
- **Always walking the same shift at the same time.** — You see the part of the work that goes well when you're there. Walk the other shift, the handover and the exception, where the gaps usually are.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **5-Whys** (`five-whys`) — A countermeasure from a 5-Whys isn't proven until it's seen working at the work. A go-see walk is how you check, with the countermeasure as the standard to look for.
- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — A work instruction is the standard you see the work against. A gap on the walk is either a reason to train to it or evidence it needs rewriting.

Downstream:

- **Leader standard work** (`leader-standard-work`) — A regular walk is one of the timed tasks on a leader's routine, and the follow-ups from a walk often become daily checks on it.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Follow-ups that take more than a day go on the countermeasure log, so they're reviewed until they're closed with evidence.
- **Spaghetti diagram: walking and carrying paths** (`spaghetti-diagram`) — When a walk shows people leaving the work to fetch things, follow one person for a shift and draw their path to see what it costs.
- **Standard work chart** (`standard-work-chart`) — When the question is whether a job keeps pace, go and time it: about ten cycles, step by step, set against takt on a standard work chart.
- **Daily management huddle** (`daily-huddle`) — Bring what the walk found to the next huddle, so the team hears it and agrees the follow-ups that need more than one person.
- **Quick changeover** (`quick-changeover`) — Filming a changeover is a go-see with a camera: watch the real changeover with the people who do it, then take the film apart element by element.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing what the walk is to find out and where to stand
- Deciding whether what you saw meets the standard, and what the gap means
- Agreeing follow-ups with the people who will do them

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting patterns across several walks: the same gap at the same handover, the stop that's never visited
- Rewriting an opinion in the record as a question to go and check
- Suggesting stops and things to look for from a standard or a work instruction

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting what was seen at each stop and which gaps have an owner
- Turning handwritten notes from the clipboard into a clean record
- Laying out the route and record and exporting them as a workbook
