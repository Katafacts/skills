# Cascade map

Deploy a plan's objectives down the organisation, plan to unit to team, each with a measure, a target and one owner, with the catchball written down: what was asked, what each level proposed back, and what was agreed. Checks find plan objectives no unit has agreed to, unit objectives that don't trace up, and unit targets that don't add up to the target above them.

Family: Hoshin Kanri · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A plan's objectives only get delivered when someone below the leadership team takes each one on as their own. A cascade map records that hand-off. Each plan objective sits at the top of a branch; under it go the objectives each unit (a department, a site, a function) takes on to deliver it, and under those, what each team or cell does. Every row has a measure, a baseline, a target and one owner, and each level is more specific than the one above: "cut lead time to 12 days" becomes "cut the wait for machining from 6.5 days to 2", which becomes "run a second machining shift". The rows aren't handed down as orders. Each level is asked for something, says what it can actually do and why, and the two agree: that back-and-forth is called catchball, and the map keeps a line of it for every row. Where the units' measures are parts of the plan's measure (days waiting at each step add up to lead time; late shipments by cause add up to all late shipments), their targets can be added up, and the map shows whether they deliver the plan's target or fall short. A shortfall found in November is a conversation; found in October next year, it's a missed plan.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A yearly plan has just been agreed at the top and now has to become work that units and teams own.
- You want each objective in the plan to have a named owner below the leadership team, not just a strategy on a slide.
- You suspect the units' targets won't add up to the plan's: everyone could hit theirs and the plan would still miss.
- Catchball is happening in hallways and you want what each level proposed back, and what was agreed, written down.

Not when:

- There is no agreed plan to deploy yet. Set the plan's objectives first, for example with a hoshin kanri X-matrix; the cascade starts from them.
- You want to break one measure into the measures that make it, with no change of owner between levels. That is a key performance indicator (KPI) tree.
- You need who does, owns, and is told about each task in one process. That is a RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart.

## How to fill it in

- **plan** — Name the plan you're deploying and when it was agreed, so everyone knows which version the cascade follows.
- **planRows** — Copy the plan's objectives as Plan rows, numbered 1, 2, 3, with the plan's own measures, baselines, targets and owners. Don't reword them; the cascade starts from what was agreed.
- **lowerRows** — Under each plan row, add what each unit takes on: number it 1.1, 1.2 and say it sits under 1. Under a unit row, add team rows the same way (1.1.1 under 1.1). Each row should be more specific than the one above and say what that unit will change, not repeat the parent's words. Two or three levels are usually enough.
- **measures** — Give every row a measure with its unit, a baseline and a target, as numbers. Where you can, choose unit measures that are parts of the parent's: days an order waits at each step, late shipments by cause. Then the targets can be added up.
- **owner** — Name one person per row, not a team. The same person can own rows at two levels when that's how the organisation works; say so rather than invent a name.
- **addsUp** — On a row whose measure the rows under it are parts of, set "Adds up" to Yes: the changes under it should then add up to its own change. Set it to No where the rows under it drive the parent without a formula (a priority queue helps repeat-customer revenue, but doesn't add up to it).
- **catchball** — For each row that was argued, write what the level above asked for, what this level proposed back and why, and what was agreed. Mark the row Agreed or In discussion. A proposal back with a reason ("a machinist can't start before April") is the most useful line in the whole map.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. Each row's change (target minus baseline) is worked out, and on a row that adds up, the total of the changes under it. A row is flagged when the targets under it fall more than 2% short of its own change, when it's a plan objective no unit has agreed to, when it's a unit or team row that doesn't trace up to an existing row, when it has no level, owner, measure, baseline or target, when it's still in discussion, or when its number is used twice.
- **nextStep** — Write what was agreed about the flags: who takes the rest of a target that falls short (or how the target changes), when the rows in discussion will be settled, and when owners review their rows.

## What good looks like

The example below is Northwind Fabrication deploying its 2027 X-matrix to the shop, written up on 28 October 2026 after the first round of catchball. It has 13 rows: the plan's 3 objectives, 8 unit objectives and 2 team objectives, every one with an owner, measure, baseline and target, and all 10 unit and team rows trace up. Lead time adds up: the waits for machining (6.5 to 2 days), release (1.5 to 0.5) and welding (2 to 1.5) change by −6 days, exactly the 18 to 12 the plan needs; under machining, a second shift and two cross-trained fabricators do the work. Late shipments don't: the plan needs 14% to 6%, −8 points, and the three units' targets by cause (promise dates −4, changeovers −2, the machining backlog −1) give −7, so the row is flagged. The missing point is late steel deliveries, which no unit in the shop controls. Repeat-customer revenue doesn't add up by design: the priority queue and fewer status calls drive it without a formula. One row is still in discussion: the front office proposed a daily status sheet for the five biggest repeat customers instead of a weekly email to all. All three plan objectives have a unit that agreed to them. The plant manager takes the steel point to the suppliers, with the plan target moving to 7% if they can't commit.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying the plan's words down instead of making each level more specific.** — "Cut lead time" at every level tells nobody what to change. Each row should name what that unit or team will do differently, measured in its own terms.
- **Handing targets down without catchball.** — A target nobody below agreed to is a wish with a name on it. Ask, listen to what comes back and why, and write down what was agreed; the reasons are what make the plan believable.
- **Not checking that the targets add up.** — Targets set unit by unit rarely sum to the plan's. Where the measures are parts of the parent's, add them up: in the example, every unit hitting its target still leaves late shipments a point short.
- **Measures that can't be compared with the level above.** — If machining reports "jobs completed" and the plan tracks lead time, nobody can tell whether machining's success moved the plan. Choose unit measures in the parent's units wherever they exist.
- **Drawing the map once in January and never looking at it again.** — The cascade is what owners review each month. When a row falls behind, the map shows which plan objective is at risk and who to talk to.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`hoshin-kanri`) — The plan being deployed: its annual objectives, targets and owners are the cascade's Plan rows, and its strategies usually become unit rows.
- **True north statement** (`true-north-statement`) — Checks that the plan's objectives move the long-term direction before they're deployed.

Downstream:

- **Key performance indicator (KPI) tree** (`kpi-tree`) — When a unit's measure is made of several others, a tree breaks it down further within the unit.
- **Performance board** (`performance-board`) — Where each row's measure is tracked against its target month by month, with a countermeasure when it misses.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — When a row stays behind its target, an A3 finds out why and what to change.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding what each unit takes on and agreeing its target in catchball
- Deciding who takes the rest of a target that falls short, or whether the plan's target changes
- Naming one owner for each row

Analysis — AI helps:

- Suggesting unit measures that are parts of the plan's measure, so targets can be added up
- Spotting unit objectives that only repeat the plan's words
- Spotting rows that trace nowhere and plan objectives nobody has taken on

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out each change and adding up the changes under each row
- Keeping the numbering and the catchball record in step with the table
- Drawing the tree and laying out the workbook
