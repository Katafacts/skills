# Standard work chart

Time one operator's repeating job over about ten cycles and set it against takt, the pace the customer buys at. Write the steps in order as work, walk or wait; the sheet takes each step's lowest repeatable time, shows its spread, draws the cycle against takt and works out the load, the operators the work needs and the standard work in progress, and flags steps too unsteady to standardise yet.

Family: Standard work · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A standard work chart is the timed baseline for a repeating job: the agreed best way to do it today, written down with the times it really takes. It holds three things. Takt time, the pace the customer buys at: the working time available divided by how many units the customer needs in it. If the trucks need 60 parts in a 450-minute shift, a part has to be finished every 7.5 minutes, whatever the line could do on a good day. The work sequence: each step in the order the operator does it, timed over several cycles. And standard work in progress: the few units that must be in place for the job to repeat without waiting. Lay the steps end to end against takt and the chart answers questions a step list can't. Does the work fit in the time the customer allows? How much of the cycle is walking and waiting rather than work? How many people does the work need: total work content divided by takt? And which steps aren't steady yet? A step that takes 64 seconds one cycle and 118 the next has no standard time; something about it changes from cycle to cycle, and that has to be found and fixed before the step is written into a standard. The chart is not a target to push people to. It describes a stable method so that problems show up as gaps against it, and so that the next improvement starts from a known baseline.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You are setting the standard for a repeating job, or resetting it after a change (a new layout, a new tool, a new product).
- You need to know whether one person can keep pace with demand, or how many people a cell or line needs.
- Output varies from cycle to cycle or person to person and you want to see which steps vary, and how much.
- A value stream map's future state asks how many people a step needs, or a spaghetti diagram has changed where things are kept, and the work needs re-timing.
- You want a timed baseline to compare against when a standard work audit shows the job drifting.

Not when:

- The work doesn't repeat in a cycle of minutes: project work, or a task done once a week. Use a work instruction for the method and leave out the timing.
- You can't watch and time the real work. Times taken from a routing or from memory leave out exactly the walking, waiting and fumbling you're looking for.
- The job is mostly a machine cycle the operator watches. Chart the machine's capacity on a value stream map first; the operator's chart comes after.
- The aim is to judge someone's speed. Timing people against each other teaches them to hide the problems the chart is meant to find.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Name the job and the operator's position, and say where one cycle starts and where it ends, and what one unit is (a part, an order, a form). Tell the operator first what you are doing and why: the times set a standard for the work, not a score for the person. Watch a few cycles before you time anything, until you can see the steps repeat. Write who timed it, when, and how many cycles.
- **takt** — Enter the working minutes in a shift or a day after planned breaks, and how many units the customer needs in that time. Use demand (what the customer or the next process needs), not what you make today: the chart is for the pace you have to reach. Takt is worked out for you in seconds a unit. Say where both numbers come from.
- **elements** — Write each step in the order the operator does it, starting with a verb: "Make up a carton", "Walk to the unload rack (4 m)". Make a step something you can see start and stop, usually 5 to 60 seconds. Mark each one Work, Walk or Wait; never bury a walk or a wait inside a work step, or it can't be seen or cut. For a step that happens once every few units (wrap a pallet every fourth order), enter how many units go by each time; the sheet spreads it across them.
- **times** — Time each step over about ten cycles and write every reading, the slow ones too: they are the evidence. Don't average them. The sheet takes the lowest repeatable time (the second-lowest reading, a time the operator reached at least twice), so one lucky cycle doesn't set the standard, and shows how far the slowest reading sits above it. A step whose slowest time is more than 30% (and at least 5 seconds) above its lowest repeatable time is flagged: find out what made the slow cycles slow before you trust its time.
- **chart** — Nothing to enter here. Each step is drawn as a bar where it starts and ends in the cycle, coloured work, walk or wait, with a line at takt. Underneath, the sheet adds up work, walk and wait a unit, the spare time against takt, the load on one operator (work plus walk, as a % of takt) and the operators the work needs (work content ÷ takt). It flags a cycle longer than takt, a load or a cycle (waits included) above 95% of takt (no room for an ordinary hiccup), a load below 70%, a step longer than takt on its own, walking over 10% of the cycle, and steps not timed enough. The bars are per unit, an evened-out cycle: if the job is really paced by orders of mixed sizes, the swing from a one-part order to a three-part one is variation the chart won't show.
- **wip** — Write the fewest units that have to be in place, and where, for the operator to repeat the sequence without waiting: the unit being worked on, the next one ready, one in a machine. The sheet totals them and turns the total into minutes of demand. Keep buffers held for upstream stops separate: they are a different decision, and hiding them inside standard work in progress makes the number meaningless.
- **layout** — Describe where things are and the path the operator walks each cycle, with distances: where material arrives, where the finished unit goes, and the occasional trips. A sketch on the floor plan helps; for a long or tangled path, draw a spaghetti diagram.
- **findings** — Write what the chart shows and what happens next: the unstable steps to fix first, the work to take out, who does what by when, and when the job will be timed again. When it holds, turn the chart into the work instruction for the job.

## What good looks like

The example below is the day-shift packer at the end of Line 2, timed over ten orders on 4 August 2026, a month after the spaghetti diagram moved the label printer and the cartons to her bench. Takt is 450 seconds a part: 450 working minutes for the 60 parts packing needs a shift. Sixteen steps, timed, give 431.8 seconds of work content a part: 96% of takt, 0.96 of an operator. With the printer wait the cycle is 442.9 seconds, 98.4% of takt and only 7.1 seconds inside it, so one bad carton makes her late. The first fix costs nothing. She prints the order's label, waits 21 seconds for it, then walks to the unload rack. Instead she starts the print and goes: fetching and checking the order takes 49 seconds, so the label is ready when she gets back. The wait drops out, the cycle falls to 431.8 seconds and the spare rises from 7.1 to 18.2 seconds. The checks also flag making up a carton, which runs from 64 to 118 seconds, an 84% spread, because the new rack holds three carton sizes in one bin: the team stabilises that, with a labelled lane per size, before its time goes into the standard. Walking is now 21.2 seconds a part, 5% of the cycle, down from about 50 before the printer moved. Then they take work out rather than ask for speed: cutting foam from the roll is 52 seconds a part, and pre-cut sleeves would bring the load to about 84%. Standard work in progress is 4 parts, the order on the bench and the next one on the rack: 30 minutes of demand.

## Common mistakes

- **Standardising work that isn't stable yet.** — If a step takes 64 seconds one cycle and 118 the next, any time you write down is wrong half the time, and training people to it teaches them the chaos. Find what makes the slow cycles slow (a missing part, mixed stock, a fiddly fixture), fix it, then time it again. Stabilise, then standardise.
- **Averaging the times.** — An average folds the problems into the standard: every fumble and every search becomes part of the expected time. Take the lowest time the operator reaches repeatedly, and treat the gap between it and the slow cycles as the list of problems to solve.
- **Setting takt from what you make today.** — Takt comes from demand. A chart built on today's output can only confirm today's output; built on demand, it shows the gap you have to close.
- **Loading people to 100% of takt.** — A cycle with no spare seconds turns every ordinary hiccup into a late unit. Load to about 95% at most, and when the load is too high take work out of the job rather than asking people to go faster.
- **Accepting a machine wait as fixed.** — Standing by a printer, an oven or a press while it runs is waiting, even when it looks like part of the job. Start the machine, then do the next manual step while it runs, and come back when it's done. Look at the waits on the chart first: re-sequencing them out costs nothing.
- **Hiding walking and waiting inside work steps.** — "Fetch cartons and make one up" buries a walk that a layout change could remove. Give every walk and every wait its own row so the chart shows them and the next improvement can target them.
- **Spreading a remainder across everyone.** — If the work needs 2.2 people, sharing it evenly across three hides 0.8 of a person in waiting. Load each person close to takt, leave the remainder visible on one, and look for that much waste to take out before staffing it.
- **Timing the person, not the work.** — If the operator thinks the stopwatch is about them, they will work differently while you watch, and the times will be fiction. Explain first, time with them, and show them the chart before anyone else sees it.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — Timing a job is a go-see with a stopwatch: plan where to stand and what to watch for, and watch a few cycles before timing any. Go and see first, then chart.
- **Spaghetti diagram: walking and carrying paths** (`spaghetti-diagram`) — A spaghetti diagram shows the walking a layout causes; after the layout changes, the standard work chart times what's left and sets the new standard. A walk share over 10% on the chart is the cue to draw one.
- **Value stream map — current and future state** (`value-stream-map`) — The value stream map sets takt for the whole stream and shows which step can't keep pace. The standard work chart works out, step by step, how the work there fits takt and how many people it needs.
- **Quick changeover** (`quick-changeover`) — A changeover is a job of its own: once it is streamlined and split between two people, chart each person's sequence so they finish together.

Downstream:

- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — Once the chart holds, its steps in order become the work instruction's major steps, with the key points that make each one go right. The chart sets the sequence and the pace; the instruction teaches how.
- **Training matrix with I, L, U, O skill levels (ILUO)** (`iluo-training-matrix`) — Before marking someone U (works it unsupervised), watch them run the chart's sequence, unaided, within takt. The chart turns "can do it" into something you can check.
- **Standard work audit** (`standard-work-audit`) — The audit checks the steps are done as written; it doesn't time them. To check pace too, time a few cycles alongside the audit and compare them with the chart.
