# Pipeline management board

Run the weekly pipeline review from one board: open deals by stage with value and count, days in stage against each stage's limit, coverage against what's left to win, and red or green against the standards your team agreed. Deals over their limit, with no dated next step, past their close date or whose close date moved since last week are flagged, and the review ends with a few actions, each with one owner and a date.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A pipeline management board is what a sales team looks at in its weekly pipeline review, laid out so the review is about the deals that need help rather than a tour of every deal. Each open deal sits under its stage with its value, how many days it has been in that stage, its expected close date and its next step with a date. Each stage has an age limit: the longest a deal should sit there, usually taken from how long won deals took. A deal past its stage's limit is stuck, whatever the rep's confidence. Across the top, a few measures read red or green against standards the team agreed beforehand: coverage (the value of deals due to close by the end of the period, divided by what's still to win), and how many deals are over their limit, have no dated next step, or are past their close date. Coverage only counts deals closing between today and the period end, so a deal whose close date has already passed doesn't prop it up. The review starts with the reds, looks at the deals behind them, and agrees a few actions, each with one owner and a date, which are the first thing read the next week. It works for the same reason a board on a production floor does: the rules are set before the meeting, so the board shows what needs attention without anyone having to argue that it does. One caution: deals by stage are stock, a snapshot of today. A board can look full while nothing new is coming in, so it also shows flow (how many deals entered and left each stage since last week) and coverage for the next period. This quarter is mostly decided; whether next quarter will be is decided by what enters the first stage each week.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Your weekly pipeline review walks every deal in turn and runs out of time before the ones that matter.
- Deals sit in the same stage for weeks, or their close dates slide a few weeks at a time, and nobody notices until the forecast slips.
- Coverage is quoted as one number without saying which deals are in it, so deals past their close date keep it looking healthy.
- Actions agreed in the review are forgotten by the next one, because nobody owns them by a date.

Not when:

- You want to find why deals stall in one stage. The board shows where; a deal A3 or a stage conversion analysis finds why.
- You want a one-off look at which open deals are stuck. A deal aging report does that without the coverage and standards.
- You're planning how a single large deal gets to signature. Use a mutual action plan with the buyer.
- You track the commercial team's measures month by month against plan. That's a visibility board; this one is about the deals.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Say which pipeline the board covers (a team, a segment, a region), the date of the review, and what the values mean: annual value, total contract value or first-year revenue. Ages are counted to the review date, so update the board just before the review, not the week before.
- **stages** — List the stages in pipeline order, and give each an age limit in days: the longest a deal should sit there before someone asks why. A fair start is how long most won deals spent in the stage last quarter, for example 85 in 100 of them. A stage with no limit can never show a deal as stuck, so the board flags it. Each week, write how many deals left each stage since the last board (moved on, won or lost); the board works out how many entered from the deals' stage dates.
- **deals** — One row per open deal: its stage (from the list), value, rep, the date it entered its current stage, the date it's expected to close, and the next step with its date. Use the date the deal entered the stage, not the date it was created. If you can, copy each deal's close date from last week's board alongside, so a date that has moved shows up instead of quietly replacing the old one. A next step counts only with a date that hasn't passed: "follow up" or "waiting on them" with no date is a deal nobody is moving. Leave won and lost deals off.
- **period** — Write the period coverage is counted for (usually the quarter), its end date, the target and what's already been won. Still to win is the target minus what's won. Add the next period's end date and target, so the board shows next-period coverage too. Each week, copy that week's two coverage figures into the week-by-week table so the trend shows.
- **standards** — Agree the standards before the review, not during it. For coverage, work out how many dollars of pipeline it takes to win one. Weight each deal closing in the period by how often deals at its stage are won, add them up, and divide the pipeline by that total: that's break-even coverage. If two in three deals at this point are won, break-even is 1.5 times what's left to win. Set the standard a little above break-even. Late in a quarter, when most of what's left is in late stages, the number is lower than at the start. For the counts, most teams start at zero deals over their limit, without a dated next step or past their close date, and read anything above that as red.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For each deal the board works out the days in stage and its status against the limit (over, at the limit today, within, or no limit), and flags no stage date, no stage limit, no dated next step, no close date, a close date that has passed, and a close date that moved since last week's board. It totals each stage and counts the deals that entered it since last week, works out coverage for this period and the next, and reads each measure red or green against its standard; next-period coverage below the standard is flagged. It also flags stages with no limit and actions with no owner or date.
- **actions** — In the review, read the reds first and then the deals behind them. Agree a few actions, not one per deal: each says what will happen, which deal or finding it answers, one owner and a due date. Next week, read these before the board.
- **review** — Write in a few lines what the board says this week, which deal or stage matters most and when it's read again. Write the pattern as well as the deals: three stuck deals in one stage is a process problem, not three bits of bad luck.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' mid-market pod at its Monday pipeline review on 16 March 2026: the same 14 open deals, $331,200 a year, as the pod's deal aging report that day, with the same stage limits. Three findings matter. First, coverage is green but thin: $115,200 is due to close by 31 March against $74,400 still to win, 1.55x against a 1.5x standard that is worked out, not guessed (weighted by stage win rates, 1.41x is break-even). It was 2.00x a week ago; Corbel Health's close date moved from 27 March to 17 April, and Brightwater Utilities alone is $43,200 of what's due. Second, deals aren't moving: four, $108,000, are over their stage's limit, three of them in demo waiting for a proposal and Fenwick & Co at 55 days in proposal and past its close date; three more reach their limit today and five have no dated next step. Third, next quarter reads 1.50x ($180,000 against $120,000), but all of it is in discovery, demo or early proposal, and only one new deal entered the pipeline this week. The review agreed four actions, each with one owner and a date that week.

## Common mistakes

- **Walking every deal in turn.** — The review runs out of time before it reaches the deals that need help, and the reps whose deals are fine sit through it. Read the reds, then only the deals behind them.
- **Counting deals past their close date in coverage.** — A deal that was meant to close last month and hasn't is the least likely to close this month. Left in, it makes coverage look healthy exactly when it isn't. Agree a new date with the buyer or close it.
- **Accepting "waiting on them" as a next step.** — A next step with no date is a deal nobody is moving. Every open deal needs something booked, with a date, that moves it forward.
- **Measuring age from when the deal was created.** — An old deal moving steadily through its stages is fine; a young one stuck in demo for three weeks isn't. Age in the current stage, against that stage's limit, shows where deals are stuck.
- **Setting the standards in the meeting.** — A standard agreed after seeing the number gets set to whatever makes it green. Agree coverage and the limits beforehand, from how won deals behaved, and change them deliberately.
- **An action for every deal, owned by the team.** — Twenty shared actions aren't followed up. Agree the vital few, each with one owner and a date, and read them first next week.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Deal aging report** (`deal-aging-report`) — Same stage limits and the same days-in-stage logic, so a deal over its limit here is over it there. The aging report is the deeper look at which deals are stuck and why; the board is the weekly view.
- **Stage conversion analysis** (`stage-conversion-analysis`) — How many deals at each stage end up won is what tells you how much coverage you need. In the worked example, 3 of 4 deals in negotiation are won and 3 of 6 in proposal, so $96,000 × 3/4 + $19,200 × 3/6 = $81,600 of the $115,200 closing should close: 1.41x is break-even, and the 1.5x standard adds a small margin.
- **Visibility board** (`visibility-board`) — The visibility board tracks the commercial team's measures week by week against plan; this board is where a sales team reads its own deals behind those numbers.

Downstream:

- **Deal A3 — win-rate root cause analysis** (`deal-a3`) — When the same stage is red week after week, the board has found a pattern, not bad luck. A deal A3 finds its cause.
- **Mutual action plan** (`mutual-action-plan`) — For a deal that matters to the period, like Northline Freight in the example, the mutual action plan holds the dated steps to signature that feed the board's next step.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — An action that runs past a week or two, or answers a pattern rather than one deal, goes on the countermeasure log so it is followed to the end.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Agreeing the standards and the stage limits
- Deciding what to do about a stuck deal, and whether to close it as lost
- Choosing the few actions that matter this week, and who owns them

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting a stage that is red week after week, or a rep whose deals keep missing next steps
- Suggesting a coverage standard from past stage conversion
- Drafting the summary of what the board says

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out days in stage, coverage and red or green for every measure
- Pulling the open deals out of the sales system into the board
- Laying the board out and exporting it as a workbook
