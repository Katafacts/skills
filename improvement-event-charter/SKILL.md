# Kaizen event charter

One page agreed before a kaizen event starts: the problem, the measures with baseline, target and how each is measured, the day-by-day agenda, what the event hands over, who owns the result afterwards, and whether you're ready to start. The 30-, 60- and 90-day check dates are worked out for you.

Family: Kaizen events · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A kaizen event charter is the agreement a team makes before a short, focused improvement event, usually two to five days. It says what problem the event tackles and how big it is today; the measures it must move, each with a baseline, a target and exactly how it's measured; what's in and out of scope; what the team works on each day and what each day produces; what the event hands over; the limits the team works within; who is on the team and who is accountable; and a readiness checklist. The change each target asks for, the date of each agenda day and the 30-, 60- and 90-day follow-up checks are calculated, and the roster is checked for exactly one accountable person.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're about to run a focused event of a few days on one process and want the team, the sponsor and the process owner to agree what success looks like before it starts.
- You want to know honestly whether you're ready: baseline measured, people released, real work booked for the event days.
- You want the result checked after the event, not just celebrated on the last day: the charter sets the yardstick and the dates for the follow-up checks.

Not when:

- You're still finding out whether there's a real problem here, or how big it is. Go and see, or scope it first; a charter needs a measured baseline.
- One person can make the fix this week. Just do it and record it; a charter for a small fix is overhead.
- The fix needs capital, a new system or months of work. That's a project with a business case and an implementation plan, not an event.

## How to fill it in

- **opportunityStatement** — Write the problem with a number that shows how big it is today, where you saw it and why it matters now.
- **objective** — Write one sentence: the measure, from what to what, measured how, and by when. Say that it must still hold at the follow-up checks, not only on the last day of the event.
- **metrics** — List the two or three measures the event must move. For each, give the baseline, the target and how it's measured: the source, where the clock starts and stops, and how often. The 30-day check must use the same yardstick, or before and after can't be compared. Add one measure that must not get worse, so the team can't win one number by losing another. The change each target asks for is calculated.
- **scopeIn** — Say where the process starts and ends, and which area, shifts or teams are included. Include every shift that does the work.
- **scopeOut** — Name the tempting nearby work you're deliberately leaving alone: a related process, a decision already made, capital spend.
- **agenda** — Write what the team works on each day and what each day must produce. Start at the work: day 1 is spent watching and timing the real process, not in a meeting room. Try changes on real work during the event, and keep the last day for writing the new standard, training and the report-out. Each day's date and the follow-up check dates are calculated from the start and end dates; a check that falls on a weekend moves to the Monday.
- **deliverables** — List what the event hands over: the new standard work or routine card, any fixtures or forms, and a report-out with open actions, one owner and a date each. Then list the constraints: no capital spend, decisions that stay as they are, and that the team is released full time.
- **roster** — List the team and each person's role: responsible, accountable, consulted or informed. Make the process owner, the person who runs the process every day after the event, the one Accountable person. The sponsor clears time and removes blockers but isn't accountable for the work. Name a team leader, include the people who do the work on every shift, and release the team full time for the event.
- **readiness** — List checkable things that must be true before day 1: baseline measured, team released full time, real work booked for the event days, the sponsor's time for the report-out. Tick each only when it's done, with an owner and a due date for the rest.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences: what's ready, what's still open and the biggest risk to the result, and who owns the result after the event.

## What good looks like

The example below charters the 10–12 March paint booth changeover event that the sustainment audit later checks. Its main measure is changeover time, last good unit to first good unit: 45 minutes now, a target of 25 (20 minutes, or 44.4%, less). A second measure is the minutes the booth stands still while people fetch things (14 to 3), and the changeovers per shift must stay at 2. The line supervisor, who runs the booth every day, is the one accountable person; the plant manager sponsors and is consulted. Four of six readiness items are done (67%). One open item matters most: no second-shift operator is released for the event, and at the 30-day check the sustainment audit finds second shift doing the changeover differently. The follow-up checks fall on 13 April (the 30th day is a Saturday, so it moves to the Monday), 11 May and 10 June.

## Common mistakes

- **Making the sponsor the accountable person.** — The sponsor leaves on Friday. The process owner runs the process every day afterwards, so the result is theirs to keep; if they aren't accountable, nobody is.
- **Measuring the result in event week only, or with a different yardstick at the follow-up.** — Everyone is watching during the event. Only the 30-, 60- and 90-day checks, measured the same way as the baseline, show whether the new way holds.
- **A part-time team, or one shift designing the new way for everyone.** — People half on the event get pulled back to their day job, and a shift that wasn't in the room has to adopt a way of working it never tried. Release the team full time and include every shift.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Scoping canvas** (`scoping-canvas`) — The canvas sets the event's goal and boundaries; the charter turns them into measures, an agenda, a team and a readiness check.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — In the worked example, the A3's sequencing rule left changeover time untouched, which is what this event goes after.
- **Impact/effort matrix: sort ideas fast** (`impact-effort-matrix`) — A Plan it idea that needs a team and a few focused days often becomes an event.
- **Spaghetti diagram: walking and carrying paths** (`spaghetti-diagram`) — A spaghetti diagram gives a layout event its baseline (metres a shift today) and a target for the proposed layout.

Downstream:

- **RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart** (`raci-chart`) — The charter names one accountable owner for the event; the chart does the same for every task and decision in the process the event changes.
- **Sustainment audit** (`sustainment-audit`) — At the follow-up checks, the audit re-measures the charter's metrics the same way and checks that the event's changes are still how the work is done.
- **Quick changeover** (`quick-changeover`) — For a changeover event, the team films a real changeover and fills in the quick changeover worksheet: internal and external, converted, streamlined and written into the standard.

Judgement — stays with you:

Analysis — AI helps:

- Drafting a measurable objective from a rough goal, and saying plainly when the input has no real number
- Drafting a readiness checklist and a day-by-day agenda that fit the scope

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out the change each target asks for, the agenda dates and the 30-, 60- and 90-day check dates
- Tallying the readiness checklist and flagging what's not ready
- Checking the roster for a missing or duplicated accountable person
