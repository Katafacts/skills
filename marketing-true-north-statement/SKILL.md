# Marketing true north statement

Write the direction a marketing team steers by as a few dimensions of an ideal state in the buyer's and the market's terms (reach, trust, easy to buy, pipeline quality, cost to acquire), each with a measure, where it stands today and the ideal. Then check that this year's revenue objectives each move one, and see which dimensions nothing this year moves.

Family: Hoshin Kanri · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A marketing true north statement is the direction a marketing team steers by over many years, written down so it can be checked. It starts with one sentence about how the market would see you at your best, then breaks that into a few dimensions that would all be true at the ideal, each about buyers and the market rather than about how sales works a deal: whether the teams you could serve know you exist, whether they trust you before the first call, whether buying from you is easy, whether the pipeline you create is pipeline sales can win, and whether what you spend reaches people who could buy. Each dimension gets the ideal in words, one measure, today's value and the measure's value at the ideal. The ideal is set at the limit, such as every account in the segment, every customer willing to vouch for you, nothing spent on buyers who could never buy, so it can't be fully reached; that is what keeps it pointing past this year's targets. The statement ends with a check against the company's revenue plan: each of this year's revenue objectives should move one of marketing's dimensions, and any dimension nothing moves this year should be a choice the head of marketing made on purpose, not something the plan dropped without anyone noticing.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- The company's revenue plan for the year has just been agreed and marketing wants to see which of its long-term aims the plan actually moves.
- Marketing's goals change every quarter with the campaign calendar and the team wants a direction that doesn't.
- The revenue plan is all win rate and deal speed, and you suspect reaching new buyers has quietly dropped out of it.
- A new head of marketing wants to agree with the leadership team what marketing is ultimately for, in numbers that can be checked.

Not when:

- You need this year's revenue objectives, targets and owners. That is the revenue hoshin kanri X-matrix; the true north sits above it.
- You need to know what marketing has agreed to deliver this year under each revenue objective. That is the revenue cascade map.
- You want to define how each marketing measure is calculated and where its data comes from. That is a marketing metric definition sheet; the true north uses its measures.

## How to fill it in

- **statement** — Write one sentence about how the market would see you at your best, in words a buyer would recognise: "every revenue team that could use us knows us and trusts us on the word of teams like theirs", not "best-in-class demand engine".
- **dimensions** — Name three to five dimensions that would all be true at that ideal, one or two words each, about buyers and the market: reach, trust, easy to buy, pipeline quality and cost to acquire cover most marketing teams. Leave sales process steps, such as deal reviews or proposal approvals, to sales. Each should still matter in ten years; if it only matters this year, it's an objective.
- **ideal** — For each dimension, write the ideal in words, at the limit: every team in the segment knows who you are; every customer would vouch for you; nothing is spent reaching buyers who could never buy. If a few years' work could reach it, push it further.
- **numbers** — Pick one measure for each dimension, one you already record (accounts actively worked, customers willing to be a reference, cost per sales-qualified lead), and give today's value and the value at the ideal, usually 100% or zero. Say which way is better. If a three-to-five-year plan has a target for that measure, add it as the long-term target; leave it blank if not.
- **owner** — Name one person in marketing who reports each dimension when the plan is reviewed. They don't deliver it alone; they make sure it's looked at.
- **objectives** — Name the revenue plan you're checking, then copy each of this year's revenue objectives as written on it and say which marketing dimension it moves, with one line on how and what marketing's part is. Use the dimension's name exactly; in the workbook it's a dropdown.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For each dimension the statement works out the gap from today to the ideal, the share of that gap the long-term target closes, and how many of this year's objectives move it. It flags a missing measure, value or owner, today already at the ideal, and a long-term target that reaches the ideal (so the ideal is really a target) or moves away from it. Each objective that moves no dimension is flagged, and the dimensions nothing this year moves are listed.
- **nextStep** — Write what was agreed: for each dimension nothing this year moves, whether it is held on purpose or taken to the revenue plan as a metric or objective, any ideal that was pushed further, and when the statement is next reviewed, usually with next year's plan.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' marketing true north, written by the head of marketing on 15 April 2026, a week after the revenue X-matrix was agreed, with values from January to March 2026. Five dimensions: reach (25% of the 40 segment accounts actively worked, ideal 100%), trust (14% of customers, 9 of 64, willing to be a reference, ideal 100%), easy to buy (48% of surveyed buyers, 12 of 25, got what they needed without chasing, ideal 100%), pipeline quality (60% of sales-qualified leads become qualified opportunities, ideal 100%; lead qualification is where the sales funnel loses most, 40%) and cost to acquire ($2,100 per sales-qualified lead, ideal $1,302, which is today's cost without the 38% of program spend that reached accounts outside the profile). Two dimensions have a long-term target: reach, 60% by March 2029, closes 47% of its 75-point gap, and cost, the agreed $1,500 or less, closes 75% of its $798 gap. All three revenue objectives move a dimension: the win rate moves pipeline quality, the proposal wait moves easy to buy, and net revenue retention moves trust. So the plan moves 3 of the 5, and nothing this year moves reach or cost to acquire. No checks are flagged. Cost to acquire is held on purpose, because the fit check added before handover in April should cut poor-fit spend and bring the cost toward $1,500, and it is reported monthly. Reach is not: accounts worked goes onto the X-matrix as a metric now, and the head of marketing takes a reach objective (25% to 40% of the segment by March 2027) to the October business review.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing sales process steps as marketing's dimensions.** — A gate in discovery or a cap on proposals is how sales works a deal, and sales owns it. Marketing's true north is about buyers and the market: who knows you, who trusts you, how easy you are to buy from, and what that costs.
- **Writing campaign targets and calling them a true north.** — "40% of the segment by March" is an objective. Once it's met, nothing points further. Set the ideal at the limit (every account in the segment) and make 40% a milestone toward it.
- **Letting the revenue plan quietly drop reach.** — Revenue plans pull toward the accounts already in the pipeline: win rate, deal speed, expansion. A year where nothing moves reach can be the right call, but decide it out loud; the statement lists the dimensions nothing moves so the choice is made on purpose.
- **A dimension with no measure.** — "Brand strength" can't tell anyone whether this year was better than last. Pick one measure you already record, such as customers willing to be named as a reference, and improve the measure later.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Ideal customer profile (ICP) canvas** (`icp-canvas`) — Defines who could buy from you, which is what the reach, pipeline quality and cost to acquire dimensions count against.

Downstream:

- **Revenue hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`revenue-hoshin-kanri`) — The revenue plan whose annual objectives are checked against marketing's dimensions; a dimension nothing moves can go onto it as a metric or objective.
- **Revenue cascade map** (`revenue-cascade-map`) — Where marketing's part of each revenue objective is agreed, with a measure, a target and an owner.
- **Marketing metric definition sheet** (`marketing-metric-definition-sheet`) — Pins down how each dimension's measure is calculated and where its data comes from, so today's value means the same thing next year.
