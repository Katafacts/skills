# Qualification standard work

Decide which deals get a full sales cycle the same way every time: a few pass/fail criteria, each with the evidence that proves it and the question that gets it, a routing rule (qualified, nurture or disqualify) worked out from the answers, and a log of the deals checked. Deals advanced without passing, criteria with no evidence standard and answers still unknown are flagged, not hidden.

Family: Standard work · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

Qualification is the decision, early in a sale, about whether a deal deserves the team's time: a discovery call, a demo, a place in the forecast. Done by feel, it varies by rep and by mood, and the pipeline fills with deals that were never going to close. Qualification standard work writes the decision down as a short list of criteria, each one something a deal clearly passes or fails: the company is in the size band, someone owns revenue operations, there's a real reason to buy now, the person who signs is reachable. Each criterion carries three things that make it usable on a call: the line it has to clear, the evidence that proves it (what the buyer says or the records show, never the rep's impression) and the one question that gets that evidence. Each also says what a failure means: nurture (not now; come back when something changes) or disqualify (not this sale). The routing rule then works itself out the same way for everyone. Any disqualifying failure: disqualify. Every criterion passed: qualified, and only then does the deal advance. Anything else, including an answer nobody has asked for yet: nurture. Under the standard sits a log of the deals checked, with what was actually done with each, so a deal pushed forward without passing shows up, and the team can see which criterion stops the most deals and which ones reps aren't asking about.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Reps decide differently which deals to pursue, and the pipeline is full of deals that stall with no decision.
- You have an ideal customer profile (ICP) and a list of disqualifiers, and want them used on every deal rather than remembered on some.
- A new rep is joining and needs the team's judgement in a form they can use on their first calls.
- Forecast reviews keep arguing about whether a deal is real. A shared standard turns the argument into a list of unanswered questions.

Not when:

- You don't know yet who your good customers are. Build the ideal customer profile canvas and its anti-profile from won and lost deals first; a standard with nothing behind it just adds steps.
- You want to rank a list of accounts before anyone has spoken to them. A fit-criteria scorecard does that; qualification needs answers from a conversation.
- You want a script for the discovery call itself. Discovery call standard work covers that; qualification decides who gets the call.
- Each deal is large and unique enough that the decision needs a full review. Use the criteria as a starting checklist, not as the rule.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Say in a sentence what qualifying decides and when it runs, for example: before a discovery call is booked, whether the deal gets a full cycle, waits in nurture or is dropped. Say what it was built from, so people trust it.
- **criteria** — Write four to eight criteria, taken from what your won and lost deals show: the profile's must-haves and its hard disqualifiers. Write each so a deal clearly passes or fails it ("someone owns revenue operations", not "good fit"), and state the line it has to clear ("100 to 2,000 employees"). Fewer is better: each one is a question a rep has to ask on an early call.
- **evidence** — For each criterion, write what proves it: what the buyer says, or what the records show ("the buyer's name and the date of the call they joined"). A form fill, a friendly tone or a rep's sense of urgency is not evidence. Then write the one question that gets the evidence, in words a rep would actually say on a call. A criterion with no evidence standard gets passed on hope.
- **route** — For each criterion, choose what happens to a deal that fails it. Disqualify when failing it means the deal won't work this time, whatever else is true (nobody to run the product; a competitor already in place). Nurture when it means not now (no trigger yet; budget next year). The rule then works itself out: any disqualifying failure, disqualify; every criterion passed, qualified; anything else, nurture.
- **deals** — For each deal, mark Pass, Fail or Unknown on every criterion from what was heard, with the date and who checked it, and note the evidence in a few words. Unknown is honest; it is not a pass. Then record what was actually done: advance, nurture or disqualify. If you advance a deal the rule doesn't qualify, say why in the note.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the sheet works it out. It routes each deal from its answers, and flags a deal advanced without passing, a decision that differs from the rule, and how many answers are still unknown. For each criterion it flags a missing pass line, evidence standard, question or route, and counts how many deals passed, failed or are unknown. It totals the routes and names the criterion that stops the most deals.
- **review** — Every few weeks, read the log with the team. A criterion that is nearly always unknown is one reps aren't asking about; one that never fails may not be needed; decisions that keep overriding the rule for the same reason mean the standard needs changing. Change the standard, then keep using it.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' qualification check for mid-market logistics deals, built on 20 March 2026 from its ideal customer profile and anti-profile. Seven criteria: company size, not a tiny self-serve team, someone owning revenue operations, no pipeline analytics tool already in place, a real trigger, a route to the economic buyer, and budget this year. The three that come from the anti-profile's hard disqualifiers (tiny self-serve team, no one owning revenue operations, a tool already in place) disqualify if failed; the rest send a deal to nurture. Budget has no evidence standard yet, and the check flags it. Seven deals were checked in the first week: Northline Freight and Harbor Line Freight qualified and advanced; Summit Carriers (too large), Bluewater Shipping (no trigger) and Crestway Transport go to nurture; Ridgeline Couriers (already runs a pipeline analytics tool) and Fleetwise Movers (a 14-person self-serve trial) are disqualified. The reps' decisions agree with the rule on six of the seven. Crestway was advanced to discovery with the buyer and the budget still unknown, and the check flags it. Company size failed the most deals (2), and budget is unknown on five of the seven, which is why agreeing what counts as proof of budget is the first change to the standard.

## Common mistakes

- **Criteria that can't fail.** — "Good fit" or "engaged contact" pass every deal a rep likes. Write each criterion as a line a deal clearly clears or doesn't, with a number or a named fact where you can.
- **Passing on interest instead of evidence.** — A form fill, a demo request or a warm call is interest, not a reason to buy now. Without an evidence standard, the criterion gets passed on hope and the deal stalls later with no decision.
- **Treating unknown as a pass.** — Unasked questions are where deals fall apart: the buyer nobody met, the budget nobody asked about. Until it's answered, the deal is nurture, however good it feels.
- **Advancing deals the rule doesn't qualify, without saying why.** — Quiet overrides turn the standard back into individual judgement. If the same override keeps happening for a good reason, change the standard; otherwise get the answers first.
- **Too many criteria.** — Twelve criteria take three calls to answer, so reps skip them. Keep the four to eight that your won and lost deals show actually separate the two.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Ideal customer profile (ICP) canvas** (`icp-canvas`) — The canvas's must-haves, with the win rates behind them, become the pass/fail criteria here.
- **Anti-ideal customer profile (anti-ICP)** (`anti-icp`) — Its hard disqualifiers become the criteria whose failure disqualifies a deal; its soft red flags usually become nurture criteria.
- **Fit-criteria scorecard** (`fit-criteria-scorecard`) — The scorecard ranks accounts before anyone has spoken to them; qualification checks the ones that respond, with answers from the buyer.

Downstream:

- **Discovery call standard work** (`discovery-standard-work`) — A qualified deal goes on to a discovery call; qualification decides who gets the call, discovery what happens on it.
- **Deal A3 — win-rate root cause analysis** (`deal-a3`) — When lost deals trace back to something qualification should have caught, the A3 finds the cause and the fix usually lands here as a new or sharper criterion.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding which criteria really separate won deals from lost ones
- Deciding whether a failure means nurture or disqualify
- Choosing when an override is right, and saying why

Analysis — AI helps:

- Sharpening a vague criterion into a line a deal clearly passes or fails
- Suggesting a question that gets the evidence on a call
- Reading call notes for answers to the criteria still marked unknown

Drudgery — automated:

- Routing each deal from its answers
- Counting passes, fails and unknowns by criterion
- Flagging deals advanced without passing and exporting the log as a workbook
