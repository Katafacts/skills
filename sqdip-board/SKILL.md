# SQDIP (safety, quality, delivery, inventory, productivity) shift board

Answers one question, shift by shift: did this shift meet its rule, and if not, why not? The performance grid on a production line: one row per letter, one square per shift, coloured from the numbers rather than argued over — where a bad changeover shows up as five reds in the same shift and the reasons, not the colours, tell you it was one problem.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: operations

## What it is

The performance grid pointed at a production area, with one row for each letter: safety, quality, delivery, inventory and productivity. One square per shift, or per day where the line runs one shift. Green met the rule, red missed it, blue means the number is not in yet, and there is no amber, because a third colour is where an uncomfortable miss goes to be called "nearly fine". Fixing the rows buys something real on a site with many cells: anyone can walk up to any board and read it without translation. It only works where every letter maps to something the cell can move inside a shift or two; where one doesn't — an office team has no meaningful inventory row — use the plain performance grid with that team's own critical few. Quality, delivery, inventory and productivity are a common four-row set; safety goes in front of them, not after, because the order is a priority order, and on a huddle that overruns the last row isn't read at all. Two rules make the board work. Mark one square per shift: on a two-shift line, marking once a day averages the shifts together, and a problem that belongs to one shift disappears into a green. And contain a red the same shift, before the next crew starts: a red that waits for tomorrow's meeting repeats on the shift in between. The worked example shows both — thirteen reds, every one on a night shift, and what three nights of late containment cost.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A production cell, line or area with repeating work and numbers that land by the end of the shift.
- A site with several areas that wants boards anyone can read without relearning them each time.
- All five rows map to something this area can move within a shift or two — not to something decided elsewhere.
- You run more than one shift and suspect the shifts are not the same, which a daily average will never show you.

Not when:

- Where a letter has no real meaning for this area. An invented inventory row is worse than four honest ones.
- Office, service and knowledge-work teams as a default — use the performance grid with that team's own critical few. The shape is identical; only the rows differ.
- As an input to appraising a shift or the people on it. The moment a square becomes a story about a crew, the reporting goes quietly green and the board stops being worth reading.
- Where the numbers only arrive the following week. A measure that cannot be marked at handover can only ever be blue.
- When the question is whether you're on track against a plan from a starting point, judged cumulatively over months, use the performance board. This board asks whether each shift met its rule.

## How to fill it in

- **measures** — Write one row per letter, in the same order on every board on the site. Safety is almost always a judgement rather than a number — any injury, any near miss, any guard or interlock bypassed, and the square is red. Quality, delivery, inventory and productivity take a standing number the area can actually move: first pass yield, schedule attainment, work in progress at handover, good units per labour hour. Name the source precisely enough that two shifts cannot read it differently, and give every row an owner who is a person on the floor rather than a department.
- **cadence** — Mark one square per shift wherever more than one shift runs. This is the single most important choice on the board: marking once a day averages the shifts together, and the difference between them is very often the finding. A cell running one shift can mark once a day. Anything longer than a day and this stops being daily management and becomes reporting.
- **entries** — Mark at handover, by the shift that worked it, in the two minutes before they go home. A number colours its own square against the standing goal; safety is marked by judgement. Every red gets one line of fact — what happened, never who — written by the people who were there. That line cannot be reconstructed the next morning, and it is worth more than the colour: when one changeover puts five rows red at once, only the reasons show that it was one problem rather than five.
- **countermeasures** — Containment first, that same shift if the next shift is at risk — a setter held over, a jam-clearing procedure re-walked, a relief operator called. Then the permanent countermeasure once the cause is understood, and keep the two apart so overtime cover is never mistaken for a solved problem. Cap what is open at once. Containment that relies on a person working extra hours is a debt, and the date on the permanent fix is when you stop paying it.
- **coverage** — Calculated from the marked board — don't type it; check it against what the shifts recorded. It shows the colours, reds with no reason, reds with nothing being done about their cause, and the Pareto of reasons. A red whose cause is already being worked counts as covered even when no countermeasure names that exact square — one bad changeover throws several reds, and a countermeasure attacks the cause rather than each square it produced.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences on what the board shows, shift by shift: which row goes red most, whether one shift carries the reds, and the reason that keeps repeating. Use only what was marked, and if there is too little history to claim a shift pattern, say so.

## What good looks like

The example below is an assembly cell on two shifts, marked per shift for a week. Fifty squares, thirteen red — and every single red is on a night shift. Not one day shift missed anything. Averaged across a day most of those misses vanish into a green, and the cell looks like it has a quality problem and a delivery problem and an inventory problem; read shift by shift it has one problem wearing four measures' clothes. Ten of the thirteen reds trace to a changeover run without a trained setter, which is why one Tuesday night shows red on all five rows at once, including a bypassed interlock. And that Tuesday night didn't need to happen: Monday night's red should have put a trained setter on the next changeover, but cover only began on Thursday night, so the two nights in between added six more reds. Same-shift containment is the rule this board exists for. Note what the repeat-miss prompt found: nothing. Reds alternate with green day shifts, so no row ever shows two in a row — the reasons found the pattern and the streak rule could not. One red has no reason recorded, and it is also the only one with nothing being done about it. Those are the same fact.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding a safety row because the letters say so, with nothing behind it but a number that is always zero** — A row that reads green every shift for a year teaches people to stop looking at it, and it will still read green on the shift somebody gets hurt. Either measure something that genuinely moves — near misses reported, safety observations done, hazards closed out — or run the four rows without safety and be honest that safety is handled somewhere else.
- **Marking once a day when the line runs two shifts** — It averages the shifts together and hides the commonest finding on a multi-shift line: that the shifts are not the same. A problem belonging to nights disappears into a green day, and the board quietly stops being able to see the thing most worth seeing.
- **Forcing a letter that does not fit** — An inventory row on a team with no inventory teaches everyone that the board is a form to be completed rather than a tool to be used, and that lesson spreads to the rows that do matter. Four honest rows beat five with a passenger.
- **Treating the safety row as a statistic** — It is the one row where the rule is a judgement and the right response to a red is always to go and look, that shift. A safety square that gets discussed at the end of the week alongside productivity has already been mis-framed.
- **Calling overtime cover a fix** — Holding a setter over is good containment and a person paying for it with their evening. Log it as containment, keep it open for as long as it is running, and put a date on the permanent countermeasure, or the cell runs on goodwill until the goodwill runs out.
- **The board becoming a report to the plant manager** — Once its audience is upward the numbers get managed instead of the work, and the shift that marks honestly looks worse than the shift that does not. The crew marks its own squares for its own use; anyone above reads the same board rather than asking for a version of it.
- **Five reds from one cause read as five problems** — A bad changeover can put every row red in the same shift. Counting colours makes that look like a cell falling apart; reading reasons shows one problem with one countermeasure. This is exactly why the reason line is worth more than the square.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Performance grid** (`performance-grid`) — The same method with the rows left open. Use that one wherever the five letters do not each map to something the team can move — which is most office, service and knowledge-work teams.

Downstream:

- **Daily management huddle** (`daily-huddle`) — The handover conversation this board exists for. On a shift pattern the huddle is where the outgoing crew tells the incoming one what went red and what has been done about it.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Where a repeat cause goes to be removed, and where you find out whether overtime containment ever turned into a permanent fix.
- **Shift handover** (`shift-handover`) — What travels with the board between crews. A red marked at handover and not explained to the incoming shift is a red that gets re-discovered rather than worked.
- **Pareto chart** (`pareto-chart`) — Which reason keeps repeating across a longer window than one week of shifts, and across several cells at once.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — The cycle a repeating cause has earned — a changeover that needs trained setters and revised standard work is not something a shift can contain its way out of.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding whether all five letters genuinely fit this area, or whether four honest rows would be better
- Setting a goal a shift can actually meet with the equipment and people it has
- Judging whether a red is worth stopping for now or watching another shift
- Deciding when containment paid for in overtime has gone on long enough to stop being acceptable

Analysis — AI helps:

- Reading the board by shift rather than by day, and saying plainly when one shift carries the reds
- Spotting that one cause has put several rows red in the same shift, so it is one problem and not five
- Turning handover notes into consistent reason codes, so a cause stops fragmenting across shifts and crews
- Saying when there is too little history to claim a shift pattern at all

Drudgery — automated:

- Colouring every square from the numbers and the standing goals
- Counting reds with no reason and reds whose cause nobody is working
- Building and ranking the Pareto of reasons with its cumulative share
- Exporting the board to xlsx in the house format
