# Marketing key performance indicator (KPI) tree

Break one marketing outcome, such as marketing-sourced pipeline, into the measures that make it: leads, the share sales accepts, the share that become opportunities and their value, and the channels and handoff measures behind them. Each has a baseline, a current value, a target, an owner and a review rhythm. The tree checks whether the lead and conversion targets actually multiply to the pipeline target.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A marketing key performance indicator (KPI) tree starts from one number the business holds marketing to, usually the pipeline created from marketing's leads, and asks: what makes this number? For pipeline the first answer is arithmetic. Pipeline is the number of leads sales accepts, times the share that become opportunities, times the average opportunity value. Accepted leads, in turn, are the marketing qualified leads (MQLs) times the share sales accepts as sales qualified leads (SQLs). Because those links are formulas, the tree can check them: if the lead target times the conversion targets doesn't reach the pipeline target, the plan was a hope, and the tree shows it before the quarter is spent finding out. Below the formulas the links become causes: case study downloads and webinars drive leads; how well leads fit the target profile and how fast sales calls them drive the share accepted. Every measure carries where it started, where it is now, its target, the one person who owns it, from marketing or sales, and how often it's looked at.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're setting next quarter's lead and pipeline targets and want them to follow from each other, not be set in separate meetings.
- Marketing hits its lead target and pipeline still misses, and people argue about why.
- Marketing and sales each own part of the funnel and nobody can say who owns the gap between them.
- A new marketing leader needs to see what drives the pipeline number and who answers for each piece.

Not when:

- You haven't agreed what counts as a marketing qualified lead (MQL), a sales-qualified lead (SQL) or an opportunity. Write the definitions first, on a marketing metric definition sheet, or every number on the tree means something different to each team.
- You want to know which channel deserves the credit for a deal. That's an attribution question; the tree shows how the funnel adds up, not which touch caused it.
- You need to find why one conversion rate dropped. The tree says where to look; a stage-by-stage conversion analysis or a deal A3 finds the cause.
- You want to track the numbers week by week. The tree is the map; a visibility board is where the weekly numbers go.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Say which outcome the tree serves and why now, and which periods the baselines, current values and targets cover. Count every measure over the same period (a quarter is usual for pipeline), or the arithmetic won't work.
- **top** — Write one top outcome you already report, with a unit: "marketing-sourced pipeline created a quarter ($k)". Not an aim ("build brand"), and not leads: leads are a driver, and a tree topped by leads rewards volume over quality.
- **branches** — Under it, write the few measures that make it, usually SQLs, the SQL-to-opportunity rate and the average opportunity value. Under SQLs, MQLs and the MQL-to-SQL rate. Under those, the measures a team can move this week: the content and events that bring leads in, the share of leads inside the target profile, the hours until sales first calls. Three or four branches each, not ten.
- **combine** — For each measure with branches, say how they make it. Multiply for the funnel (SQLs × rate × value; a percentage counts as a fraction). Add where channel totals sum to a whole. Influence where a measure drives its parent with no formula, as webinars drive MQLs. Use a formula wherever one is true: it's the only link the tree can check. A conversion rate is a ratio, so count it over the same leads as the measure it multiplies (this quarter's MQLs and the share of them accepted, however late), or the link will "fail" for timing, not for a real gap.
- **numbers** — For every measure: unit, which way is better, baseline, current and target, using the same definitions as your metric definition sheet. Take numbers from the CRM (customer relationship management system) and the campaign reports, not from memory; leave a gap rather than invent one. Write money in the same unit all the way down ($k with $k).
- **owners** — Name one person for each measure, not a team: marketing for the leads and their fit, sales for acceptance and follow-up. A measure both teams think the other owns, such as time to first contact, is exactly the one to name. Say how often each is reviewed: a driver at least as often as the measure above it.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For every Add, Subtract or Multiply link the tree works out what the branches give for the baseline, current and target, and flags an entered value more than 2% away. A target that doesn't add up is the key finding: the lead and conversion targets won't deliver the target above them. Each measure also gets a status (on target, behind target, or worse than baseline) and is flagged when it has no owner, no target, no baseline or current value, no review rhythm, or a review slower than the measure above it.
- **nextStep** — Write what marketing and sales agreed about the flags: which target changes, who owns the measures without an owner, and by when. Share it with every owner on the tree, and redraw the tree when the targets are reset.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' tree for marketing-sourced pipeline, drawn up on 8 July 2026 by the head of marketing with demand generation, marketing operations and sales. Pipeline rose from $376k a quarter to $488k, against $900k by the end of the year. The top link adds up: SQLs × SQL-to-opportunity rate × average value gives $375.6k at baseline, $488k now and 120 × 25% × $30k = $900k at target. The link below it doesn't: 360 MQLs × 25% gives 90 SQLs, not the 120 the definition sheet sets, because the two targets were set separately; 120 from 360 needs a third accepted, against 19% today. The other flag is the hours from MQL to first sales contact, up from 22 to 31 with no owner or target. Two of the ten measures are on target. Marketing and sales choose by 24 July between 480 MQLs a quarter (up to $180k of spend at the $1,500 ceiling per SQL) and a $675k pipeline target, and sales names an owner for first contact by 17 July.

## Common mistakes

- **Setting the lead target and the pipeline target in different meetings.** — They're linked by conversion rates, so they can't be chosen separately. In the example, hitting the MQL target at the conversion marketing is aiming for gives 90 SQLs, not the 120 sales was promised. Work the lead target back from the pipeline target through rates you've measured.
- **Putting MQLs at the top of the tree.** — A tree topped by lead volume rewards cheap leads that sales won't accept. Put the outcome the business needs at the top; leads are one of its drivers, and their fit and conversion sit beside them.
- **Assuming a conversion rate will jump to make the targets work.** — If the plan needs the MQL-to-SQL rate to go from 19% to 33% in two quarters, say so, name who owns it and what will change it. Otherwise it's the number that quietly carries the whole plan.
- **Leaving the handoff between marketing and sales unowned.** — Time to first contact and lead acceptance sit between the two teams, so each assumes the other watches them. Name one person; it's usually where the funnel leaks first.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Marketing metric definition sheet** (`marketing-metric-definition-sheet`) — Defines what an MQL and an SQL are, how they're counted and who owns them. The tree uses those definitions, so both teams read the same numbers.

Downstream:

- **Stage conversion analysis** (`stage-conversion-analysis`) — When a conversion rate on the tree is behind, the stage-by-stage analysis shows where between lead and opportunity the leads drop out.
- **Marketing attribution reality check** (`marketing-attribution-reality-check`) — The tree counts the pipeline from marketing's leads; the attribution check asks how much the credit between channels depends on the rule chosen, before budget moves on it.
- **Visibility board** (`visibility-board`) — The tree is the map; the board is where its measures are tracked week by week against plan, with a countermeasure when one misses.
- **Marketing maturity assessment** (`marketing-maturity-assessment`) — What the tree finds, such as targets set separately or an unowned handoff, is evidence for the demand generation and sales handoff scores.
