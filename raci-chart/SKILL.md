# RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart

Agree who does what: list the tasks and decisions in a process or project, list the roles, and mark each cell R (does the work), A (owns the outcome, one per task), C (asked before) or I (told after). The checks are computed, not typed in: a task with no owner or two, a task nobody does, too many people consulted, and a role doing nothing or carrying more than half the work.

Family: Kaizen events · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A RACI chart is a grid with the tasks and decisions of a process or project down the side, the roles across the top, and one letter in each cell saying how that role takes part. R, responsible: does the work. A, accountable: owns the outcome and answers for it, and there is exactly one per row. C, consulted: is asked before the work is done or the decision made, and their view can change it. I, informed: is told afterwards. A role that both owns a task and does it is marked A/R. The chart earns its keep in two moments. When you fill it in with the people in the roles, the disagreements come out ("I thought you approved those"), and settling them there is far cheaper than finding out when an invoice goes unpaid. And when you read it back, the gaps are plain: a row with no A is a decision nobody owns, two As mean each owner waits for the other, a row with no R is work nobody is named to do, and a column full of letters is one person the whole process depends on.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Work keeps falling between people: things get dropped, done twice, or wait while everyone assumes someone else has it.
- A process or project crosses several teams and nobody has written down who decides what.
- You're setting up a kaizen event, a project or a new process and want roles agreed before the work starts.
- A process has just changed and the old understanding of who does what no longer holds.
- Decisions take too long because too many people expect to be asked first.

Not when:

- One person does the whole process. There's nothing to divide; write a work instruction instead.
- You need to plan when things happen and in what order. This chart says who, not when; use an implementation plan for dates and owners.
- The real problem is that a step is slow or error-prone. Who owns it matters less than how it runs: map the process and find the cause.
- You want a chart to settle a dispute without the people in it. Filled in alone, it records one person's opinion and settles nothing.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Say which process or project the chart covers and where it starts and ends. Keep it to one process or one project: a chart for "everything finance does" has a hundred rows and nobody reads it. If you've outlined the process on a SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers), use the same start and end.
- **roles** — List the roles that take part, across the top. Use roles, not names ("Accounts payable supervisor", not "Priya"), so the chart still holds when someone changes job; note who holds each role today if it helps. Include roles outside your team that decide or approve things. Six to eight roles is plenty; more usually means the rows are too detailed.
- **tasks** — List the tasks and decisions down the side, each starting with a verb: "Match purchase order, receipt and invoice", "Decide what happens to a mismatch". Include the decisions people argue about and the exceptions (the over-limit sign-off, the mismatch, the chase), not only the routine steps; that's where ownership is usually missing. Ten to fifteen rows covers most processes.
- **letters** — Fill each cell with one letter, with the people in the roles in the room. R does the work. A owns the outcome: exactly one per row, the person who answers for it if it goes wrong. A/R owns it and does it. C is asked before and can change the answer. I is told after. Leave a cell empty if the role has no part. Fill in how it works today first; where people disagree about a letter, that's the finding. Agree the change and write it in the note for that row.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the chart is checked for you. For each task it counts the As, Rs and Cs and flags no A, more than one A, no R, and more than 2 roles consulted. For each role it counts the tasks it does and owns and flags a role that does the work on nothing, or does or owns more than half the tasks. Each flag is a question to settle with the people involved, not a rule to obey blindly: a manager who only ever owns outcomes may rightly have no R.
- **nextStep** — Write what was agreed and what happens next: the changes to make, who tells whom, and when the chart will be checked again. Share it with everyone on it. A chart that lives in one person's folder changes nothing.

## What good looks like

The example below is Meridian Business Services' accounts payable team's first pass at who does what in their vendor invoice approval process, inside the boundary they agreed on their SIPOC. Six roles, twelve tasks and decisions, filled in with the people in each role. The checks found exactly the kind of gaps a first pass is for: nobody owns the decision on a purchase order mismatch while four roles expect to be asked, so the team split it at $10k into two rows with one owner each (the supervisor below, the finance manager above); both the department approver and the finance manager think they own the over-$10k sign-off; and the finance manager owns the payment run but nobody is named to confirm it actually released. Each of the four flagged rows carries the change the team agreed. The chart also shows the accounts payable supervisor doing or owning 7 of the 12 tasks, which the team took into their waste walk rather than papering over.

## Common mistakes

- **More than one A on a row.** — Two owners feels fair and works badly: each assumes the other has it, and when it goes wrong nobody answers for it. Pick one A. The other can be C if their view changes the decision, or I if they only need to know.
- **Everyone consulted on everything.** — Every C is a conversation someone has to wait for. Consult the roles whose input would change the answer; tell the rest afterwards. If a row has three or more Cs, ask which of them would actually change anything.
- **Filling it in alone.** — A chart one person fills in records that person's view of the process. The value is in the disagreements that surface when the people in the roles fill it in together; settle them in the room.
- **Naming people instead of roles.** — A chart full of names goes out of date the first time someone moves, and it makes ownership about a person rather than the job. Use roles; note who holds them today on the side.
- **Only the routine steps on the list.** — The routine steps usually work. Ownership goes missing in the exceptions and the decisions: the mismatch, the over-limit approval, the confirmation nobody does. Put those rows on the chart.
- **Drawing the chart and never using it.** — A RACI chart is only worth the changes it leads to. Write what was agreed on each flagged row, share it with everyone on it, and check it again when the process changes.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Kaizen event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — The charter names the event team and its one accountable owner. A RACI chart does the same for every task and decision in the process the event changes.

Downstream:

- **Implementation plan** (`implementation-plan`) — Each agreed change from the chart becomes an action with an owner and a date. The A on a row is usually the right owner for its action.
- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — Where a role is R for a repeated task, a work instruction says how that task is done, so the R means the same thing whoever holds the role.
- **Tiered huddle board** (`tiered-huddle-board`) — When a problem raised at a huddle needs a decision, the chart says whose decision it is, so it goes to the right level the first time.
- **Mutual action plan** (`mutual-action-plan`) — The same who-does-what thinking for work shared with a customer: each step names an owner on each side, so nothing waits on an assumption.
