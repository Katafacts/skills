# Eight wastes (DOWNTIME) worksheet

A structured waste walk against eight categories (DOWNTIME: Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent, Transportation, Inventory, Motion, Extra-processing), tallied so the vital few stand out, with any category nobody looked for named.

Family: Foundations · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

The eight wastes (DOWNTIME) worksheet is how a waste walk actually gets recorded: real observations, each tagged to one of eight standard categories, tallied so the vital few waste types stand out instead of a wall of anecdotes. DOWNTIME is Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent, Transportation, Inventory, Motion, Extra-processing. Seven of those are the classic wastes of lean manufacturing. The eighth, non-utilized talent (people's skills, ideas and creativity going unused), was added later as lean spread beyond the factory, along with the DOWNTIME memory aid. Office and knowledge work tends to underweight it, even though it's often the largest waste. A category with zero observations logged is flagged honestly rather than hidden — it either means that waste genuinely isn't present, or it means nobody actually looked for it yet, and the worksheet says so rather than pretending the two are the same thing.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're walking a real process — shop floor or office — and want to record what you actually see, tagged consistently, instead of a running list of complaints.
- You want an honest tally showing which waste category actually dominates, not a gut feeling about what's probably the biggest problem.
- You're kicking off an improvement event or a Transactional Process Improvement (TPI) review and need real evidence of where the waste is before committing to what to fix.

Not when:

- You already know exactly what to fix and just need to plan the countermeasure — go straight to an implementation plan or A3; this worksheet is for finding the problem, not solving it.
- You're tempted to fill in every category just to look thorough — an honestly sparse worksheet, concentrated in the categories that actually apply, is more useful than a padded one.
- You haven't actually walked the process yet — this tool structures real observations, it doesn't generate plausible-sounding waste from a description alone.

## How to fill it in

- **observations** — Walk the process and write one line for each thing you actually saw: what happened, at which step, how often, which of the eight wastes it is, and how big its impact is. Describe the work, never the person, and leave out any waste you assume is there until someone has watched it happen.
- **coverage** — Calculated from your observations — don't type it; check the tallies match what you saw, and treat a waste with no observations as a place you may not have looked yet, not proof it's absent.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences on what the walk showed: the waste that dominates, the high-impact observations, and anything that surprised you. Use only what's on the worksheet.
- **nextSteps** — List what you'll do about the top waste or a high-impact observation, ranked by impact and effort, each with an owner and a date. Tie each step to a named observation, so you can go back later and check it's gone.

## Common mistakes

- **Padding the worksheet to fill every category.** — A waste walk that finds real waste in three categories and honestly nothing in the other five is more useful than one that manufactures a plausible-sounding entry for every category — the empty categories are real information.
- **Logging a generic complaint instead of a specific observation.** — "The process is slow" isn't a DOWNTIME observation — "invoices sit in the inbox for 2-3 days before anyone picks them up" is. A vague entry can't actually be acted on.
- **Underusing Non-utilized talent.** — It's the easiest of the eight to skip — skilled people doing routine work below their capability rarely gets logged as "waste" the way a literal delay does, even though it's often the most expensive one.

## What it connects to

Downstream:

- **Spaghetti diagram: walking and carrying paths** (`spaghetti-diagram`) — Motion and transport waste found on the walk can be measured: follow one person or part for a full cycle and the diagram shows how far they travel and which legs cost the most.
- **Makigami process map** (`makigami`) — Once waste is identified by category, a Makigami follows one real case step by step and shows exactly where — and in whose lane — each waste lives, with the waits and handoffs timed.
- **Value stream map — current and future state** (`value-stream-map`) — A waste walk's findings are often the raw material a value stream map's current-state analysis draws on to compute where the flow is actually choked.
- **Improvement event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — A concentrated cluster of high-impact waste in one category is exactly the kind of finding that justifies chartering a real improvement event, not just a next-steps list.
