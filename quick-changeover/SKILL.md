# Quick changeover

Film one real changeover, from the last good unit to the first good unit, and take it apart element by element. Mark each element internal (the machine has to be stopped) or external (it can be done while it runs), move what can go outside the stop, streamline what is left, and write the new way into the standard. The sheet works out the changeover time at each step, what is still inside the stop and what the saved minutes buy: more output, or more, smaller batches.

Family: Flow and pull · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A changeover is the time a machine or line stands still between the last good unit of one run and the first good unit of the next: a colour change in a paint booth, a die change on a press, a new product on a filling line, a new job on a print run. While it stands still it makes nothing, so long changeovers push people into long runs, and long runs mean big batches, stock piling up and customers waiting for their turn. The quick changeover method cuts that time in four moves. First, see it: film one real changeover and write down every element in order with its time. Second, separate: mark each element internal, if the machine really has to be stopped for it, or external, if it could be done while the machine still runs. Third, convert: move the external work out of the stop. Fetching tools and materials, mixing, preheating, finding the right part and putting the old one away can almost always happen before the stop or after the restart. This step costs next to nothing and often takes a third or more off the time. Fourth, streamline what has to stay inside the stop: two people working at once, quick fastenings instead of bolts, settings fixed so that nothing needs adjusting, and waits overlapped with other work. Then write the new way down, train everyone who does it, and decide what the saved time is for. The time to aim at is the one you measure from last good unit to first good unit, so the trial runs and adjustments at the end count; that is often where the biggest waste hides.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Changeovers take long enough that people avoid them: runs get longer, batches bigger, and orders wait for a slot.
- A value stream map shows a step whose changeover time sets the batch size or keeps it from keeping pace.
- A kaizen event or a target condition is about changeover time, and you need to see where the minutes go before changing anything.
- Changeover times vary a lot from one person or shift to another, and you want one way that everyone can do.
- You need more output from a step without new equipment, and much of its time goes on changeovers.

Not when:

- The step rarely changes over, or its changeovers are already a small part of its time: the minutes are elsewhere. A value stream map or a Pareto chart of lost time will show where.
- You can't watch a real changeover. Times from memory or from the plan leave out the searching, the walking and the trial runs this method exists to find.
- The problem is how often changeovers happen, not how long each one takes: orders run in the order they arrive cause extra changeovers. Fix how orders are grouped into runs first. Putting the runs in an order that makes each change small is different: that belongs in this method, when you streamline.
- The aim is to judge the operator. Filming people to score them teaches them to hide the problems the film is meant to show.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Pick one changeover that happens often and say which: from what to what, on which machine. Set the start and end: the last good unit of the old run to the first good unit of the new one. Starting at "machine stopped" or ending at "machine restarted" leaves out the run-down and the trial runs, which are often the longest part. Write the target, and who filmed it, when, and who did the changeover.
- **elements** — Film the whole changeover, after asking the operator and saying why: the film is for the method, not for judging them. Watch it back with the people who do the changeover, on both shifts if there are two; they will see things you don't. Write each element in order, starting with a verb, with its minutes read off the film. Split an element where the work changes, so fetching and fitting aren't one row. Pick its kind of work: fetch or prepare, remove or fit, clean or flush, adjust or trial run, check, wait, or other.
- **classify** — For each element, as it is done today: Internal if the machine is stopped while it happens, External if it happens while the machine runs. Then ask of every internal element: does the machine really have to be stopped for this? Be strict. Fetching a nozzle, finding a panel or mixing a colour only feels internal because it has always been done during the stop.
- **convert** — Where an internal element could be done while the machine runs, set After converting to External and say how: staged on a cart before the stop, mixed in the last minutes of the old run, cleaned after the restart. Leave the column blank where nothing moves. Do this before anything else. It needs no money, only a checklist of what has to be ready before the stop, and it shows how much of the changeover was never really changeover work.
- **streamline** — For what stays inside the stop, write the new time where it changes (0 if the element goes) and what changes. The usual moves: a second person working alongside (put B in Who; the stop shrinks, the labour doesn't); quick fastenings instead of threads and bolts; parts and tools at the same height and size; settings fixed for each product, so the machine is set to a known value instead of tried, looked at and adjusted; waits overlapped with other work. And plan the order of products so each change is the smallest one: light colours before dark, similar sizes together. That shortens the cleaning without touching the cleaning standard. Don't shorten a cleaning or checking step to hit the target unless you prove the first units stay good.
- **totals** — Nothing to enter. The sheet adds up the changeover time now (internal minutes as filmed), after converting and after streamlining. When two people work inside the stop, the longer of their two lists is the shortest the stop can be: the best case. If one person waits on the other's step, the stop is longer. Draw both sequences on a timed chart and confirm with a filmed trial. Their working minutes together are the labour; a wait inside a list is idle time, so it is left out. It counts the elements still inside the stop and draws the three times to one scale against the target. Each element's Check flags work still fetched or prepared inside the stop, adjusting or waiting still inside it, a cleaning or checking step made shorter, a change not written into the standard, and anything not marked or timed.
- **capacity** — Enter how many changeovers happen in a shift, day or week and what the machine makes in an hour while it runs. The sheet turns the saved minutes into time freed, the extra units that time would make, or the number of changeovers the same stopped time now buys, and so how small batches could get. Decide which you want before the minutes disappear into longer breaks and longer runs. Smaller batches are usually the bigger prize: customers wait less, and less stock piles up between steps.
- **standard** — Say where the new way is written: the routine card with the steps in order, what is staged before the stop, who does what when there are two people, and the settings for each product. Say when it was revised and who is trained, and who isn't yet. Mark each changed element Yes or No in the standard; any change still marked No is the next thing to write down.
- **findings** — Write what moved, what was streamlined and what it bought, the checks still open with an owner and a date each, and how you will know it holds: a chart of changeover minutes at the machine, and the same last-good-to-first-good yardstick at every check.

## What good looks like

The example is the Line 2 paint booth's colour changeover in August 2026. The March kaizen event set out to take it from about 45 minutes to under 25; by the April audit it was back at 46, and the staged flush cart had brought it to about 35 by May. On 11 August the paint booth lead filmed the 10:00 changeover, and on 13 August the operators of both shifts took the film apart: 18 elements, 36.0 minutes inside the stop and 4.0 outside it. Converting came first and cost nothing: mixing the new colour, cleaning the old nozzle set, finding the colour's standard panel and putting the old pot away moved outside the stop, 9.5 minutes, leaving 26.5. Streamlining then took the changeover to 20.0 minutes, 44% less than filmed and inside the 25-minute target. Each colour's pressure and fan width went on its queue card, so 4.5 minutes of spray, look and adjust became one confirming spray of 1.0; a quick-release coupling cut priming from 2.5 to 1.5 minutes; and the second operator wipes the floor grates (2.0 minutes) while the first works on the gun, so 19.0 working minutes, plus a 3-minute wait, fit in a 20-minute stop. That 20.0 is the best case, not a promise: if one operator waits on the other the stop is longer, and the ten trial changeovers ran 19 to 24 minutes. The 90-second flush, the white test card and the first-part check were not touched. Twelve elements are still inside the stop, and three checks stay open: a 3-minute wait for the walls to dry (the next trial connects and primes the new pot while they dry), a confirming spray that is still an adjustment, and the second operator's part not yet on the routine card. At 4 changeovers a day the booth gets 64 minutes back, about 10.7 parts at 10 an hour; or the same stopped time buys 7 changeovers instead of 4, so batches could be 57% of today's size if demand and the colour mix stay the same. Seven doesn't split across two shifts, so the decision on the table is a third slot a shift: 6 a day, 120 of today's 144 stopped minutes, still freeing 24.

## Common mistakes

- **Timing from memory instead of filming a real changeover.** — People remember the work they do, not the time they spend looking for a spanner or waiting for a wall to dry. The film catches the searching, the walking and the trial runs, which are usually the cheapest minutes to remove.
- **Measuring from machine stopped to machine restarted.** — The run-down at the end of the old job and the adjusting and trial runs at the start of the new one are part of the changeover. Measure from the last good unit to the first good unit, or the biggest waste stays outside the yardstick.
- **Buying equipment before converting.** — Moving the fetching, preparing and putting away outside the stop costs almost nothing and often takes a third or more off the time. Quick fastenings and new fixtures come after, aimed at what is really left inside.
- **Shortening the adjustment instead of removing it.** — Adjusting means nobody knows the right setting in advance. Find the setting for each product, fix it with a mark, a stop or a gauge reading, and set straight to it. Getting faster at trial and error still leaves the trial and error.
- **Cutting a cleaning or checking step to hit the target.** — A faster changeover that sends bad first units downstream has moved the cost, not removed it. Protect the steps that make the first unit good; if one really can be shorter, prove it on the first units of several changeovers before it goes on the card.
- **Counting two people's work twice, or forgetting the second person's part.** — Work done in parallel shortens the stop, not the labour. The longer of the two lists is the best case: if one person waits on the other's step, the stop is longer. Draw both sequences on a timed chart and confirm with a filmed trial. And if the second person's part isn't on the card, the shift that wasn't there won't do it.
- **Leaving the new way in people's heads.** — An event can hit its target on the day and be back where it started within a month when the new order, the staging list and the settings never reach the card. A changeover is quicker when the standard says so and both shifts are trained on it.
- **Letting the saved minutes disappear.** — Without a decision, the freed time drifts into longer runs and nothing changes for the customer. Decide whether it goes to output or to more, smaller batches, and make the change in the schedule.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — Filming a changeover is a go-see with a camera: plan where to stand and what to watch for, ask before filming, and watch the real work, not a demonstration.
- **Value stream map — current and future state** (`value-stream-map`) — The map shows which step's changeover time forces big batches or stops it keeping pace, and so which changeover to film first.
- **Kaizen event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — A changeover event's charter sets the yardstick (last good unit to first good unit), the target and the team, including the operators of every shift; the worksheet is what the team fills in during the event.

Downstream:

- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — The new changeover, element by element, becomes the routine card: the steps in order, what is staged before the stop, who does what, and the key points that keep the first unit good.
- **Standard work chart** (`standard-work-chart`) — When the changeover is done by two people, a timed chart of each person's sequence shows they finish together and nobody waits on the other.
- **Sustainment audit** (`sustainment-audit`) — At the 30-, 60- and 90-day checks, watch a changeover on each shift against the card and read the log on the same yardstick: is the new way how it is really done?
- **Run chart** (`run-chart`) — Plot every changeover's minutes in order, with a mark where the new way started, to see whether the gain is real and holding, not one good week.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding with the operators which elements really need the machine stopped
- Deciding which quality steps are protected and never shortened
- Deciding whether the saved time goes to output or to smaller batches

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting fetching, adjusting and waiting inside the stop from the element list
- Suggesting how work could be split between two people, for the team to try
- Comparing changeover times before and after, by shift, from the log

Drudgery — automated:

- Adding up the stop now, after converting and after streamlining
- Working out the longer of two people's lists and the labour
- Turning the saved minutes into output or batch size
