# Scoping canvas

A one-page, multi-section definition of what an initiative actually covers — problem, goal, in scope, out of scope, team, timeline, assumptions — before anyone starts work on it.

Family: Problem definition · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A scoping canvas is a one-page definition of what an initiative actually covers, built before anyone starts work on it — not a project plan, and not a full charter, just the boundary-setting document that keeps a team from discovering three weeks in that they disagreed about what was in scope. A canvas is defined by which sections apply to the situation, the same way any good scoping exercise is — problem/goal/in-scope/out-of-scope/team/timeline/assumptions is the default set, not a rigid universal structure.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- An initiative is real enough to commit time to, but its boundaries haven't been written down anywhere a disagreement could be checked against.
- Multiple people or teams are involved, and 'what's actually in scope' has already come up as a question once.
- You want a fast way to see what's still undefined — a thin section here is a real gap, not just an editing task.

Not when:

- The initiative is small enough that one person can hold the whole scope in their head with no real risk of drift — a canvas adds ceremony without adding value there.
- You need a full charter with a resourced budget and formal sign-off — an improvement event charter or business case is the right tool for that weight of commitment.
- The boundaries are already written down and agreed somewhere else — don't duplicate an existing source of truth.

## How to fill it in

- **problem** — Write the situation and why it matters now, in one or two sentences — not a full background essay.
- **goal** — Write what this initiative needs to achieve, specifically enough that you'd know whether it succeeded — ideally a number and a date.
- **in-scope** — List what's genuinely in scope: the processes, teams, systems or customers this will touch. Be specific enough that two people would agree on whether something is in or out.
- **out-of-scope** — List what's deliberately excluded, and why. If you can't name anything, the scope isn't settled yet — an empty list is a finding, not a section to skip.
- **team** — List who's involved and their role: who leads, who does the work, who decides, and who needs to be kept informed.
- **timeline** — List the key milestones or phases with dates. Check them with the people doing the work, not just the sponsor.
- **assumptions** — List what has to be true for this to work, and any constraint you're working within — budget, people, systems, deadlines. Write each so it can be checked; these are the first things to revisit when the plan slips.
- **success-measure** — Name the one number that will show the work succeeded: what is measured and where, its unit, today's baseline, the target and the date. The change needed and the change as a share of the baseline are worked out for you; if you can't give a baseline yet, measuring it is the first milestone.

The example below scopes an improvement event that picks up where a problem-solving A3's countermeasures left off: tightly bounded to one paint booth and one procedure, explicitly deferring the other line and any capital spend rather than letting scope creep in by default. ## Common mistakes

- **Leaving out-of-scope empty because nothing's been explicitly excluded yet.** — An empty out-of-scope section almost never means nothing's excluded — it usually means the boundary hasn't been thought through yet, which is exactly the ambiguity this canvas exists to catch before it causes a real disagreement.
- **Writing a goal statement vague enough that almost any outcome would count as success.** — A goal that can't fail isn't a real goal — the same discipline an A3's problem statement or a business case's cost-of-inaction section already holds itself to.
- **Treating the canvas as a one-time exercise nobody revisits once the initiative starts.** — Scope drifts in real work — a canvas that's never checked against again is ceremony, not a working document.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — A recurring problem an A3 surfaces sometimes needs work bigger than one countermeasure can carry — that's exactly when it graduates into a scoped initiative of its own.

Downstream:

- **SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers)** (`sipoc`) — Turns the agreed scope into a process boundary, the handful of main steps, and the customers and suppliers on either side.
- **SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis** (`swot-analysis`) — Once the scope and goal are set, a SWOT scored against that goal shows what you're building on and exposed to.
- **Improvement event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — Once an initiative is scoped and worth a dedicated event, the charter is where it gets resourced, dated, and given a roster — heavier commitment than a canvas alone implies.
- **Business case** (`business-case`) — An initiative whose value needs to be argued for, not just scoped, is where a business case's investment/value-driver discipline takes over.
