# Run chart

Plot one measure in time order around its median and test it with three plain rules, so you can tell a real change from ordinary ups and downs and judge a change on its before and after, not on the latest point. The median, natural limits, signals and checks are computed, not typed in.

Family: Measurement · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A run chart is one measure plotted in time order, one point per day, week or batch, with a line across at the middle value (the median). Its job is to answer one question honestly: has something really changed, or is this just the usual up and down? Every process varies. Most of that variation comes from many small causes that are always there: it is noise, and no single point of it means anything. Now and then something specific changes the process, for better or worse: that is a signal, and it is worth finding out what happened while it is fresh. Treating noise as a signal (explaining every bad week, changing things after every dip) wastes effort and usually makes the process jumpier. Missing a signal throws away the chance to learn what made things better, or to stop what made them worse. The chart replaces the argument about whether "it feels worse" with three agreed tests. First, a shift: 6 or more points in a row on the same side of the median. In a steady process each point is as likely to fall above the median as below, so six in a row on one side is like six heads in a row: it happens by chance only about 3 times in 100. Second, a trend: 5 or more points in a row, each higher than the last, or each lower; a repeated value is skipped, neither adding to the trend nor breaking it. Third, a point beyond the natural limits: the average plus or minus 2.66 times the average jump from one point to the next (the average moving range). A steady process almost never goes beyond those limits, so a point outside them is worth a look on its own. The limits come from the process's own numbers, not from a target: they say what the process does, not what you want it to do. The line is the median, used for the run rules; the limits are worked out around the average. On lopsided data the two differ a little; that is expected. To judge a change, mark a baseline (the points from before it, ideally 10 to 12) and the point after which the change was made. The median and limits come from the baseline only, and the points after the change are judged against them.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You've made a change and want to know whether it worked, without declaring victory on one good week.
- A bad day or week has people asking for action, and you want to know whether it is a real change or the usual variation.
- A measure is reviewed regularly on a board, and you want the history read the same way every time instead of argued about from memory.
- A process seems to be drifting slowly and you want to see it before it becomes a problem.
- You're about to set a target and want to know what the process actually delivers today.

Not when:

- You want to compare categories (which defect, which customer, which stage) rather than see change over time. Use a Pareto chart.
- Two teams count the measure differently, or the way it is counted changed partway. Pin the definition down on a metric definition sheet first, or the chart shows the counting, not the process.
- The thing you count is rare, so most periods are zero. The rules have little to work with; chart the number of days (or units) between events instead.
- You need a decision today and have only three or four points. Look at the work instead; the chart needs history to say anything.

## How to fill it in

- **measure** — Name one measure, per period, with its unit: "old-colour rejects at final inspection per week", not "quality". Say where the numbers come from and how they are counted. Pick a period short enough to give a point often (daily or weekly) and long enough that most periods have something in them. Count it the same way every period: a change in how you count looks exactly like a change in the process.
- **points** — Enter the points oldest first, one per period, with no gaps. Write a short note against any period where something happened (a new person, a breakdown, a change to the routine): when a signal appears, the notes are the first place to look. Don't leave out a period because it looks odd; odd periods are what the chart is for.
- **baseline** — Say how many of the first points are the baseline: points from one steady stretch of the process as it was before anything was changed. Ten to twelve points make the median and limits trustworthy; with fewer they are provisional, so act only on strong signals. Don't stretch the baseline back into a period when the process was different: a short, honest baseline beats a long one that mixes two processes. If the baseline itself has a signal in it, find out what happened before you trust its limits.
- **change** — If you made a change, give the last point before it, and say what changed and when. The chart marks the change and judges the points after it against the baseline. A change made partway through a period: count that period as before, and say so in its note. Leave it blank if nothing has been changed yet: the chart then simply watches for signals after the baseline.
- **signals** — Nothing to enter here: the chart is checked for you. It works out the baseline's median, average, average moving range and natural limits, flags every point that breaks one of the three rules, and gives a verdict: a signal after the change, too early to judge (fewer than 6 points since the change), or no signal. It also checks whether the baseline is long enough and steady, whether the change sits inside the baseline, and whether a signal shows before the change was made (something else moved the process). Limits are moved only once a shift is confirmed after the change and there are 10 points from where it began; then it works out the new ones.
- **nextStep** — Write what happens because of what the chart shows. A signal after the change: check it really was the change (what else happened then?), make the change the standard, and move to the new limits once there are enough points. A signal you didn't cause: go to the work and find out what happened in that period. No signal: leave the latest point alone, and if the level isn't good enough, the process itself has to change, so work on it with an A3, a 5-Whys or an improvement kata storyboard.

## What good looks like

The example below continues Line 2's paint booth after the 10–12 March changeover event. The event and the storyboard after it shortened the changeover to about 35 minutes, and the 5-Whys found that the shortened routine's "flush until clear" left old colour in the gun; from Friday 3 July the routine card put a timed 90-second flush back, with a white test card before the first part. The chart counts old-colour rejects at final inspection each week, from the reject tags, the same way the Pareto chart and the 5-Whys did. The baseline is the 7 weeks from 18 May, when the new routine had settled, to the week of 29 June: 10, 5, 9, 7 (the 31 rejects the 5-Whys started from), then 6, 8 and 11. Its median is 8, its average 8 and its average moving range 2.83, so the natural limits are 15.54 and 0.46. The week of 29 June, at 11, felt like a crisis at the time and is inside the limits: noise. The chart flags that 7 points is a short baseline and the limits are provisional; the team kept them because the process before 18 May was a different one, and judged only on strong signals. After the card, all 10 weeks to 7 September sit below the median (1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 2, 0, 1: six rejects in ten weeks): a shift, confirmed when the run reached six in the week of 10 August. Five of those weeks, the ones with no rejects, are also below the lower limit, the first of them the week of 13 July. The verdict is a signal after the change, from point 8. With 10 points since the shift began, the chart works out new limits from the week of 6 July: median 0.5, average 0.6, upper limit 3.56, and no lower limit (it works out below zero). So from now on a week with 4 or more old-colour rejects is a signal to chase; a week with 1 or 2 is ordinary, and each reject is still traced to its changeover by the daily check without changing the routine for one.

## Common mistakes

- **Explaining every point.** — Most ups and downs are noise. Asking "what went wrong last week?" about a point inside the limits sends people hunting for a cause that isn't there, and changing the process in response makes it jumpier, not better. Look for a cause only when a rule is broken.
- **Judging a change on one or two points, or on before-and-after averages.** — One good week after a change proves nothing: the process had good weeks before it too. Two averages always differ a little. Wait for a rule to be broken against the baseline, usually the run of six, before calling it.
- **Drawing the target as if it were a limit.** — A target says what you want; the natural limits say what the process does. A point that misses the target but sits inside the limits is not a special event, it is the process doing what it always does. Hitting the target then means changing the process, not chasing the point.
- **Moving the limits whenever the numbers move.** — Recalculating after every few points, or folding the after-change points into the baseline, makes every change look like noise. Keep the baseline limits until a shift is confirmed and there are about 10 points to set new ones from.
- **Building the baseline from a different process.** — Points from before an earlier change, or a stretch with a known upset in it, give limits that judge nothing. Use one steady stretch from just before the change, even if it is short, and say it is short.
- **Doing nothing because there's no signal.** — No signal means the process is steady, not that it is good. If its level isn't good enough, reacting to points won't help: the process has to change. That is work for an A3 or a storyboard, judged afterwards on this same chart.
- **Changing how the measure is counted partway through.** — A new definition, a new data source or a new person counting shows up on the chart exactly like a real change. Fix the definition first; if it has to change, start a new baseline and note why.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Kaizen event report-out** (`kaizen-event-report-out`) — An event's report-out reads each measure at three follow-up checks. A run chart with the event marked shows whether that result really held between the checks, not just on the days someone looked.
- **Metric definition sheet** (`metric-definition-sheet`) — Define the measure first: formula, source, what is left out, who owns it. A run chart of a number two people count differently shows the counting, not the process.
- **5-Whys** (`five-whys`) — A 5-Whys ends with a countermeasure and a check. The run chart is how the check is read: the countermeasure is the change, and the points after it are judged against the baseline.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — The A3's effect confirmation says how the metric will be checked after the countermeasure. Read that check on a run chart, so the A3 closes on a confirmed shift rather than one good week.
- **Quick changeover** (`quick-changeover`) — After a changeover is streamlined, plot each changeover's minutes in order with a mark where the new way started, to see whether the gain holds.

Downstream:

- **Sustainment audit** (`sustainment-audit`) — The audit asks whether a result has held. A run chart with the change marked answers that with evidence: still shifted against the old baseline, or drifting back.
- **Performance board** (`performance-board`) — The board shows each measure against plan, month by month. A run chart of the same measure says whether a red month is a real change worth a countermeasure or the usual variation.
- **Stage conversion analysis** (`stage-conversion-analysis`) — Plot each cohort's conversion rate on a run chart before calling a drop. A change of a few points between two or three cohorts is usually within ordinary variation.

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing the measure and the baseline stretch, and deciding whether a short baseline is good enough
- Finding out at the work what caused a signal
- Deciding what to change when the process is steady but not good enough

Analysis — AI helps:

- Applying the three rules to every point and listing the signals
- Spotting a change in counting or a gap in the data before it is read as a real change
- Comparing a signal's timing with notes, logs and other changes made at the same time

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out the median, average moving range and natural limits
- Recalculating the limits once a shift is confirmed
- Drawing the chart and exporting it as a workbook
