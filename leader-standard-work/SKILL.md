# Leader standard work

A leader's own routine, written down and timed: each task at its time slot, daily, weekly or monthly, with what to check, and a tick for each day it was done or a cross when it was missed. Under it, what the checks found and the action on each. The share of the routine actually done, the day it fell apart, and findings with no owner or past their date are computed, not typed in.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

Leader standard work is the short list of things a leader checks, at set times, to keep the team's standards alive: the huddle at 06:45, a walk of the area before the first job, watching one changeover a day, reading yesterday's rejects, the handover to the next shift. Each task has a time slot, a frequency (daily, weekly or monthly), how long it takes, and what to check, written as what good looks like. Across the week there's a box for each day: a tick when it was done, a cross when it was due and missed, blank when it isn't due. Under the routine sits a list of what the checks found and what's being done about each, with an owner and a date. It does two jobs. The routine keeps a new standard from fading once the people who introduced it move on, because someone looks at it every day. And the record is honest about the leader's own work: how much of the routine happened, where it fell apart, and whether what it found was followed up. The share of the day falls with level: most of the shift for a team lead, an hour or two a day for a supervisor, a small slice of the week for senior leaders, but no leader has none. Each layer also checks the routine of the layer below, at the work: that check is what keeps the routines themselves from fading.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A new standard or countermeasure is in place and you want it still being followed in three months, not just this week.
- Your days go to whatever shouts loudest, and the checks you know matter (the walk, the huddle, the follow-up) happen when there's time, which is rarely.
- A team lead role is changing hands and the new lead should inherit the routine that works, not rebuild it from memory.
- You coach other leaders and want to see what their days really hold, and to talk about the misses rather than the intentions.
- The same problems keep coming back after they were fixed, which usually means nobody checks the fix.

Not when:

- You want to write down how a frontline task is done. That's a work instruction; this is the leader's routine for checking that the work is done that way.
- You want to inspect people's performance. The routine checks the process and the standard; used to catch people out, it stops finding anything.
- You're planning a one-off project with dates. Use an implementation plan; leader standard work is what repeats every day and week.
- There's no standard yet to check. Settle how the work should be done first, or the routine becomes a walk with nothing to look for.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Write in a sentence or two what the routine keeps running: the standards and results it protects. Then write the Monday of the week; the dates across the top follow from it. Roughly how much of the day the routine takes is worth saying too. Name who checks this routine and how often: the leader one layer up, walking the sheet with you at the work, not reading it in an office.
- **tasks** — One row per task: the daily tasks first, in time order, then the weekly and monthly ones in the order they fall. Give each a time slot ("06:45", not "morning"), a frequency, and the minutes it takes. Start the task with a verb: "Watch the first changeover slot." Then write what to check as what good looks like: "Nozzle off; flush timed 90 seconds; white test card clean before the first part." Put weekly and monthly tasks on a set day and say which ("Friday", "fourth Thursday"). Start with five to eight tasks you can really do; a routine that needs a perfect day never happens.
- **ticks** — At the end of each day, mark ✓ for every task done and ✗ for every task that was due and missed. Leave a day blank only when the task isn't due that day, such as a weekly task on the other four days. Mark the misses honestly: they're the most useful part of the sheet, because they show where the routine breaks.
- **findings** — When a check finds something off standard, write it down as a fact with the date and the task number: "Flush cart down to 3 test cards." Write the action taken or to take, one owner and a due date, and mark it Done when it's done. When the same task keeps getting missed, write that here too, with the reason, and change the routine (a new time, someone who covers) rather than promising to try harder.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the sheet works it out. For each task it counts the days done and due and the share done, and flags a missing time, frequency or check, a weekly task with no mark, and how many times it was missed. For each day it counts what was done and due and the minutes of routine planned, and flags a day with half or less done. For the findings it counts how many have an action, owner and date, how many are closed, and which are open past their date as of the date you give.
- **reflection** — At the end of the week, write what the numbers showed and what changes in the routine as a result. Then go through it with your own manager: the misses, the findings and what's overdue. A leader's routine that nobody else ever looks at decays exactly like the standards it was meant to protect.

## What good looks like

The example below is the Line 2 paint booth lead's routine for the week of 20 July 2026, the week after the line supervisor's go-see walk. It shows only his checking tasks, about an hour a day; the rest of his shift is running the booth. Its nine tasks keep the changeover standard from the 5-Whys alive on both shifts: five daily (huddle, booth walk, watching the first changeover, reading reject tags, handover), three weekly and one monthly. One of the weekly tasks is the layer above checking this one: every Friday the line supervisor walks the sheet with him at the booth and looks for the evidence behind two ticks. It was a real week, not a perfect one: 25 of 29 planned tasks done (86%). Every miss traces to two days. Wednesday, when a mixing room pump failure took the morning and its restart the evening, only 3 of 6 got done; on Thursday the monthly card review ran into the handover. The checks found six things, every one with an action, an owner and a due date; two are closed and one, the late flush check on the 21:00 changeover, is overdue. The fixes go into the routine itself: the line supervisor covers the first changeover when the lead is pulled away, and the monthly review now starts at 12:30.

## Common mistakes

- **A routine nobody could finish.** — Twenty tasks that need a perfect day get abandoned by Wednesday. Start with five to eight that matter most, time them, and add only when the week shows there's room.
- **Tasks with no time and no check.** — "Walk the area" with no time happens when there's time, and with no check it's a stroll. A time slot protects it; what good looks like tells you what you're walking to see.
- **Only ticking, never crossing.** — A sheet of ticks filled in on Friday tells you nothing. The crosses show where the routine breaks, and that's what you change.
- **Checks that find things nobody follows up.** — If a finding leads nowhere, the team learns the routine is for show. Every finding gets an action, one owner and a date, and the open ones are read at the next check.
- **Fixing misses with willpower.** — If the same task is missed whenever something breaks down, promising to try harder won't change next week. Move the time, agree who covers, or drop something else.
- **Nobody looks at the leader's routine.** — Without a regular review with your own manager, the routine slides like any other standard. That conversation is what keeps it alive.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — A planned walk shows where the standard slips; the follow-ups become daily checks on the routine, and a regular walk is often one of its tasks.
- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — The work instruction is the standard the routine checks. "What to check" on a task is usually one or two of its key points.

Downstream:

- **Daily management huddle** (`daily-huddle`) — Running the huddle is usually the first task on the routine, and what the routine finds goes to the huddle the next morning.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Findings that need more than a quick fix move to the countermeasure log, so they're reviewed until they're closed with evidence.
- **Tiered huddle board** (`tiered-huddle-board`) — Each tier's leader has a routine; the higher tiers' routines include checking that the lower tiers' huddles happened and their escalations were answered.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding which few checks matter enough to go on the routine
- Deciding what to change when the same task keeps being missed
- Talking through the week with your own manager

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting patterns across weeks: the same day, the same task, the same kind of finding
- Suggesting what to check from a work instruction or a recent countermeasure
- Drafting a sharper "what good looks like" from a vague task

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting ticks and crosses, the share done and the day totals
- Tracking which findings are open, closed or overdue
- Laying out next week's sheet and exporting it as a workbook
