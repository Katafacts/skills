# SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis

Take an honest look at where you stand against one goal before you plan: strengths and weaknesses inside the team, opportunities and threats outside it, every item backed by evidence and scored for impact, ranked down to the top three per box, then paired into a few actions with an owner and a date.

Family: Hoshin Kanri · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A SWOT analysis sorts what you know about your situation into four boxes. Two are about you, the things inside the team's control: strengths that help and weaknesses that hold you back. Two are about the world around you, things you don't control: opportunities you could use and threats that could cost you. Laid out as a 2x2, it gives a planning team one shared picture before anyone argues about priorities. Most SWOTs fail in the same way: they become four lists of opinions ("great people", "strong brand") that nobody acts on. This version adds three habits that make it useful. Every item needs evidence, a number or a record someone could check, so the boxes describe what's true rather than what the room believes. Every item is scored from 1 to 5 for its impact on one stated goal, so each box can be ranked down to the vital few. And the analysis ends with actions that pair one inside item with one outside item: use a strength to take an opportunity, fix a weakness that blocks one, use a strength to blunt a threat, or protect a weakness from a threat. Each action gets an owner and a date. Without that last step a SWOT is a description, not a decision.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Before an annual plan or a strategy review, so the objectives start from where you really stand rather than from last year's slides.
- Before a big choice (entering a market, launching a service, taking on a large customer), to see what you'd be building on and what you'd be exposed to.
- When a new leader or team needs a shared, evidenced picture of the situation quickly.
- When people disagree about priorities and you want the disagreement to be about facts and impact, not volume.

Not when:

- You have one specific, measured problem to solve. That needs a cause, not a survey of the situation: use A3 problem solving or a 5-Whys.
- You already know the options and need to choose between them. Score the options against weighted criteria on a decision matrix instead.
- There's no goal or decision the analysis will feed. Without one, impact can't be scored and the boxes fill with everything anyone can think of.
- Nobody can get at the evidence (sales records, quality data, customer feedback). A SWOT from memory reflects the loudest view in the room; gather the data first.

## How to fill it in

- **goal** — Write the one goal or decision this analysis serves, such as "choose next year's three objectives" or "decide whether to bid for the hospital contract". Then write the scope: which team, product or site, and which period the evidence covers. Everything after this is judged against the goal, so if the room can't agree on it in a sentence, stop and settle that first.
- **impactScale** — Before anyone scores, agree what 1 and 5 mean against your goal, in a line each. For example, 1 = "barely touches whether we hit the goal" and 5 = "decides it on its own". Agreeing the ends first stops one person's 4 being another person's 2.
- **boxes** — Fill the four boxes, one line per item. Strengths and weaknesses are inside the team's control (your lead time, your skills, your costs); opportunities and threats are outside it (customers, competitors, suppliers, rules). For every item, write the evidence beside it: a number, a record or a source someone could check, such as "9 of 14 lost quotes went on lead time, quote log, Jul to Sep". If one item is really the cause of another, merge them and keep the cause as evidence. If you can't find evidence, leave the item out or go and get it. Then score each item's impact on the goal from 1 to 5. The tool ranks each box and lifts the top three into the vital few. Three to seven items a box is typical; more than seven usually means the box is a list that needs merging and cutting.
- **actions** — Turn the vital few into action. Each action cites one inside item (S or W) and one outside item (O or T), which tells you what kind of move it is: use a strength to take an opportunity, fix a weakness that blocks an opportunity, use a strength to blunt a threat, or protect a weakness from a threat. Write what will be done in plain words, an owner and a due date. Pair from the top three of each box, and make sure each box's top item is answered by at least one action; the tool flags an action that cites an item outside its box's top three. Three to six actions is plenty; they become the candidates for your plan's objectives and strategies.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the analysis is checked for you. It counts each box, flags items with no evidence or no score, flags a box with more than seven items, flags evidence that reads like a view with no number in it, flags an opportunity or threat written as something the team does ("Improve onboarding" is an action, not an opportunity), flags actions that don't cite an item from each side or have no owner or date, and flags an action that starts from an item outside its box's top three. Read each flag and decide; they're prompts, not verdicts.

## What good looks like

The example below is Northwind Fabrication's plant team, in October 2026, getting ready to set next year's plan. The goal is plain: decide the plan's few objectives for becoming the shop customers call first for time-sensitive custom work. Every item cites a record (the order system, the shipping log, the quote log, customers' own scorecards and emails), so the boxes are short and hard to argue with. The single machining operator was first written as a weakness of its own, then merged into the lead-time item it causes, which leaves the top weakness as an 18-day average lead time, over a third of it queuing for the one machining operator. The top opportunity points the same way from outside: the largest customers' new supplier scorecards double the weight on lead time, and one offers up to 8% more for delivery within 10 days. Five actions pair the vital few, using all four kinds of move and answering the top item in every box: a second machining shift, a repeat-customer priority queue offered first to the customer planning to dual-source, honest promise dates and a weekly review of late shipments, a standard changeover, and a fast call to a closed competitor's customers. Those actions are the candidate strategies for next year's hoshin kanri X-matrix.

## Common mistakes

- **Opinions instead of evidence.** — "Great team" and "strong brand" feel true in the room and can't be checked, so they crowd out the items that matter. Ask for the number or the record behind every item. If nobody can find one, the item is a hypothesis to test, not a fact to plan on.
- **No goal.** — A strength for one goal is irrelevant to another. Without a stated goal, impact can't be scored and every box fills with everything anyone can think of. Write the goal first and score every item against it.
- **Four long lists.** — Twelve strengths and fifteen weaknesses read as thorough and decide nothing. Merge items that say the same thing, cut the low scorers, and work from the top three of each box.
- **Inside and outside mixed up.** — "Improve our website" is an action, and "our pricing" is a strength or weakness, not an opportunity or threat. Opportunities and threats are things that happen whether or not you act. Mixing them up hides what you control from what you have to respond to.
- **Stopping at the 2x2.** — A finished grid feels like progress, but nothing changes until someone owns an action by a date. Pair the vital few into actions in the same session, while the evidence is on the table.
- **Only one kind of move.** — Teams often list only ways to chase opportunities with their strengths. The pairs that protect a weakness from a threat are usually the ones that keep the plan alive when something goes wrong; look for at least one.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Scoping canvas** (`scoping-canvas`) — Settles the scope and the goal the analysis serves, so impact has something to be scored against.

Downstream:

- **Hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`hoshin-kanri`) — The SWOT's actions become candidate annual objectives and strategies on the plan, each with an owner and a measure.
- **Revenue hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`revenue-hoshin-kanri`) — The same step for a sales and marketing plan: carry the vital few and the paired actions into the revenue objectives.
- **Weighted decision matrix** (`decision-matrix`) — When the SWOT throws up several competing actions, score them against weighted criteria before committing.
- **Business case** (`business-case`) — An action that needs money or people gets a business case, with the SWOT's evidence as its starting point.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing the goal and agreeing what the impact scores mean
- Deciding which evidence is good enough to plan on
- Choosing which pairs become actions, and owning them

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting items that read like opinions or sit in the wrong box
- Suggesting what record would prove or disprove an item
- Suggesting pairs between the top items that the room might have missed

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting each box and ranking it by impact
- Flagging items with no evidence, long boxes and actions without both sides, an owner or a date
- Laying out the 2x2 and exporting to xlsx in the house format
