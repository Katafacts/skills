# Revenue cascade map

Deploy a revenue plan's objectives to sales, marketing, customer success and revenue operations, each with a measure, a target and one owner, with the catchball written down: what was asked, what each function proposed back, and what was agreed. Checks find plan objectives no function has agreed to, rows that don't trace up, and targets that don't add up to the plan's.

Family: Hoshin Kanri · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A revenue plan only gets delivered when the functions that do the work take each objective on as their own. A revenue cascade map records that hand-off. Each of the plan's objectives sits at the top of a branch; under it go the objectives sales, marketing, customer success or revenue operations take on to deliver it, and under those, what a team within the function does. Every row has a measure, a baseline, a target and one owner, and each level is more specific than the one above: "cut the wait at the proposal stage to 3.5 days" becomes "price standard deals from the new tiers, without approval", measured in days waiting for approval. Targets aren't handed down as orders. Each function is asked for something, says what it can actually do and why, and the two agree; that back-and-forth is called catchball, and the map keeps a line of it for every row. Some revenue measures are made of parts: the proposal wait is the days waiting for pricing plus the days waiting for the rep, and net revenue retention is expansion minus churn and downgrades, in points. Where they are, the map adds up the functions' targets and shows whether they deliver the plan's number or fall short. Others, like a win rate, are driven rather than added up, and the map says so.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A revenue plan for the year has just been agreed and now has to become work that sales, marketing, customer success and revenue operations each own.
- Marketing and customer success sit in the planning meeting but nothing in the plan says what number each of them carries.
- You suspect the functions' targets won't add up to the plan's: every function could hit its number and net revenue retention would still miss.
- Catchball about targets is happening in one-to-ones and you want what each function proposed back, and what was agreed, written down.

Not when:

- There is no agreed revenue plan yet. Set its objectives first with a revenue hoshin kanri X-matrix; the cascade starts from them.
- You want to break one measure, such as pipeline, into the measures that make it within one team. That is a key performance indicator (KPI) tree.
- You need this week's numbers against target for one team. That is a visibility board, which tracks the measures the cascade hands out.

## How to fill it in

- **plan** — Name the revenue plan you're deploying and when it was agreed, so every function knows which version the cascade follows.
- **planRows** — Copy the plan's objectives as Plan rows, numbered 1, 2, 3, with the plan's own measures, baselines, targets and owners. Don't reword them; the cascade starts from what was agreed.
- **lowerRows** — Under each plan row, add what each function takes on: number it 1.1, 1.2 and say it sits under 1. Under a function's row, add a team's row the same way (1.1.1 under 1.1), such as sales enablement under sales. Each row should say what that function will change, not repeat the plan's words. Not every function belongs under every objective: put marketing under the win rate only if it is actually changing something that moves it.
- **measures** — Give every row a measure with its unit, a baseline and a target, as numbers. Where you can, choose measures that are parts of the plan's: days waiting for pricing approval and for the rep add up to the proposal wait; expansion, and churn and downgrades written as negative points, add up to net revenue retention. Then the targets can be added up.
- **owner** — Name one person per row, not a function. The same person can own rows at two levels, as a sales manager often does; say so rather than invent a name.
- **addsUp** — On a row whose measure the rows under it are parts of, set "Adds up" to Yes: their changes should then add up to its own. Set it to No where the rows under it drive the parent without a formula: two engaged contacts per deal and better-fit target accounts raise a win rate, but don't add up to it.
- **catchball** — For each row that was argued, write what was asked for, what the function proposed back and why, and what was agreed. Mark the row Agreed or In discussion. A proposal back with a reason ("the company data we need renews in June") is the most useful line in the map.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. Each row's change (target minus baseline) is worked out, and on a row that adds up, the total of the changes under it. A row is flagged when the targets under it fall more than 2% short of its own change, when it's a plan objective no function has agreed to, when it doesn't trace up to an existing row, when it has no level, owner, measure, baseline or target, when it's still in discussion, or when its number is used twice.
- **nextStep** — Write what was agreed about the flags: who takes the rest of a target that falls short (or how the plan's target changes, and when), when the rows in discussion will be settled, and when owners report their rows.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics deploying its revenue X-matrix for April 2026 to March 2027 to sales, marketing, customer success and revenue operations, written up by the vice president (VP) of sales on 24 April 2026 after the first round of catchball. It has 13 rows: the plan's 3 objectives, 7 function objectives and 3 team objectives, every one with an owner, measure, baseline and target, and all 10 function and team rows trace up. The proposal wait adds up: waiting for pricing approval (4.5 to 1.5 days) and for the rep to write the proposal (1 to 0.5) change it by −3.5 days, exactly the 7 to 3.5 the plan needs; legal review's 1.5 days stays as it is this year. Net revenue retention doesn't. Written in points, it is 100 plus expansion minus churn and downgrades (100 + 4 + 2 − 8 = 98 today). The plan needs +6; growth reviews (+3), marketing's upgrade campaign (+1) and saved renewals (+1) give +5, so the row is flagged. The missing point is the two largest accounts, which renew early in 2027 with budget cuts. The win rate doesn't add up by design: the two-contact rule and better-fit target accounts drive it without a formula. One row is still in discussion: asked for 90% of outbound target accounts fitting the profile from May, marketing proposed 85% from July, because the company data it needs comes on a contract that renews in June. All three plan objectives have a function that agreed to them. The VP Sales and the head of customer success each sponsor one of the two renewals; if they can't be held, the plan's target moves to 103% at the October business review.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving marketing and customer success out because the plan's numbers are sales numbers.** — Win rate and retention depend on who is in the pipeline and what happens after the sale. If those functions carry no row, nobody below the leadership team owns their part.
- **Copying the plan's words down instead of making each row more specific.** — "Lift the win rate" on the sales row tells nobody what to change. Each row should name what that function will do differently, measured in its own terms.
- **Adding up targets that aren't parts of the same measure.** — Retention points from expansion and from churn add up; a win rate and the share of deals with two contacts don't. Set "Adds up" only where the arithmetic is real, and write churn as negative points so the signs work.
- **Handing targets down without catchball.** — A target a function never agreed to is a wish with a name on it. Ask, listen to what comes back and why, and write down what was agreed; the reasons are what make the plan believable.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Revenue hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`revenue-hoshin-kanri`) — The plan being deployed: its annual objectives, targets and owners are the cascade's Plan rows, and its strategies usually become function rows.
- **Marketing true north statement** (`marketing-true-north-statement`) — Marketing's own long-term direction: its rows in the cascade should move one of its dimensions, and a dimension no row moves is a choice to make on purpose.
- **Sales funnel value stream map** (`sales-funnel-map`) — Shows where the days in a stage go, which is how a wait like the proposal stage's splits into parts each function can own and add up.

Downstream:

- **Visibility board** (`visibility-board`) — Where each function tracks its row's measure against target week by week, with an action for every miss.
- **Deal A3 — win-rate root cause analysis** (`deal-a3`) — When a row stays behind its target, an A3 finds out why and what to change.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding what each function takes on and agreeing its target in catchball
- Deciding who takes the rest of a target that falls short, or whether the plan's target changes
- Naming one owner for each row

Analysis — AI helps:

- Suggesting function measures that are parts of the plan's measure, so targets can be added up
- Spotting function rows that only repeat the plan's words
- Spotting rows that trace nowhere and plan objectives no function has taken on

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out each change and adding up the changes under each row
- Keeping the numbering and the catchball record in step with the table
- Drawing the tree and laying out the workbook
