# Impact/effort matrix: sort ideas fast

Sort a list of ideas or countermeasures in minutes: agree what high and low mean, score each idea for impact and effort, and read off what to do now, what to plan, what to keep for when there's time and what to drop, with checks for a grid that's being gamed.

Family: Problem solving / root cause analysis · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

An impact/effort matrix is a quick way to sort a list of ideas before deciding what to do. Each idea gets two scores from 1 to 5: how much it would move the goal (impact) and how much it would take to do (effort). Plot them on a 2x2 with effort across and impact up, and four groups fall out. Do now: high impact, low effort, so start this week. Plan it: high impact, high effort, so these are worth doing properly, with a plan and an owner. If time: low impact, low effort, so pick them up when there's slack. Drop: low impact, high effort, so say no out loud and stop carrying them. The value is speed and shared sight: a team can sort twenty ideas in half an hour and see why each one landed where it did. The risk is that it's easy to game. People score their own ideas up, everything drifts into Do now, and the hard choices hide. So this version asks the team to agree what 1 and 5 mean on each scale before anyone scores, to name who agreed each score, and it flags a grid where most ideas are quick wins, nothing is dropped, ideas sit exactly on the line, or a big commitment is being decided on a rough grid that deserves a proper weighted comparison.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have a list of ideas or countermeasures (from a huddle, a waste walk, a fishbone or an improvement event) and need to decide what to start first.
- The ideas are small to medium and roughly independent, so doing one doesn't rule out another.
- You want the team to agree the order quickly and see the reasoning, not have it handed down.
- You need to clear a backlog: find the few to do now and the ones to stop carrying.

Not when:

- The choice is big, expensive or hard to undo, or two options are close. Two rough scores can't carry that: use a weighted decision matrix with criteria agreed up front.
- You don't yet know what's causing the problem. Sorting fixes before you know the cause just ranks guesses: find the cause first with a Pareto chart, fishbone or 5-Whys.
- The options are alternatives to each other (three ways to do the same thing). Compare them side by side on a decision matrix instead.
- Only one person is scoring. The grid's value is the shared conversation about each score; on your own, a ranked list is quicker.

## How to fill it in

- **sorting** — Say what list you're sorting and what decision it feeds, such as "ten ideas from Monday's huddle; we'll start the quick ones next week and put one bigger one forward for budget". Then write the goal impact is measured against, with a number if you have one ("cut finish rejects from 100 in four weeks"). Impact without a goal turns into "sounds good".
- **scale** — Before anyone scores, agree in one line each what an impact of 1 and of 5 means, and what an effort of 1 and of 5 means. Make the ends concrete: "removes 10 or more rejects in four weeks", "the team can do it this week with no spend", "needs capital or a contractor and more than a month". Scores in between are judgement against those ends. This one step does more than anything else to stop scores drifting up.
- **matrix** — Nothing to enter here: each scored idea lands in its quadrant and on the chart, labelled by its number. A 1 or 2 counts as low and a 3, 4 or 5 as high. A 3 sits exactly on the line: the grid counts it as high on both scales, draws its dot just on the high side, and flags it so the team agrees the call rather than letting the arithmetic decide. Start the Do now ideas, choose which Plan it ideas deserve a plan and an owner, and drop the Drop ideas out loud.
- **items** — List the ideas one per row, in plain words someone could act on. Score impact and effort together as a group, using the scale ends, and name everyone who agreed each score, separated by commas. Use the notes to say why a score is what it is: how much of the problem an idea really reaches when it's only part, and the call you made on any 3. Ten to twenty ideas is a good size; for more than that, group the duplicates first.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the grid is checked for you. It counts each quadrant, and flags missing scale ends, unscored ideas, ideas on the line, ideas scored by one person or nobody named, a grid where more than half the ideas are in Do now (a sign the scale isn't being used honestly), a grid with nothing in Drop, and big (effort 5) or tied Plan it ideas that should go to a weighted decision matrix before anyone commits.

## What good looks like

The example below continues Line 2's paint booth. The 5-Whys fixed the biggest cause of rejects, colour carried over from the spray gun, so at the 6 July huddle the team listed ten ideas for what was left: runs and sags, residue on hanging fixtures, dust, orange peel, overspray and thin coverage. Before scoring they agreed the scale ends against the Pareto chart's count of 100 rejects in four weeks: an impact of 5 removes 10 or more of them, an effort of 5 needs capital or a contractor. Every impact score follows that scale, and where an idea reaches only part of a defect's count the note says how much and why: a weekly fixture strip is a 4, not a 5, because residue still builds up between Fridays. Three people scored every idea together, with the maintenance technician joining for the two that needed equipment. Three ideas land in Do now, led by a viscosity check at every shift start and weekly filter changes; three are Plan it, three If time and one Drop. One idea sits on the line, and its note records the call. Two Plan it ideas tie on impact, a set of fixtures for each colour and a robotic sprayer, and the sprayer needs capital, so the grid sends both to a weighted decision matrix rather than letting two dots decide the budget request.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoring without agreeing the scale.** — One person's 4 is another's 2, so the grid shows who was loudest, not what matters. Agree in a line each what 1 and 5 mean on both scales before the first score.
- **Everything lands in Do now.** — When most ideas look high impact and easy, people are scoring hope, not evidence. Re-score against the scale ends, starting with the ideas the team is least sure of, and ask what each would really take.
- **Nothing gets dropped.** — The Drop box is half the value: it's permission to stop carrying ideas that cost more than they're worth. If it's empty, check the low-impact ideas weren't scored up to keep someone happy.
- **Deciding big bets on the grid.** — Two numbers from 1 to 5 are fine for sorting a list and too rough for a capital request or a choice between close options. Use a weighted decision matrix for those, with the criteria agreed first.
- **Scoring your own ideas alone.** — People rate their own ideas higher and effort lower. Score as a group, include someone who does the work, and name who agreed each score.
- **Sorting fixes before finding the cause.** — A neat grid of countermeasures for a problem nobody has traced is a ranked list of guesses. Find the cause first, then sort the ideas that address it.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Improvement idea funnel** (`improvement-idea-funnel`) — Collects the ideas. Use the matrix to screen a batch from the funnel's first stage and decide which move on.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — When the A3's root cause throws up several possible countermeasures, sort them here and carry the chosen ones back into the A3.

Downstream:

- **Weighted decision matrix** (`decision-matrix`) — For the big or close calls the grid flags: compare the options against weighted criteria before committing.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — The Do now ideas go on the log with an owner, a date and the result you expect, so you can check they worked.
- **Improvement event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — A Plan it idea that needs several people and a few focused days often becomes an improvement event; charter it.

Judgement — stays with you:

Analysis — AI helps:

Drudgery — automated:
