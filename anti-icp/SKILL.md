# Anti-ideal customer profile (anti-ICP)

Decide who not to pursue: hard disqualifiers that should end a deal outright, softer warning signs that need a specific response, process red flags, and the patterns behind customers who churned.

Family: Problem definition · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

An anti-ICP names who not to pursue, or who to pursue only with a specific countermeasure — the same one-page, multi-section discipline as the ICP canvas, deliberately not a mirrored version of it with the polarity flipped. Not every red flag deserves the same weight: a hard disqualifier (an account under a firm size floor, no one to own the tool post-purchase) should end a deal outright, while a softer red flag (a single-threaded champion, a vague trigger) predicts risk without being disqualifying on its own — proceed, but with a specific next step. A real anti-ICP also carries churn-pattern evidence: not just why a deal didn't close, but why a customer who did buy later left, which is often the clearest signal of all for what to watch for earlier.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have real win/loss and churn evidence and want to turn the patterns in it into concrete, actionable disqualifiers and red flags — not a rep's general sense of 'red flags.'
- Reps keep spending real time on deals that predictably won't close or won't stick, and there's no written list of the specific patterns that predicted it before.
- You want to separate what should end a deal outright from what should just trigger a specific countermeasure, instead of treating every red flag the same way.

Not when:

- You don't have real churn or loss evidence yet — an ICP evidence log first, then an anti-ICP built from what it actually shows, beats guessing at disqualifiers from scratch.
- You're tempted to just invert the ICP canvas's own sections — a real anti-ICP is disqualification rules and churn evidence, not a mirrored canvas; forcing that shape misses the hard/soft distinction that makes this genuinely useful.
- You already have a recent, accurate disqualifier list elsewhere — don't duplicate an existing source of truth.

## How to fill it in

- **hard-disqualifiers** — List the facts about a company — size, industry, the systems it runs — that should end a deal outright, however well it seems to be going. Take them from deals you lost and customers who left, not from instinct, and keep the list short: every rule here turns real buyers away.
- **soft-red-flags** — List the warning signs that predict trouble but shouldn't kill a deal on their own, and for each one write the specific next step when it shows up. A flag with no next step is either a disqualifier or not worth listing.
- **process-red-flags** — List the timing or buying-process patterns that have come before a bad outcome, whatever the account looks like on paper — no access to whoever signs, say, or a deadline set by someone outside the deal. Write only patterns you've seen in your own deal history.
- **churn-patterns** — Write the real reasons customers have left, taken from cancellation records, exit notes or the account team — not a guess. Group repeats together: one reason that shows up five times matters more than five that show up once.
- **screening-checklist** — Turn the boxes into a screening checklist: one row per signal with its type (hard, soft or process), the question or check that reveals it, when in the deal it shows up, what to do, and the evidence (how many lost or churned deals showed it). A signal with no count behind it is flagged until something backs it. To screen a live deal, name it and answer Yes, No or Not checked for every signal: any hard disqualifier present ends the deal, a soft or process flag means act on its countermeasure.

The example below splits three firm disqualifiers from two softer red flags and one process red flag, each with a countermeasure and a count of the lost deals that showed it (a single champion with no route to the buyer was behind 3 of the 4 noted losses in the quarter, and none of the wins). ## Common mistakes

- **Treating every red flag as an automatic disqualifier, including soft ones like a single-threaded champion.** — Over-disqualifying kills winnable deals that just need a specific countermeasure (multi-threading, a firmer trigger) — flattening hard and soft red flags into one list throws away exactly the distinction that makes this artifact useful.
- **Only capturing pre-sale loss reasons and skipping churn evidence entirely.** — Churn evidence is often the clearest signal of all for what to watch for earlier — a customer who bought and later left for a specific, findable reason is more informative than a deal that just didn't close.
- **Running the anti-ICP check from memory instead of the actual defined disqualifiers.** — Anti-ICP patterns exist because specific past deals and accounts already failed this way — a rep's general sense of 'red flags' isn't the same as disqualifiers actually derived from real evidence.
