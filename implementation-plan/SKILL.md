# Implementation plan

Milestones through go-live to a check that the change worked, each with one named owner, a start and due date and what it waits on, on a week-by-week timeline. Overdue steps, knock-on warnings, unowned steps and a plan with no check of the result are worked out for you.

Family: Kaizen events · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

An implementation plan turns a decision already made — a countermeasure, a new standard, a system change — into dated milestones. Each milestone has one named owner, even when two teams work on it together, sits with your team, another team or both, and lists the milestones it waits on. The plan doesn't end at go-live: its last milestone starts with "Check:" and confirms the new way is followed and the result moved, measured the way the baseline was, and a change to how work is done also books its sustainment audit. The plan then shows you which steps are overdue, which later steps are at risk because something they wait on is late, which steps have nobody named, and whether anything checks the result. When a date moves, keep the first one beside the new one so the slip stays visible. The split between teams is shown too, as a note: if every milestone sits with your own team, ask whether the others have really committed.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A rollout needs more than one team to do something — maintenance, controls, IT, another shift — and you want a dated sequence instead of a vague understanding.
- You want to know honestly which milestones are already behind schedule and which have nobody named against them yet.
- You're rolling out something (a countermeasure, a new standard, a system change) where an outside team's action is a real dependency, not just your own team's work.

Not when:

- Everything genuinely depends on one person or one team — a plan with no real outside dependency doesn't need this structure; a simple task list will do.
- You don't have a real target completion date yet — pin that down first, or the days-remaining and overdue math has nothing real to measure against.
- You're still deciding what to do, not how to sequence doing it — that's an A3 or a kaizen event charter's job; this tool assumes the commitment is already made.

## How to fill it in

- **milestones** — List the milestones in date order. For each, say whose it is (your team, another team, or both), name one person as owner, and give a start date, a due date and the numbers of any milestones it waits on. A step both teams work on still gets one owner; name the partner in the milestone itself. Agree the other teams' milestones with the people who will do them rather than writing them alone; a step with nobody named is the one that slips. End with a milestone that starts with "Check:": the new way is followed and the result moved, measured the way the baseline was. When a date moves, keep the first one in 'was due' and move the steps that wait on it.
- **coverage** — Calculated from your milestones and dates — don't type it. Check the done, overdue and unowned counts match what you know and read the knock-on warnings. It also flags a plan with no milestone that checks the result, and a milestone with more than one owner.
- **risks** — List what could realistically make this plan slip — a change freeze, a shift with no supervisor, a part on long lead time. Write each one specifically enough that someone could act on it.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences on where the plan stands: what's on track, what's overdue or unowned, and the biggest risk to the date. Use only what the plan shows.

## What good looks like

The example rolls out a new andon escalation rule on a fabrication cell's three shifts: go-live on May 1, then the sustainment audit booked and, by May 15, a check from two weeks of logged calls that the rule is followed and response times moved. Ten milestones, each with one owner. The controls change has already slipped from April 5 to April 14, and the trainings were moved behind it. The escalation card is two days overdue, so all three trainings carry a knock-on warning. Third-shift training has no owner yet.

## Common mistakes

- **Ending the plan at go-live.** — Go-live is when the change starts, not when it has worked. Without a dated check that the new way is followed and the result moved, nobody finds out until the problem comes back.
- **Giving a shared step two owners.** — When two people own a step, each assumes the other has it. Name one owner and put the partner in the milestone.
- **Leaving a milestone unowned and moving on rather than naming the gap.** — An unowned milestone isn't a detail to fill in later — it's a real risk to the go-live date, and the plan should show it rather than look more complete than it is.
- **Moving a late date without moving what waits on it, or without keeping the old date.** — The knock-on warnings only help if the later steps are re-planned too, and a date that quietly changes hides how often the plan has slipped.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart** (`raci-chart`) — Agree who owns each task and decision first; the A on a row is usually the right owner for the action that changes it.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — A countermeasure an A3 identifies is exactly the kind of commitment that needs a real, dated, cross-functional plan to actually happen.
- **Kaizen event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — Once a kaizen event's plan is set, rolling out what it produced across a wider area is what this tool sequences.

Downstream:

- **Kaizen event report-out** (`kaizen-event-report-out`) — When the rollout comes from an event, its finished milestones close the matching action on the event's report-out, and the report-out's follow-up checks show whether the result held.
