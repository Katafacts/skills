# Sales funnel value stream map

See where deals wait, where they are lost and where rep time goes: real stage-to-stage timing from your customer relationship management (CRM) system, flow efficiency, and the stage that uses the most rep time once you count how many deals it handles.

Family: Value stream mapping · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A sales funnel map lays your pipeline out stage by stage, from qualified lead to signed contract: how long reps actually work each deal at each stage, how long deals sit waiting before the next one, what share is lost at each stage, and how many deals wait in the queue. From that it works out how long deals spend at each stage, what share of that time anyone is working on them, where deals wait longest, and, given how many deals you need to win, how many rep hours each stage needs a month. Two things a seasoned eye adds. The stage holding the funnel back is the one using the most rep time a month (deals in × hours per deal), not the one with the longest calls: an early stage with short calls handles the most deals. And a deal lost late has already used every earlier stage's rep time, so losing a poor fit at the first call is cheap and losing a deal after the proposal is not. The customer relationship management (CRM) system already holds every number it needs.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You can pull real per-stage timestamps from the CRM — when a deal actually entered and left each stage — not just a quarter-end pipeline snapshot.
- The segment is bounded: one deal source, one team, or one product line with a genuinely comparable sales motion, not the whole pipeline blended together.
- You want a defensible flow-efficiency number instead of an impression that deals 'take too long,' with a specific stage named as the constraint.

Not when:

- You only have secondhand or quarter-end data — go pull actual per-stage CRM timestamps first; a funnel map built from memory inherits every gap in that memory, same as any other value stream map.
- The pipeline blends deal types with genuinely different natural cycles (self-serve and enterprise in one map) — scope down to one comparable segment first, the same way a shop-floor map covers one product family.
- You're trying to redesign the sales process before measuring the current one — you can't fix a flow you haven't quantified, and 'the process feels broken' isn't a finding.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — State what's in and out in a sentence or two — which deal source or segment, which start and end stage (typically sales-qualified lead to closed-won or closed-lost). A funnel mapped with no stated boundary quietly blends every deal type into numbers that describe none of them.
- **steps** — List each pipeline stage in order with its process time (time actually being worked — calls, writing proposals) and wait time after it (time stalled before the next stage starts). Hours or days both work. Add the share of deals lost at the stage (lost %) and the deals waiting after it, only where your CRM actually has the data. Leave reps blank when the same people work every stage. Average the waits over every deal, lost ones included, or the map hides the deals that sit for weeks before going quiet; put the long waits where deals really stall. Then check them stage by stage against today's open deals: open deals at a stage ≈ deals in per month at that stage × months spent there. Add the stages up and compare with the open-deal count. A one-day count swings by about 2 × √(expected) by chance, so a small gap is normal and a large one means a number is wrong. Size a queue against the deals that pass it, not against wins.
- **demand** — Optional, but worth it. Enter how many deals the team needs to win per week or month and the selling hours the team has. Working back through each stage's lost %, the map shows how many deals each stage must take in, the rep hours each stage needs (deals in × rep time per deal), whether all stages together fit the hours, and how much rep time goes on deals that are later lost.
- **map** — Drawn from the stages — don't draw it by hand; check each box and queue against your stage data. Each stage is a box, stalled deals are queues between them, and a timeline makes the waiting impossible to miss. To change the picture, change the stage data.
- **metrics** — Calculated from the stage data — don't type them. With waits averaged over every deal, the lead time is the stage averages added up, not any one deal's cycle: it is longer than a won deal takes, because lost deals stall. A funnel's flow efficiency is usually strikingly low, often single digits, because a deal spends almost all its time waiting rather than being worked; that's the finding, not a bug. With demand entered, the stage using the most rep time a month is the one to watch, not the stage with the longest calls; if all stages together need more hours than the team has, something waits; and if they need fewer and deals still wait, the cause is how work is chosen and approved, not capacity. The share of qualified leads that become contracts is the stages' advance rates multiplied together.
- **wasteFindings** — Waiting, deals piling up and deals lost are calculated from your stage data — don't type them; check them against the pipeline. For deals lost, read where the rep time went, not just the lost %: the costly losses are the late ones. Add the other five wastes only when your notes give them something concrete.
- **pareto** — Calculated from the stall time after each stage — don't type it; check the top stage is where deals really sit. If a few large deals matter more to the forecast than raw time, look at the stage conversion analysis alongside it.
- **futureState** — Copy today's stages and redesign them — a shorter pricing approval, a cap on deals between demo and proposal — and the before-and-after table shows what the redesigned funnel would deliver in lead time, flow efficiency and conversion.
- **countermeasures** — Rank by impact and effort, and name the specific finding each one addresses. When deals pile up at a stage, the first fix is to stop starting new work (no new demos while proposals are overdue), not to chase each deal. For losses, move the check earlier so a deal that will be lost is lost cheaply. 'Reps should move faster' doesn't trace to a finding.

## What good looks like

The example below maps Beacon Analytics' outbound mid-market pod on 16 March 2026, the day of its deal aging report: a small team winning about 1 deal a month, with about 90 rep hours a month for new outbound deals. Averaged over every deal, the stages add up to 2,950 hours (about 123 days), worked for 22 of them, a flow efficiency of 0.75%. That is not how long a deal takes: most won deals close in 6 to 8 weeks, and the averages are pulled up by lost deals. The longest wait, 1,200 hours (50 days), is after the proposal, where deals with one contact stall; 7 of those days are Beacon's own approvals and writing, the part the team controls. Checked stage by stage against the aging report (deals in at each stage × time there, added up), the map expects about 10 open deals against 14 counted, inside a one-day count's chance swing, and most of the gap is the demo queue: 4 deals waiting for a proposal, 1.93 months of the 2.07 deals a month that pass it. Rep time is not the constraint: lead qualification uses the most, 15.61 hours a month, and all six stages together need 62.46 hours, 69.40% of what the reps give this pipeline. So the waits are policy (new demos win the afternoon, two pricing approvers) and stalled deals, not capacity. About 40 of the 62 hours go on deals later lost, the most (11.79) on deals lost after the proposal at 15 hours each. The countermeasures stop starting new demos while proposals are overdue, cut the approval wait, and hold the deal A3's discovery-to-demo gate, so a deal that will stall is lost after 5 rep hours, not 15.

## Common mistakes

- **Stage timing comes from 'days since deal created' instead of actual stage-entry and stage-exit timestamps.** — Deal-age conflates every stage into one number and hides exactly where the time actually goes — the whole point of mapping the funnel is naming which stage is the problem, not confirming the pipeline overall feels slow.
- **Naming the stage with the longest rep time per deal as the constraint.** — A stage with long calls on a few deals can use less rep time than a short stage on many. Multiply by the deals each stage handles and compare with the hours the team has; when the same reps work every stage, add the stages up.
- **Every stage gets flagged as a bottleneck.** — Naming the one or two stages actually worth acting on beats cataloguing every stage with nonzero wait — the same vital-few discipline as any value stream map.
- **The share lost at each stage is estimated from a rep's gut sense of 'about half fall through' instead of pulled from CRM stage-history.** — Reps' recall skews toward memorable losses, not typical ones. The CRM's actual stage-transition history is the ground truth here, the same way direct observation is for a shop-floor value stream map.
- **The pipeline segment mixes deal types with genuinely different natural cycles.** — A funnel mapped with no scope boundary blends a two-week self-serve deal and a six-month enterprise deal into an average that describes neither — same failure mode as an unscoped operational value stream.
- **Countermeasures target the stage that's easiest to change organizationally, not the one the data names as the constraint.** — The longest wait and the stage using the most rep hours are where the funnel actually loses time and deals. A politically easy fix elsewhere feels productive and moves nothing.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Qualification standard work** (`qualification-standard-work`) — Defines what actually earns sales-qualified status — the funnel map's start boundary is only meaningful if that definition is consistently applied before deals enter the mapped segment.
- **Deal aging report** (`deal-aging-report`) — Counts the stalled deals at each stage boundary that feed this map's work-in-progress counts. Generating leads faster than the funnel's constraint can absorb them shows up there as inventory, not pipeline health.

Downstream:

- **Deal A3 — win-rate root cause analysis** (`deal-a3`) — The funnel map's longest wait or heaviest stage is exactly the kind of bounded, data-backed problem a deal A3 exists to close in depth.
- **Pipeline management board** (`pipeline-management-board`) — Once the funnel map names the stage where deals pile up, the weekly pipeline board watches it: deals over that stage's age limit show red every week until the fix holds.
