# SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers)

Outline a process on one page before you map or change it: where it starts and ends, its 4 to 7 main steps, what it produces and who receives each output, what it needs and who supplies each input, and what each customer needs and how you'd measure it. The counts and the gap checks (an input nobody supplies, an output nobody receives, a customer with no stated need) are computed, not typed in.

Family: Problem definition · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A SIPOC is a one-page outline of a process, named after its five columns read left to right: suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers. In the middle sit the handful of main steps between a clear start and a clear end. To the right, what the process produces and who receives each thing. To the left, what the process needs and who provides each thing. Under it, one line per customer saying what they most need and how anyone would know they got it. It takes an hour with the right people in the room, and it settles three arguments before they waste a week: where this process starts and stops, who it is actually for, and which hand-offs from other teams it depends on. It is deliberately high level. Four to seven steps is enough to see the whole process at once; the detail of who does each step, how long it waits and where it loops back belongs in a process map drawn afterwards.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're about to map, measure or improve a process and the team doesn't yet agree where it starts and ends.
- Nobody has written down who the process serves, or the customers named are just whoever shouts loudest.
- Problems seem to arrive from outside the process (late, incomplete or wrong inputs) and you need to see who supplies what.
- You're starting an improvement event or a project and need a shared picture of the process for the charter in an hour, not a week.
- Someone new to the area needs the whole process on one page before they go and watch it.

Not when:

- You need to see where the work waits, loops back or gets re-keyed. This one-page outline is too high level for that; follow a real case with a process map instead.
- The process is already outlined and agreed, and the question is why a measure is off. Go and look at the work and count the problem.
- You're trying to design a new process from scratch. Outline it afterwards to check it, but start from what the customer needs.
- The "process" is really a whole organisation or value stream crossing many teams. Outline one process at a time, or draw the value stream.

## How to fill it in

- **boundary** — Name the process, then write where it starts and where it ends, each as an event someone could see happen: "Vendor sends an invoice", "Payment leaves our bank account". Avoid starts like "invoice processing begins", which nobody can point at. Then write one sentence on what this outline is for, such as agreeing scope before a waste walk. Most of the value of a SIPOC is in this argument, so have it with the people who do the work, not alone.
- **steps** — Write 4 to 7 main steps between the start and the end, each starting with a verb ("Match purchase order, receipt and invoice"). Main stages only: if you have more than seven, group them; if you have fewer than four, you're probably describing the outcome, not the process. Write the process as it runs today, not as the procedure says it should. Fill this column first: it's the easiest to agree, and the other four columns hang off it.
- **outputs** — List what the process produces, as things someone receives: a payment, an email, a posted record, a report. For each output, name the customer who receives it. Customers can be outside the organisation (the vendor) or inside it (the controller, a department). Include outputs nobody thinks of as the point of the process, like a record another team relies on; they often explain complaints from unexpected places.
- **customers** — Give each customer one row: the one thing they most need from what they receive, in their words if you can, and how anyone would know it's met. "Paid within 3 business days without chasing" measured as "business days from invoice to payment" is useful; "good service" measured by "feedback" is not. If you don't know what a customer needs, write that down and go and ask: guessing here sends the whole improvement in the wrong direction.
- **inputs** — List what the steps need to run (documents, data, approvals, rules, materials) and, for each, the supplier who provides it. Suppliers can be other teams, systems or outside parties. An input with nobody named as its supplier is a gap worth noticing: when it arrives late or wrong, there's no one to ask to fix it at the source.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the outline is checked for you. It counts each column (each supplier and customer once, however many rows name them) and flags a missing start or end, fewer than 4 or more than 7 steps, an input with no supplier, an output with no customer, a customer receiving an output whose need isn't written, a requirement with no measure, and a customer who receives nothing. Each flag is a question for the team, not a mistake to hide.

## What good looks like

The example below is the outline Meridian Business Services' accounts payable team agreed before walking their vendor invoice approval process for waste and mapping one invoice through it step by step. The boundary runs from the vendor sending an invoice to the payment leaving the bank, the same boundary the later map uses. Six main steps sit between them. Five outputs go to four customers, and each customer has one requirement with a measure: the vendor's "paid within 3 business days without chasing" became the target for the whole improvement. Six inputs come from five suppliers, including two the team hadn't thought of as suppliers at all: the controller, who sets the approval limits, and finance systems, who own the vendor record and the payment calendar. Every check passes.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with suppliers because they're the first column.** — The columns are read left to right but filled in from the middle out. Start with the steps, then outputs and customers, then inputs and suppliers. Starting on the left produces a list of every team you've ever worked with.
- **Writing twenty steps.** — A SIPOC with the detail of a process map is a bad process map. Keep 4 to 7 main stages so the whole process fits in one look, and draw the detail afterwards when you know which part of the process to look at.
- **A vague boundary.** — "Starts when we get the request" lets everyone keep their own version of the process. Write the start and end as events you could see happen, and the arguments about scope end in the first meeting instead of the fifth.
- **Customers without a requirement, or a requirement nobody can measure.** — Naming a customer is easy; knowing what they need is the work. Without a stated requirement and a measure, the team will improve what's easy to improve and call it a win, whether or not anyone receiving the output notices.
- **Drawing the process as the procedure says, not as it runs.** — The written procedure is usually tidier than reality. Outline what actually happens today, with the people who do the work, or the improvement will fix a process that doesn't exist.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Scoping canvas** (`scoping-canvas`) — Sets what's in and out of scope for the improvement. The SIPOC turns that scope into a process boundary and the handful of steps inside it.

Downstream:

- **Voice of the customer (VOC) collection plan** (`voc-collection-plan`) — When a customer's requirement is a guess, plan how to hear it from them directly, then come back and rewrite it in their words.
- **Critical-to-quality (CTQ) tree** (`ctq-tree`) — Breaks a customer's broad need from the requirements table into specific, measurable features the process has to get right.
- **Makigami process map** (`makigami`) — Follows one real case through the process the SIPOC outlined, with every step, hand-off, document and wait, inside the same boundary.
- **Value stream map — current and future state** (`value-stream-map`) — When the outline shows the problem crosses several processes, map the whole flow from customer request to delivery to find where it waits.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Agreeing where the process starts and ends
- Deciding who the customers really are and what they need
- Choosing which part of the process to map or improve next

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting inputs with no supplier, outputs with no customer and customers with no stated need
- Suggesting how to group a long list of steps into 4 to 7 main stages
- Pointing out a requirement that can't be measured as written

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting each column, with every supplier and customer counted once
- Turning a whiteboard photo or meeting notes into the five columns
- Laying the outline out on one page and exporting it as a workbook
