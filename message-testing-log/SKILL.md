# Message testing log

Test one change to a message at a time: the new version (the variant) against the current one (the control), same audience, same time, with the prediction written before the send. The sample each side needs, both rates, the difference and an honest verdict (variant likely better or worse, no clear difference, too few to tell) are computed, and tests with no prediction, no control or too few responses to decide on are flagged.

Family: Experimentation · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A message testing log is a record of small, fair experiments on what you say to buyers: a subject line, an opening line, a call to action, a button. Each test changes one thing. The current message is the control; the new version is the variant. Both go to the same kind of people at the same time, split at random, so the only difference between the two groups is the message. Before anything is sent, you write down why you think the change will work and how much better you expect it to do, in points ("3% of people reply today; we predict 6%"). That prediction does two jobs. It stops a result being read as a win after the fact, and it tells you how many people each side needs: small lifts need thousands, big ones need hundreds. When the counts come in, the log works out each side's rate and the difference, and gives a plain verdict. Variant likely better, or likely worse: a gap that size turns up by chance less than 1 time in 20, and it says which way it went. A variant that is reliably worse is worth knowing too. Too few to tell: fewer than 10 responses in all, or fewer people than the prediction needs. No clear difference: enough people and no gap worth trusting, which is a real answer too. The decision and the next test go beside it, so over a quarter the log shows which changes really moved replies, meetings or demo requests, and which only looked like they did.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You send the same kind of message often enough (cold email, follow-ups, ads, a landing page) to split it and count the results.
- People argue about which subject line, opener or offer works better, and the answer keeps coming from whoever speaks loudest.
- A change has been declared a winner after a good week, and you want to know whether it would hold up.
- You're about to roll a new claim or proof point out to every channel and want evidence it earns more responses first.

Not when:

- The audience is too small to split: a list of 40 target accounts will never give enough responses. Talk to them instead, with a customer interview guide.
- You want to know whether a claim is true and cleared for use, not whether it lands. That is a proof point inventory.
- You want to know why buyers chose you or didn't. Rates tell you what changed behaviour, not why; use win-loss interviews.
- You want to change five things at once. Test them one at a time, or treat it as a new message and compare it whole.

## How to fill it in

- **purpose** — Say what the messages are for and who they go to, and how each rate is counted, so a reply means the same thing in every test: for example, reply rate is human replies (not out-of-office) over emails delivered; meeting rate is meetings booked over people messaged; conversion rate is form submissions over page visitors. Use the definitions your team already reports.
- **change** — Name the one thing that changes ("Subject line: the trial result instead of the topic"). Write the control as it is sent today and the variant with only that one change. Pick the channel, the metric that tells you whether it worked, and the audience: the same kind of people for both sides, split at random, sent at the same time. Sending the variant this month and comparing it with last month's rate is not a test; too much else changed.
- **prediction** — Before the send, write the hypothesis ("Because these buyers have seen every pipeline subject line, a result they can check will earn more replies") and the prediction: the control's usual rate from past sends, and how many points better you expect the variant to do. Be honest about the size: most wording changes move a rate by a point or two, not ten. Then name the guardrail: the measure further down that must not get worse while this rate goes up, usually meetings from replies or qualified leads from sign-ups. A subject line can double replies and still bring in worse conversations.
- **sample** — Nothing to enter: the log works out how many people each side needs for a real lift of the size you predicted to show up about 4 times in 5. Use it to plan. If you can't reach that many in a sensible time, test a bolder change, pick a channel with more volume, or accept that this test can't tell you much.
- **results** — When each side has reached the number needed, enter how many people got each version and how many did what the metric counts. Stop at the planned number, not when the result looks good: peeking and stopping early is how chance gets declared a winner. Count the guardrail on both sides too and say whether it held.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter. The log works out both rates, the difference in points and the verdict: variant likely better, variant likely worse, no clear difference, too few to tell, or no control. It flags a test with no prediction, a result with no control, a decision taken on too few responses (unless the decision is to run it bigger), a variant adopted without a clear win, and a variant adopted while its guardrail got worse or went unchecked. A flag is a question to answer before the change goes everywhere.
- **decision** — Record what was done: adopt the variant, keep the control, run it bigger, or drop the idea. Write the next test, usually the next thing in the same message. Read the log every few weeks: which wins held, which tests were too small, and what is worth testing next.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' demand generation lead's log of five message tests run from April to June 2026, aimed at revenue operations and sales leaders at mid-market logistics companies, using only the proof point cleared for use outside the company (41 of 44 stalled deals flagged within 48 hours). Four of the five had a prediction written first. One variant is likely better: the trial result in the subject line took replies from 3.1% to 6.3% with 750 people a side, against 745 needed, and its guardrail held (9 meetings against 5). Two are too few to tell: the social opener was adopted after 8 meetings in all (3.3% against 10%), which the log flags, and the landing page button has 1,200 visitors a side against the 4,559 its 1-point prediction needs, so it keeps running. The second-email offer made no clear difference (6.2% against 6.9%) with more people than the 1,205 a side needed, and its replies became fewer meetings (9 against 21), so the meeting ask stays. The explainer email to the nurture list was called a win with no control and no prediction, and is flagged three ways. Two of five tests have a check to look at, and the review says what changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing the new message with last month's numbers instead of a control.** — Timing, list quality, holidays and luck all move a rate. Without the current message sent to the same audience at the same time, any difference could be any of them. Send both, split at random.
- **Calling a winner on a handful of responses.** — Six meetings against two looks like triple, and it is well within what chance produces. Wait until each side reaches the sample the prediction needs; if that is more people than you can reach, test a bigger change.
- **Writing the prediction after the result.** — Once the numbers are in, almost any result can be told as a win. A prediction written first, with its size, is what makes the test honest and tells you how many people it needs.
- **Adopting a variant on the tested rate alone.** — A rate near the top of the funnel is easy to lift with a sharper hook and harder to turn into business. Count the guardrail further down on both sides; a variant that wins replies and loses meetings has not won.
- **Changing several things at once.** — If the subject, the opener and the call to action all change and replies rise, nobody knows which one did it, and the next message can't build on it. One change per test.
- **Treating no clear difference as a failed test.** — A fair test with enough people that finds no difference is an answer: the change doesn't matter enough to fight over. Keep the simpler version and spend the next test on something bigger.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing what is worth testing and predicting how much it will move
- Deciding what to do with a result, especially no clear difference
- Judging whether a variant says something true and fair to the buyer

Analysis — AI helps:

- Writing variants that change one thing and keep the rest word for word
- Spotting tests that changed more than one thing or compared different audiences
- Suggesting the next test from what the log has already shown

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out the sample each side needs, the rates and the difference
- Checking every result for a control, a prediction and enough responses
- Laying the log out and exporting it as a workbook
