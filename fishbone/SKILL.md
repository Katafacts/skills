# Fishbone diagram

Lay out every possible cause of one measured problem under six headings (people, method, equipment, materials, measurement, environment), then go and check the likeliest ones and mark each verified, ruled out or not checked. The verified causes are what you take into a 5-Whys. The counts and the warnings (an empty heading, a fix written as a cause, a cause that blames a person, too many unchecked) are computed, not typed in.

Family: Problem solving / root cause analysis · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A fishbone diagram is a way of listing the possible causes of one problem so a team looks wide before it digs deep. The problem goes at the head of the fish, the spine runs back from it, and each bone off the spine is a heading for one kind of cause. The usual six headings are people, method, equipment, materials, measurement and environment, and they are there to make the team ask about each one on purpose, rather than stopping at the first idea someone says out loud. The drawing is the easy part. What makes it useful is the second step most teams skip: every cause on the diagram is only an idea until someone goes to where the work happens and checks it. So each cause here is marked verified (seen happening), ruled out (checked, and it isn't this) or not checked. The diagram doesn't find the root cause by itself. It narrows a long list of ideas to the few that are real, and those go into a 5-Whys to find out why they happen. You'll also hear it called a cause-and-effect diagram.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have one specific, measured problem (often the top bar of a Pareto chart) and several possible causes, and the team doesn't agree which one it is.
- A team keeps jumping to the same favourite explanation and you want to check the others before acting.
- The problem crosses more than one part of the work (people, equipment, materials and how the work is measured) and nobody can see all of it alone.
- You can get to where the work happens to check causes, not just talk about them in a meeting room.

Not when:

- You don't know yet which problem to work on, or the problem is a broad label like "quality issues". Count and split it first with a Pareto chart.
- The cause is already clear and has been seen happening. Go straight to a 5-Whys on it.
- There's one sudden failure with an obvious trigger (a broken part, a missed delivery). A short 5-Whys is quicker.
- Nobody can go and check any of the causes. A fishbone filled in and never checked is a list of opinions, and it gives them more weight than they deserve.

## How to fill it in

- **effect** — Write the problem at the head of the fish as a measured effect: what happens, how often or how much, over what period, where. "31 of 100 parts rejected for colour carryover from the gun or nozzle, four weeks, Line 2 paint booth", not "paint quality". A number makes it something you can check later, and a narrow effect keeps the causes specific. If it's a broad label, split it with a Pareto chart first.
- **categories** — Start with the six generic headings: people (who does the work and how they're supported), method (the steps and the order they're done in), equipment (machines and tools), materials (what goes in), measurement (how the work and the result are checked) and environment (temperature, light, layout, time of day). They're prompts, not rules: rename, merge or add a heading if it fits your work better. Keep every heading on the diagram even if it ends up empty, so the gap shows.
- **causes** — With the people who do the work, list possible causes under each heading. Write each one as something that is happening ("flush stops once the solvent runs clear"), not as a fix ("flush longer") and not as blame ("operator is careless"). If a cause is about a person, write the part of the process around them instead: what makes the mistake easy, and why nothing catches it. Aim for the causes that could really produce this effect, not every idea in the room.
- **status** — Pick the likeliest one to three causes and go and check them where the work happens: watch it, measure it, or pull the real record. Mark each cause verified (seen happening, and it could produce the effect), ruled out (checked, and it isn't this) or not checked, and write in one line what was seen or why it hasn't been checked yet. Ruled-out causes stay on the diagram: they show what was looked at, and stop the same idea being raised again next month.
- **summary** — Calculated from the diagram — don't type it; check the counts against the causes you wrote. The verified causes are the ones to take into a 5-Whys. The warnings flag an empty heading, a cause written as a fix, a cause that blames a person, more than five causes not checked, no verified cause yet, and an effect with no number in it.
- **nextStep** — Say which verified cause goes into a 5-Whys first, and why. If several verified causes are linked (two parts of the same step, say), one chain may cover them. Say what happens to the causes not checked yet: usually, leave them unless the problem doesn't move after the countermeasure.

## What good looks like

The example below continues Line 2's paint booth. The Pareto chart found that 31 of 100 finish rejects in four weeks came from old colour left in the spray gun or nozzle, so that is the effect at the head of the fish, with its numbers. The team listed twelve possible causes across the six headings, then watched four changeovers at the booth. Three causes were verified: the flush stops once the solvent runs clear, the nozzle is left on during the flush, and nothing checks the gun is clean before the next colour. Six were ruled out with evidence, including the obvious suspect (one shift being worse) and a new solvent. Three are not checked yet and are left for later on purpose. The verified causes go into the 5-Whys.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the diagram as the answer.** — A fishbone full of causes looks thorough, but every cause on it is a guess until someone has checked it. Teams that stop at the drawing usually pick the cause the loudest person favoured. Go and check the likeliest ones, and mark what you found.
- **Writing fixes instead of causes.** — "Need more training" or "buy a new gun" is a solution in disguise. It skips the question of what is actually happening, and the fix often lands on the wrong cause. Write what is going on, and keep the fixes for after the cause is confirmed.
- **Blaming people.** — "Operator error" or "careless" ends the conversation and fixes nothing: the next person in the same process makes the same mistake. Write the part of the process that let it happen, so there's something you can change.
- **A vague effect at the head of the fish.** — "Paint problems" invites every complaint anyone has ever had about the booth. A narrow, measured effect keeps the causes specific, and the number lets you check later whether the countermeasure worked.
- **Hiding the empty headings.** — A heading nobody filled in is often the part of the work nobody thought about, which is where surprises come from. Keep it on the diagram, ask about it on purpose, and if it really doesn't apply, say so.
- **Trying to check every cause.** — Twenty causes checked lightly teaches less than three checked properly. Pick the likeliest one to three, check them at the work, and only widen the search if none of them hold up.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Pareto chart** (`pareto-chart`) — Finds which part of the problem to work on first. The top bar, split again until it's specific, becomes the effect at the head of the fish.
- **Go-see protocol** (not in the catalogue yet) — How to go and check causes where the work happens, so the verified and ruled-out marks come from what was seen, not what was remembered.

Downstream:

- **5-Whys** (`five-whys`) — Takes a verified cause and asks why it happens, one step at a time, until it reaches something in the process you can change.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — When the problem is worth a full one-page record, the fishbone's verified causes and the 5-Whys chain feed the A3's root cause section.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Tracks what is done about each confirmed cause, and whether the cause comes back after the fix is closed.
