# 5-Whys

Start from one specific, measured problem and ask why it happens, then why that happens, checking each answer where the work happens, until you reach a cause in the process you can act on. End with a countermeasure tied to that cause. The chain checks (stopped early, an answer that doesn't follow, a step not checked, a chain that ends by blaming a person, a countermeasure that misses the cause) are computed, not typed in.

Family: Problem solving / root cause analysis · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A 5-Whys is a chain of questions that walks from a problem back to a cause you can do something about. You state the problem, ask why it happens, write the answer, then ask why of that answer, and keep going. Each answer becomes the next question. The name comes from the rough number of steps it usually takes, but five is a guide, not a rule: some chains reach an actionable cause in three, some need six. The point is to get past the first, obvious answer ("the gun was dirty") to the reason the process allowed it ("the routine has no standard flush and no check"), because fixing the obvious answer only treats the symptom and the problem comes back. Two habits separate a useful chain from a story. First, every answer is checked where the work happens or in the real record before you ask the next why: an unchecked answer is a guess, and every why after it rests on that guess. Second, the chain ends at the process, not at a person. People make mistakes in every process; the useful question is why this one let the mistake through.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have one specific, measured problem, ideally a cause already verified on a fishbone diagram or the top bar of a Pareto chart.
- The quick fix has been tried and the problem keeps coming back.
- You can go to where the work happens, or pull the real record, to check each answer as you go.
- You want a short written record of how the team got from the problem to the countermeasure, so others can check the reasoning.

Not when:

- The problem is broad or made of many separate events ("quality is bad"). Count and split it with a Pareto chart first, or the chain will wander.
- There are several possible causes and nobody knows which is real. Lay them out and check them on a fishbone diagram first, then run the chain on the one that holds up.
- Nobody can go and check the answers. A chain built in a meeting room from memory reaches a confident-sounding cause that may be wrong.
- The problem has several causes acting together, each needed for it to happen. One straight chain will miss the others: run one chain per verified cause, or use a fishbone to keep them all in view.

## How to fill it in

- **problem** — Write the problem you're starting from as a measured fact: what happens, how often, over what period, where. "31 of 100 rejects in four weeks were colour carryover from the gun or nozzle", not "paint quality". A narrow, measured problem keeps the chain on track and gives you a number to check against after the countermeasure.
- **startingPoint** — Say where the chain starts and why there: usually the top bar of a Pareto chart, or the verified cause from a fishbone diagram that's most likely to explain the problem. Say whether you're starting from what was seen at the work. Starting from a verified cause rather than the general problem saves two or three whys and keeps the chain from wandering.
- **whys** — Ask why the problem happens and write the answer as a plain statement of fact. Then ask why of that answer, and keep going. Each new why is asked of the answer just before it, so each answer should lead on from the last. For each step, write how the answer was checked: seen at the work, from the record, or not yet checked. If it isn't checked, go and check it before asking the next why. Stop when the answer is something in the process you can change (a missing standard, a step with no check, a rule that pushes the wrong way), usually three to seven whys in.
- **checks** — Calculated from the chain — don't type it; read each flag and decide whether it's right. It counts the answered whys and how many were checked at the work, and flags a chain that stopped early (fewer than 3), one that's very long (more than 7), an answer that shares no words with the one before (a sign the chain jumped to a different cause), a step not checked at the work, a chain that ends by blaming a person, an empty root cause, and a countermeasure that doesn't mention anything from the root cause. These are structural checks, not a judgement of the logic: read the flagged step again and decide.
- **rootCause** — Write the cause the chain ends on, in words someone could act on. A good root cause names part of the process (a step, a standard, a check, a rule), not a person and not a general condition like "time pressure". Test it: if you fixed this, would the problem stop coming back? If the answer is "only if people try harder", ask one more why.
- **countermeasure** — Write what will change, tied directly to the root cause, using the same words so the link is plain. Name an owner and a due date. Say how you'll know it worked: usually the same count as the problem statement, over a period of the same length, after the change. A countermeasure that only reminds people to be careful hasn't changed the process.

## What good looks like

The example below continues Line 2's paint booth. The fishbone diagram verified, at the booth, that the changeover flush stops as soon as the solvent runs clear with the nozzle still on, and that nothing checks the gun is clean before the next colour. The chain starts there and takes five whys, each checked at the booth or in the booth's own records: old paint is left behind the nozzle seat; the short flush doesn't reach it; the routine card says only "flush until clear"; the card was rewritten that way when the changeover was shortened; and no step checks the gun before the next colour runs. The root cause is the routine, not the operators, and the countermeasure puts back a standard, timed flush with the nozzle off and a test-card check before the first part.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at a person.** — "Operator error", "not careful enough" or "needs retraining" feel like answers but fix nothing: the next person in the same process makes the same mistake. Ask why the process allowed it. What made the mistake easy, and why did nothing catch it?
- **Answering from memory.** — A chain built in a meeting room sounds convincing and is often wrong at step two, and every why after a wrong answer is wrong too. Check each answer at the work or in the real record before asking the next why.
- **Starting from a vague problem.** — "Why is quality bad?" has a dozen true answers and the chain follows whichever one comes up first. Start from one measured problem, ideally a verified cause from a fishbone or the top bar of a Pareto chart.
- **Jumping to a different cause halfway down.** — If an answer doesn't follow from the one before, the chain has switched problems, and the root cause at the bottom won't explain the problem at the top. Read the chain back from the bottom up with "and that's why" between each step: it should make sense both ways.
- **Forcing exactly five.** — Five is a rough guide. Stopping at five when the answer is still a symptom leaves the cause in place; pushing past an actionable cause to "the company's priorities" reaches something nobody on the team can change. Stop at the first cause in the process you can change.
- **A countermeasure that doesn't touch the root cause.** — A good chain followed by an unrelated fix (a reminder at the huddle, a sign on the wall) wastes the work. If the countermeasure doesn't change the thing the root cause names, the problem will come back.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Fishbone diagram** (`fishbone`) — Lays out the possible causes and checks the likeliest at the work. A verified cause from the fishbone is the best place to start a chain.
- **Pareto chart** (`pareto-chart`) — Finds which part of the problem to work on. When there's one obvious cause, the chain can start straight from the top bar.

Downstream:

- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — The A3's root cause section runs a short 5-Whys inline. When the chain needs more room, run it here and carry the root cause and countermeasure into the A3.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Records the countermeasure with its owner and expected result, and whether the root cause comes back after it's closed.
- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — When the root cause is a missing or unclear standard, the countermeasure is often a written standard. A work instruction makes it the way the job is done.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing which problem or verified cause to start from
- Checking each answer where the work happens
- Deciding when the chain has reached a cause the team can change
- Choosing the countermeasure and owning it

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting an answer that doesn't follow from the one before
- Rewording an answer that blames a person into a question about the process
- Suggesting what to check at the work to confirm or rule out an answer

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting the whys and the steps checked at the work
- Flagging a chain that stopped early, ended on a person or has a countermeasure that misses the cause
- Laying out the chain and exporting to xlsx in the house format
