# Consistency audit

Check that what the market sees says the same thing: take a sample of live web pages, ads, sales deck slides and emails, and check each claim against your message pillars, their approved phrasing and proof, and the words to avoid. Drift is flagged, not argued about, every drifted asset gets a fix with an owner and a date, and the score, the most common drift and its most common cause are worked out for you.

Family: Standard work · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A message is agreed once and then repeated by dozens of people in dozens of places: the website, ads, the sales deck, rep emails, case studies, event banners. Each copy drifts a little. A number gets rounded up, a claim gets ahead of its proof, an old slide survives, a pillar the team decided to hold back turns up on the homepage. A consistency audit checks what is actually live. You take a sample of assets as the market sees them today, copy each claim word for word, and check it against the standard: the message pillars, the approved way to say each one, the proof behind it and whether it's cleared for use outside the company, and a short list of words to avoid. Two questions per asset are judgement: does the claim match the approved phrasing, and does it, as worded, have proof cleared for outside use? The rest is mechanical: which avoid-list words appear, whether the pillar is on the list and cleared, and so whether the asset is consistent. The score is the share of assets with nothing flagged; the drift counts show the pattern, which matters more than any one asset. Every drifted asset gets a cause and a fix with one owner and a date; the cause matters most, because fixing a copy without fixing its source just schedules the next drift. The audit is standard work applied to a message: compare what's done with the standard, and fix either the work or the standard.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Your message pillars have been in use for a month or more, across more than one team or channel.
- You suspect claims are running ahead of their proof: rounded numbers, a retired case study, a customer named without approval.
- A proof point has changed status (expired, withdrawn, newly approved) and you want to know where it's still used, or not yet.
- Before a launch, a big event or a new sales hire cohort, when many copies are about to be made from whatever is live.

Not when:

- There is no agreed message yet. Write the pillars and their proof first; there's nothing to audit against.
- You want to know whether the message works with buyers. This checks that it's said consistently, not that it persuades; test the message itself with buyers.
- You want to check how reps say it on calls. Listening to calls needs a different sample and scoring; this audit is for written and published assets.
- The aim is to catch a person out. Drift comes from old copies and missing guidance far more than from anyone ignoring the standard.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Say what you sampled, from where, and how you chose it. Take assets as the market sees them today (the live page, the deck reps actually send, the email that goes out), not the approved versions in a shared folder. Ten to twenty assets across every channel that carries the message is enough to see the pattern.
- **pillars** — Copy the pillars from your message architecture, word for word, with its avoid list. For each, write the approved phrasing (how it should be said), the proof behind it and any limit on its use ("internal only until the customer approves"), and whether it's cleared to use at all. A pillar held back until it's proven is marked No: any asset using it is drift.
- **avoid** — List the words and phrases nobody should use, with why: overclaims ("guarantee", "real-time" when it's 48 hours), retired figures ("30% faster" from an old study), words that belong to a held-back pillar. A word is found anywhere in a claim, whatever its case, even inside a longer word: "guarantee" also finds "guaranteed", and "AI" would find "maintain". Keep each one specific enough not to catch innocent words.
- **assets** — One row per asset: what and where it is ("Sales deck slide 4"), its channel, the pillar it uses (blank if it uses none) and the claim copied word for word. Copy it exactly; a paraphrase hides the drift you're looking for.
- **ratings** — Answer two questions. Matches: does the claim say what the approved phrasing says? Yes, Partly or No; a change of number, scope or certainty is at least Partly. Proof: does the claim, as worded, have proof cleared for use outside the company? "Most teams" from one customer's trial is No, even though the trial is real.
- **fixes** — Under every drifted asset, say why it drifted: old copy still live, the standard never reached the writer, the approved wording doesn't fit, or the claim was pushed past its proof. Then write the fix (what the claim becomes, or that the asset comes down), one owner and a due date. The cause decides where the fix goes: old copy is retired at its source, a standard that never reached the writer is a sharing problem, and wording that doesn't fit sends you back to the standard.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. Each asset is checked for no pillar, a held-back pillar, wording drift, no proof and words to avoid, then for an unfinished check and a drift with no fix owner or date. An asset with nothing flagged is consistent. The score (% consistent), the count of each drift and each cause with the most common one, drifted assets with no cause named, and the results by channel are worked out for you.
- **nextStep** — Write what the pattern says, not just the list of fixes: which drift is most common, which channel or source it comes from, what changes at the source, and when you'll audit a fresh sample. If a pillar keeps drifting the same way, the approved phrasing may be the problem: fix the standard.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' first consistency audit, on 15 June 2026, five weeks after the message architecture it checks against. Ten live assets from six channels: four are consistent, a score of 40%. The most common drift is no proof: 5 of the 10 make a claim that, as worded, has no proof cleared for use outside the company, such as "real-time" alerts in an ad and one customer's trial turned into "most teams" in a rep email. The most common cause is old copy still live (3 of the 6 drifted assets): the homepage still leads with the held-back trust pillar, and both sales deck slides still carry claims taken out of the standard in May. So the fix starts with the deck at its source, not with each copy reps have sent.

## Common mistakes

- **Auditing the approved files instead of what's live.** — The shared folder is usually fine; the drift is in the copies people actually send and publish. Sample the live page, the deck a rep sent last week, the email as it arrives.
- **Paraphrasing the claim.** — Drift lives in the exact words: "within 48 hours" becoming "real-time", "one customer" becoming "most teams". Copy each claim word for word or the audit can't see it.
- **Counting a claim as proven because the proof exists somewhere.** — Proof has to back the claim as worded and be cleared for use outside the company. A real internal figure quoted to a buyer, or one trial stretched to "most teams", is a claim with no proof.
- **Fixing assets one at a time.** — When most of one channel drifts, there is usually one source everyone copies. Fix the source and replace the copies, or the next audit finds the same drift.
- **Blaming the person who wrote the copy.** — Drift comes from old copies, missing guidance and pressure to sound bigger. Fixing the conditions keeps the message straight; blame just hides the next drift.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Message architecture** (`message-architecture`) — The standard the audit checks against: the pillars, the approved wording for each audience, the proof each may use and the words to avoid. Copy them across word for word.
- **Proof point inventory** (`proof-point-inventory`) — Says which proof backs each pillar and whether it may be used outside the company. The audit checks every live claim against it, so the inventory has to be current first.
- **Value hypothesis** (`value-hypothesis`) — The deal-level version of the same message: what this buyer needs and the measures that prove it. Proven value hypotheses are where new pillars and proof come from.
- **Standard work audit** (`standard-work-audit`) — The same discipline applied to how work is done: compare what's done with the standard, find why it differs, and fix the work or the standard.

Downstream:

- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Fixes that take more than a week, like reprinting a banner or replacing a deck everyone has copied, go on the countermeasure log so they're followed to the end.
- **Pareto chart** (`pareto-chart`) — Across several audits, a Pareto chart of drift by kind and by source shows the few causes behind most of it.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding whether a claim matches the approved phrasing
- Deciding whether a claim, as worded, is backed by proof cleared for outside use
- Choosing whether to fix the asset or change the standard

Analysis — AI helps:

- Suggesting which pillar each claim is closest to, for the auditor to confirm
- Spotting claims that change a number, a scope or a level of certainty
- Finding where a retired proof point is still used across many assets

Drudgery — automated:

- Collecting the live assets and copying each claim word for word
- Finding avoid-list words, scoring the sample and counting each drift
- Laying the audit out and exporting it as a workbook
