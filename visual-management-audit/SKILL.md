# Visual management audit

Walk an area and check every visual control in it, from board zones to floor markings and lights: is it up to date, can you tell normal from abnormal in a few seconds from where people stand, and when it shows abnormal, is something being done? Evidence for each, a computed score, and a fix with an owner and a date for every No.

Family: Visual management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

Visual controls are the things in a work area that show at a glance whether everything is as it should be: a board's zones, a marked bay for held parts, a minimum line on a shelf, a light that goes red when a machine stops. They decay quietly. A chart stops being filled in, a sign fades, a light goes red and nobody comes. A visual management audit is a walk through the area to catch that before people stop trusting what they see. For each control you ask three questions, standing where the people who use it stand. Is it up to date: does it show how things are now? Is it readable at a glance: can you tell normal from abnormal in a few seconds from there? And when it shows abnormal, is something being done about it? You write what you saw as evidence, each Yes scores a point, and every No gets a fix with an owner and a date. The third question matters most: a control that shows a problem nobody acts on teaches everyone that it doesn't matter.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A board or set of visual controls has been in use for a few weeks and you want to know whether it still does its job.
- People walk past the board without looking, or problems show on it for days before anyone notices.
- You're a leader taking over an area and want an honest picture of how well it shows its own problems.
- On a regular rhythm, every month or two, so the controls don't decay between redesigns.

Not when:

- The board hasn't gone up yet. Design it first with the visual board design kata; there is nothing to audit.
- You want to know whether the work itself follows its written standard. Watch the work with a standard work audit.
- You want to check whether the results of an improvement held. Use a sustainment audit, which re-measures them against the baseline.
- You can't walk the area at the times the controls are used. An audit from the office checks the paperwork, not the controls.

## How to fill it in

- **area** — Say which area you walked, when, and who walked with you. Go at the times the controls are used, such as the start-of-shift huddle, and on more than one shift if you can: controls often hold up on the shift the supervisor works and slip on the other.
- **checkedAgainst** — Note what the controls are meant to show: the board design with its zones and signals, the standard for the area, the agreed rules ("no part on the red rack without a hold tag"). That's what each control is checked against.
- **controls** — List every visual control in the area, one per row, with where it is. Treat each zone of a board as its own control. Include the ones nobody thinks of as visual management: the restock line on a tray, the marked bay, the light above a door.
- **questions** — Stand where the people who use each control stand, then answer three questions. Up to date: does it show how things are now, not last week? Readable at a glance: could you tell normal from abnormal in a few seconds from there, without walking up or asking? Abnormal acted on: if it shows a problem, is something being done about it? Pick "Nothing abnormal" when it shows normal, so the question can't be tested; it isn't counted either way. Then ask the person who uses the control two things: what does normal look like here, and what do you do when it shows abnormal? A control its user can't explain fails at a glance however clear it looks to you.
- **seen** — Write what you saw: what was on the control, what was missing, and what people did. "Bars filled to Thursday day shift; Thursday's bar below the 60 line with no reason beside it" is evidence. "Chart not maintained" isn't.
- **fix** — For every No, agree a fix with the person who owns that control, then write it with an owner and a due date. Ask why before deciding: a stale chart usually means the numbers don't reach the person who updates it, not that they don't care.
- **score** — Nothing to enter here: each Yes is a point, and the score is the points as a % of the questions that could be answered. The summary also shows how many controls are fully working and the pass rate for each question, so you can see which of the three is weakest. Check flags a control not fully rated, with no evidence, with a No and no fix, or with a fix missing its owner or date.
- **nextStep** — Write what happens next: share the score and the fixes at the next huddle, check each fix on its due date, and book the next walk.

## What good looks like

The example below is the plant manager's walk of the Line 2 paint booth area on Monday 21 September 2026, six weeks after the booth board went up, with the line supervisor at both shift starts. Twelve controls: the board's nine zones, the flush cart's restock line, the red rack for held parts and the booth andon light. The finding that matters: 3 of the 9 controls showing a problem had nothing being done about it. A red hour with no reason, three red first pass yield days with no problem card, and two untagged parts on the red rack were all being walked past. The unplanned stops zone, which had no response when the board was designed, now works: a shift past the 10-minute line was at the huddle the next morning. The rest is smaller. Up to date: 10 of 12 (the hour-by-hour boxes and the maintenance name card had both slipped). Readable at a glance: 11 of 12 (the changeover sign-off circles were printed at half the tested size and look alike from 3 metres). The score is 82% (27 of 33 points), with 7 of the 12 controls fully working. Every No has a fix and an owner; one, filling the sign-off circles in colour, still needs its date, which the check flags.

## Common mistakes

- **Auditing from the office.** — A photo or a spreadsheet tells you whether the board was filled in, not whether anyone can read it from where they stand or whether red leads to anything. Walk it, at the time it's used.
- **Checking only that the board is up to date.** — A board can be perfectly current and still useless: unreadable from the huddle, or showing problems nobody acts on. Ask all three questions of every control; the third usually finds the most.
- **Writing a verdict instead of what you saw.** — "Not maintained" starts an argument; "no bars since Thursday day shift" starts a fix. Evidence is also what lets the next walk show whether things improved.
- **Blaming the person who updates the control.** — Most stale controls are starved of information or time, not of effort. Ask why with the person before writing the fix, and fix the flow of information, not the person.
- **Finding gaps and leaving them.** — A No with no owner and date tells the area that the audit doesn't matter either. Every No gets a fix, an owner and a date, and the fixes get checked on those dates.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Visual board design** (`visual-board-design-guide`) — The board design says what each zone is for, what abnormal looks like and who updates it. That's what this audit checks against.
- **Escalation matrix** (`escalation-matrix`) — Lights, red racks and alarms are visual controls too. The matrix says who should respond and how fast, so you can check whether they did.

Downstream:

- **Leader standard work** (`leader-standard-work`) — Fixes that keep slipping usually need a check in someone's daily routine: "red rack tagged and read at the huddle".
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Track each fix with its owner and date until it is done and holding.
- **Sustainment audit** (`sustainment-audit`) — When a board was put up as part of an improvement, the sustainment audit re-measures that improvement's results; this walk's findings feed its practice checks.
