# Message architecture

Agree one standing message that every rep, the website and the deck use, and that you can back up: a core positioning statement, three or so pillars each tied to a buyer need, the proof points under each pillar with their strength and whether they are cleared for outside use, and the approved words for each audience. The checks are computed: pillars with no proof or only weak proof cleared, variants citing proof that isn't cleared, audiences with no approved words.

Family: Voice of the customer · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A message architecture is the agreed answer to 'what do we say, to whom, and how do we know it's true?'. It has four layers. At the top, one positioning statement: who the product is for, the problem it solves, what it does about it and what it replaces, in two or three plain sentences. Under it, three or so pillars: the few things every buyer in the market should hear, each one answering a buyer need you have evidence for. Under each pillar, the proof points: the measured results, customer statements and cases that back it, each with how strong it is and whether it is cleared for use outside the company, because a customer's own figures often can't be quoted until they say yes. At the bottom, a variant per audience: the pillar a finance approver hears first is not the one a daily user hears first, so each audience gets its approved phrasing, the proof it may cite and the words to avoid. Written down once, it stops each rep inventing their own pitch, and it stops a claim nobody can prove from reaching a buyer.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Reps, marketing and customer success describe the product differently, and buyers hear several versions of the story.
- You have proof from real customers and want to turn it into the message everyone uses, with the limits on each claim written down.
- You are launching into a new market or to a new buyer role and need approved words before the first campaign.
- Someone has quoted a customer's number in public without asking, and you want that not to happen again.

Not when:

- You don't yet know what these buyers need. Map their jobs, pains and gains first with a value proposition map; pillars invented before that are guesses.
- You are preparing for one deal. A value hypothesis or a business case for that buyer fits better than a standing message.
- You have no proof at all yet. Write the pillars as hypotheses to test, not as a message to roll out.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Name the product and the market this is the standing message for: one group of buyers. If two markets need different pillars, they need two architectures. Say what it was built from.
- **positioning** — Write two or three plain sentences: who it is for, the problem they have, what the product does about it, and what it replaces. Leave out adjectives you couldn't defend to a sceptical buyer. Every pillar should fit under it; if one doesn't, either the pillar or the statement is wrong.
- **pillars** — Pick three or so: more and nobody remembers them. Give each a short name, a one- or two-sentence supporting message, and the buyer need it answers, with where buyers said it. A pillar that answers no need you can point to is a slogan; the sheet flags it.
- **proofs** — List each proof point once: the claim with its number, the pillar it proves, its source (where, when, how many customers), its strength, and its clearance. Strong means measured and repeated across customers; moderate, measured but at one customer; weak, one person's account rather than a measurement, second-hand or old. Mark it Cleared only when the customer, or whoever owns it, has agreed it can be used outside the company; until then it is Awaiting approval or Internal only.
- **audiences** — List the roles you talk to. For each, pick the pillar they hear first, write the approved phrasing in words that role would use, cite up to two proof points by id, and list the words to avoid (claims you can't back, jargon, promises). Leave the phrasing blank until it is agreed; the sheet will show the audience has no variant.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the architecture is checked for you. Each pillar counts its proof and how much is cleared, and flags a pillar with no buyer need, no proof, no proof cleared for use, or cleared proof that is all weak: weak proof can support a pillar but not carry it, so the pillar doesn't count as ready. Each proof point is flagged if it names no pillar or has no source. Each audience is flagged if it has no approved phrasing, cites an id not on the list, or cites proof that is internal only or awaiting approval.
- **nextStep** — Say what changes now: which variant is fixed, whose approval is being asked for, which pillar stays out of external copy until it has proof. Name who and by when, and one owner for the architecture with the date it is next reviewed: when a proof point changes status, and at least each quarter, starting with a consistency audit of what is live. Then share the sheet with everyone who talks to buyers.

## Common mistakes

- **Pillars that are adjectives.** — 'Fast, easy, powerful' says nothing a buyer can check, and every competitor says it too. A pillar names the need it answers and has proof underneath; if you can't say which proof, it isn't a pillar yet.
- **Using a customer's figure before they have agreed.** — A customer's own numbers are often the strongest proof you have, and the quickest way to lose that customer if they turn up in a deck without permission. Mark clearance honestly and keep anything not cleared out of every external variant.
- **One message for every audience.** — A finance approver and a daily user care about different things. Same pillars, different lead, different words: each audience needs its own approved variant, or reps will write one on the spot.
- **Too many pillars.** — Six pillars means nobody remembers any of them, and the proof gets spread thin. Three or so, each with real proof, beat a long list where half are hopes.
- **Writing it once and never checking it.** — Proof ages, approvals arrive, customers leave. Look at the clearance and the checks whenever proof changes, and listen to what reps actually say: a message nobody uses is not the message.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Value proposition map** (`value-proposition-map`) — The map shows which buyer needs your offer answers with proof. Those top pains and gains become the pillars; the gaps it flags stay out of the message until they are closed.
- **Value hypothesis** (`value-hypothesis`) — Value worked out for single buyers, in their own numbers, is where most proof points start. A result proven in several deals is ready to become a pillar's proof.
- **Proof point inventory** (`proof-point-inventory`) — The inventory checks each proof point at its source and records whether it may be used outside the company. Take strength and clearance from it rather than from memory.

Downstream:

- **Business case builder** (`business-case-builder`) — A pillar about value in money becomes real deal by deal: the business case puts the buyer's own figures behind it, where a reference figure from another customer can't go.
- **Consistency audit** (`consistency-audit`) — Once the message is agreed, the audit samples live web pages, ads, deck slides and emails, checks each claim against the pillars and flags where it drifts.
- **Message testing log** (`message-testing-log`) — Each pillar is a bet that buyers care. The testing log tries its wording with real buyers, a prediction written first, and records what to adopt, keep or drop.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing the few pillars that matter to these buyers
- Agreeing the words each audience will hear
- Deciding when a proof point is strong enough to lead with

Analysis — AI helps:

- Checking that each variant only cites cleared proof and avoids the banned words
- Drafting audience variants from the pillars and proof for the team to edit
- Spotting claims in decks and web copy that match no proof point

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting proof and clearance for every pillar
- Keeping ids, links and clearance up to date as proof changes
- Laying the architecture out and exporting it as a workbook
