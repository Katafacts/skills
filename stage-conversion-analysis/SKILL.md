# Stage conversion analysis

How many deals enter each funnel stage and how many move on: each stage's conversion, the overall win rate, which losses cost the most, and whether a change is real or ordinary variation.

Family: Measurement · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A deal that does not move out of a funnel stage is a loss the same way a unit failing inspection is: stage conversion is first-pass yield applied to a sales pipeline. Follow one cohort of deals (say, everything qualified in one quarter) through to won or lost, and for each stage count the deals that entered and the deals that moved on. From that the tool works out each stage's conversion, the overall win rate from first stage to won (for a cohort, simply deals won ÷ deals in), and the stage with the lowest conversion. Two things a seasoned eye adds. A lost deal is not automatically a defect: turning away a poor fit at the first call is quality at the source, and it costs a couple of hours; a deal lost after the proposal has cost ten times that, so read the lost reasons before deciding which loss to fix. And a few dozen deals per stage swing by several points from cohort to cohort by chance alone, so the tool checks each change against that normal swing before you call it a drop.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You can pull real per-stage counts from the CRM — how many deals entered a stage and how many advanced to the next one — for a bounded segment, not the whole blended pipeline.
- You want to know which stage loses deals, and which of those losses cost the most rep time, not just an overall win rate.
- Leads come in but few turn into deals, and you need to find the stage where most of them stop moving.

Not when:

- You only have an overall win rate with no stage-level breakdown — that's a start, but it can't tell you which stage to fix.
- You want time-based findings — lead time, flow efficiency, which stage uses the most rep time — that's the sales funnel value stream map.
- The pipeline segment blends deal types with genuinely different natural conversion patterns — scope down to one comparable segment first, same discipline as every other VSM-family artifact.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Name the deal source or segment and the start and end stage — typically sales-qualified lead to closed-won or closed-lost. Keep to one comparable segment; a blended pipeline produces a conversion number that describes neither.
- **stages** — Pick one cohort, such as the deals that became sales-qualified in one quarter, and wait until they have all been won or lost. List each stage in order with how many of that cohort's deals entered it and how many moved on. Take the counts from the CRM's stage history, not a gut sense that "about half fall through."
- **rolledYield** — Calculated from the stage counts — don't type it. Check each stage's conversion against your counts and that the overall win rate matches deals won ÷ deals in at the first stage; if it does not, the counts are not one cohort. Then read each change from the last cohort against its normal swing (about two standard errors: 2 × √(p × (100 − p) ÷ deals in)). At 25 deals a stage that is up to about ±20 points, the outer edge of what chance alone does; a change inside it is not yet a drop. The earlier cohort was counted on as few deals, so its rate is noisy too: where you can, compare against the earlier cohorts pooled, and count that pool's noise as well, 2 × √(p × (100 − p) × (1 ÷ new deals + 1 ÷ pooled deals)). On a small team, read the late-stage losses deal by deal rather than as a rate.
- **narrative** — Read the lost reasons and last call notes on the lost deals, stage by stage, and say which losses should have happened (poor fits turned away early) and which cost the most (deals lost late, after the proposal). Name the costly loss, not just the lowest conversion. If nobody has checked a reason yet, say it's still a guess.
- **countermeasures** — List two or three, ranked by impact and effort, each with an owner and a date. Aim them at the losses the lost reasons show are costly and avoidable, often by moving the check earlier so a deal that will be lost is lost cheaply, not at whichever stage converts lowest.

## What good looks like

The example below follows the 25 deals Beacon Analytics' outbound mid-market pod qualified in October to December 2025 through five stages to won or lost. Qualified to discovery has the lowest conversion (14 of 25, 56%) and loses the most deals (11), but the lost reasons show 9 were poor fits closed at the first call, the cheap place to lose a deal. The costly losses are the 2 between proposal and negotiation, after about 15 rep hours each, both deals with only one contact. Every stage is a few points below the cohort before, but each change sits inside its normal swing, and the cohort before was just as small, so its rates are noisy too. The overall win rate is read against the two earlier cohorts pooled: 3 of 25 (12%) against 8 of 48 (16.67%), a change of 4.67 points against a swing of ±18.38, counting the noise in both the new cohort and the pooled 48. So the countermeasures hold the deal A3's discovery-to-demo gate, read the late losses deal by deal and keep plotting cohorts rather than declaring a drop.

## Common mistakes

- **Using period counts instead of following a cohort** — Deals that entered a stage this quarter are not the same deals that moved on this quarter: some came from last quarter's pipeline, some will close next quarter. Dividing one quarter's counts gives conversions that can top 100% or swing with timing alone. Follow one cohort from first stage to won or lost.
- **Estimating stage counts from memory instead of pulling the CRM's stage history** — Reps remember the memorable deals, not the typical ones. The stage-transition counts in the CRM are the ground truth.
- **Fixing the lowest-conversion stage without asking whether those losses should have happened earlier** — Early losses are often the funnel doing its job: a poor fit turned away at the first call costs two hours. The costly losses are late ones, after a demo and a proposal. Read the lost reasons, and aim at the losses that should have happened sooner.
- **Calling a change from two or three cohorts** — A stage that 25 deals pass through can move by up to about 20 points from cohort to cohort by chance alone, and the earlier cohort's rate is as noisy as the new one. Check each change against its normal swing, plot cohort win rates on a run chart, and act on a change beyond the swing or a run that keeps moving one way.
