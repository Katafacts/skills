# Error-proofing

Stop a mistake that keeps reaching the customer by changing the work, not by asking people to be careful. For each error, write what the person did and what it produced, where it is caught today, and the strongest practical device: make it impossible, stop the work at the step, or warn. Then prove the device by making the mistake on purpose. The sheet ranks each control, counts the errors stopped and the devices not yet proven, and compares defects reaching the customer before and after.

Family: Quality at source · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

People make mistakes. A tired, skilled, careful person will still, now and then, pick the wrong label, leave a part on the trolley or skip a step when interrupted. Error-proofing accepts that and changes the work so the mistake can't turn into something the customer receives. It starts by pulling apart two things that usually get one name. The error is what the person did: took the label from the top of the tray. The defect is what it produced: a carton addressed to the wrong customer. Errors can't be wished away; defects can be stopped, if the error is caught before the work moves on. So for each error you ask where it can be caught, and you climb a ladder. Best, make the error impossible: a part that only fits one way, a printer that won't print the next label until this order is closed. Next, stop the work at the step: the order won't close until the label and the packing list scan as a match. Then, warn at the step: a light, beep or red screen set off by the error itself. A colour code or a tag helps the eye but still relies on attention. Below those come catching it at a later step, which is late and costly, and relying on attention ("be careful", "check by eye"), which is what the error already got past. The device is often small and cheap: a fixture, a lock in the sequence, a count, a sensor, a scan. Then comes the step most people skip: prove it. Make the mistake on purpose and see the device catch it, and keep trying again on a schedule, because devices wear out, get unplugged and get worked around. The idea is old and widely practised, sometimes called mistake-proofing, or by its Japanese name, poka-yoke.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- The same kind of mistake keeps reaching the customer or the next step: wrong part, wrong label, a step missed, a count short.
- A root cause analysis ends at "the person made a mistake" and the countermeasure on the table is retraining or a reminder.
- A failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) has scored a failure high and its current control is "operator checks".
- A first-pass yield tracker shows one step losing units to errors rather than to the machine or the material.
- A new process or product is about to start and you can design the obvious errors out before they happen.

Not when:

- You don't know yet what the error is, only the defect. Go and see it happen, or ask why until you reach what the person did; a device for the wrong error is wasted.
- The defect comes from variation in a machine or material, not from something a person did or missed. Work the cause with a fishbone and the 5-Whys.
- The "mistake" is people not following a method they were never taught. Write the work instruction and train it first; then error-proof the steps that still go wrong.
- It happens once a year and costs little. Put the effort where the escapes are.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Name the work the sheet covers, from where to where, and say exactly what counts as a defect reaching the customer (or the next step, if that is where you count). Name the record you count from, such as a returns log or the next step's reject list, and the before period. Count from a record, not from memory.
- **errors** — Write one row per mistake, starting from the defects that really reached the customer. Give the step where it happens, then the error as an action someone took ("took a carton from the wrong lane"), then the defect it produced ("part packed in a carton too big; arrives scuffed"). If you can only write the defect, you don't know the error yet: watch the step, or ask the person who made it, without blame. Keep to the few errors behind most of the escapes.
- **escapes** — Count the defects that reached the customer for each error in the before period. Once the devices are in, count again over the after period, from the same record, the same way. The sheet compares them a week, so periods of different lengths compare fairly. Leave the after count blank until you have one.
- **today** — Write what catches the error today and pick its level from the ladder: prevents the error, stops the work at the step, warns at the step, caught at a later step, relies on attention, nothing catches it. Be honest. A person reading a label against a list is relying on attention, however conscientious they are; so is a final check by eye.
- **device** — Describe the device so someone could build it, pick its type (shape or fixture, sequence lock, count or checklist, sensor or scan, colour or visual) and the level it reaches. Climb as high as is practical: first ask whether the error can be made impossible; then whether the work can stop at the step until it's put right; a warning only if neither is practical. Catch it at the step where it happens, not downstream. Design it with the people who do the work; they know the workarounds. Cheap and simple beats clever: a fixture, a lock in the sequence or a scan often does it. Name an owner and a date.
- **proof** — Make the mistake on purpose and write what happened: the wrong label stuck on, the order packed one part short, the held part reached for. Try the hardest case: the lightest part on the scale, the two cartons that look most alike. A device nobody has tried to beat is a hope, not a control. Then set how often someone tries again (each shift, daily, weekly) and put both the device and its check on the standard work, so the next person keeps them and an audit can check them.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For each error the sheet counts how many rungs the device climbs and flags a device that relies on attention ("be careful", "by eye", "double-check", "remind" and similar words count too), one no stronger than today, a failed or missing test, no check frequency, a device not on the standard work, and defects still reaching the customer. The summary gives the errors prevented or stopped today and with the devices, the devices not yet proven, and defects reaching the customer before and after, a week.
- **findings** — Write what the sheet shows and what happens next: whether escapes fell, which device is weakest or unproven, who tests or steps it up by when, and when you'll count again. A device that still lets defects through is the next row to work, not a reason to add an inspection.

## What good looks like

The example below is the Line 2 packing bench on 27 October 2026, after the standard work chart had timed the packer's new bench in August. Dispatch's returns and complaints log showed 14 defects reaching customers in the six weeks to 11 September, 2.3 a week, from four errors: the next order's label, printed ahead under the new start-the-print-and-go method, stuck on this order's carton; a carton taped shut with a part still on the trolley; the wrong size of carton taken; and a held part picked for an order. All four relied on someone noticing and none was stopped at the step. Three devices climb three or four rungs: the label and packing list must scan as a match before the order closes and the next label prints (a sequence lock that stops the work), a check-weigh that won't close a light order (stops), and a locked hold cage the packer has no key to (prevents, up from a red hold tag). The fourth, colour-matched cartons, helps the eye but still relies on attention: no step up. That is 3 of 4 errors prevented or stopped, 75%. Those three were proven on 24 September by making the mistake on purpose, the scale with the lightest part Line 2 paints, and each is tried again every shift or week. Over the next four weeks one defect reached a customer, 0.3 a week, 89% fewer. The checks point straight at it: the colour-matched cartons rely on attention, are no stronger than the lane labels, nobody has tried to beat them, and they aren't on the standard work. So the next step is a stop: Dev adds a size barcode to each carton lane and puts it in the close-by-scan, so the order won't close in the wrong carton (a stop, using the device already proven on error 1). And a lesson: a speed-up created the label error, so check new methods for the errors they open up.

## Common mistakes

- **Settling for "be careful", retraining or a reminder.** — The person already knew; attention is what the error got past. Retraining a skilled person after a slip teaches nothing new and tells everyone the problem was them. Change the work so the slip can't become a defect.
- **Adding an inspection at the end.** — A check further down the line finds the defect after the work is done, costs rework, and is itself a check by eye that misses on busy days. Catch the error at the step where it happens, or before it.
- **Writing the defect and calling it the error.** — "Wrong label on carton" is what came out; the error is what the person did (took the top label from the tray, which was the next order's). A device can only act on the action. Until you can name it, go and watch.
- **Choosing a warning when the work could stop.** — A light or a beep is only as good as the response to it, and people learn to ignore alarms. If the step can refuse to finish until the error is put right, that is worth more than a louder warning.
- **Never testing the device.** — Sensors drift, scales go out of tolerance, locks get propped open, and a device that has never been beaten on purpose may never have worked. Make the mistake deliberately before you count it, and keep doing it on a schedule.
- **A device that stops good work too.** — If it raises false alarms or slows every cycle, people will find a way round it, and then it protects nothing. Test it on good work as well as bad, and fix the nuisance before it gets bypassed.
- **Leaving the device off the standard work.** — If the device and its check aren't in the work instruction, the next person on the job won't know it's there, and nobody will notice when it stops being checked.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **5-Whys** (`five-whys`) — When the chain ends at "the person made a mistake", keep going: ask why the process let that mistake through. Where it lands, at a step with no way to catch the error, is where this sheet starts.
- **Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)** (`fmea`) — The analysis ranks which failures matter; a high score whose current control is "operator checks" is a row for this sheet. Its detection score should improve once the device is proven.
- **First-pass yield tracker** (`first-pass-yield-tracker`) — Shows which step loses the most units first time. When those losses are human errors rather than machine or material problems, error-proof that step, then watch its yield.

Downstream:

- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — The device and its check go into the instruction as key points, with the reason: "scan the label and packing list together; the order won't close if they don't match".
- **Standard work audit** (`standard-work-audit`) — The audit checks the device is still in place and that its each-shift or weekly test is really being done, not just written down.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Each device goes on the log with its owner and date, and is closed only when its after count shows the defect has stopped reaching the customer.
