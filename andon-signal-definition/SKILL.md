# Andon signal definition

Agree what each andon signal means where the work is done: when to pull it, who comes and within how many minutes, what they do first, when it goes up and to whom, whether work stops and how it restarts. Then log every pull and see the response times against the promise. Checked for you: signals with no responder or no time, and pulls answered over target.

Family: Visual management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

An andon is a signal anyone at the point of work can pull the moment something isn't right: a cord, a button or a light at the station. It works only if the pull is answered, quickly and every time, so the signal is defined before it's needed. For each signal the team agrees when to pull it, in terms anyone at the station can see ("a part with runs at unload", not "a quality issue"); who comes, by name or rota; within how many minutes; what they do first when they get there; after how many minutes it goes up and to whom; whether work keeps going or stops; and, if it stops, who restarts it and after what check. Then every pull goes in a log with the time it was pulled and the time someone arrived. The log is where the promise meets the shop floor: it shows which signals are answered on time, and which ones people have learned not to bother with.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- People spot problems at the station but have no quick, agreed way to call for help.
- There is an andon cord or light, but nobody is sure who answers it, or how fast.
- Pulls go unanswered, or are answered slowly on some shifts, and people have stopped pulling.
- You're starting an andon on a line or cell and want it defined and tested before it goes live.

Not when:

- The problem can't wait even the few minutes a response takes, such as a fire or a medical emergency. Those follow the site's emergency plan, not an andon.
- You need the ladder for problems the first responder can't close. That is the escalation matrix; the andon is the first call.
- You want to find why the same pull keeps happening. Count the log by cause, then use a Pareto chart or a 5-Whys.

## How to fill it in

- **area** — Say where the signals work (the stations and shifts), who can pull them (everyone at the station, including cover and new starters, with no need to explain first) and how a pull is made and seen: which cord or button, what lights up or sounds, and where.
- **signals** — List the signals, three or four at most, each with a colour and a condition anyone at the station can see for when to pull it. Keep safety and "the line has stopped" apart from "I need help", because they need different people and different speeds.
- **response** — For each signal, write who comes: the role, and the name or where the rota is posted, on every shift the signal is live. Then the minutes they must be there within, counted from the pull, and the first thing they do when they arrive. A role with no name on second shift is a pull nobody answers.
- **stop** — Say after how many minutes the problem goes up, and to whom, if it isn't fixed; that time should come after the responder is due. Then whether work keeps going, stops at the end of the cycle or stops now; for a stop, who restarts the line and after what check.
- **pulls** — Whoever answers a pull writes it in the log before the end of the shift: the date, the station, the signal, the clock time it was pulled, what happened, when they arrived, who they were and what they did. The response time and the target are worked out for you.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. Each signal is checked for a missing trigger, no one to come or no name, no response time, no first action, a step up with no time or no person (or before the responder is due), and a stop with no restart rule; its pulls and pulls over target are counted from the log. Each pull is checked for a signal that isn't in the list, no arrival time, and a response over target.
- **nextStep** — Write what you'll change, by when and who, and when you'll look at the log again. Look for the pattern behind slow answers (a shift, a station, a responder covering two areas) and change the definition, not just ask people to hurry.

## What good looks like

The example below is the Line 2 paint booth's four signals and the week of pulls from 20 to 24 July 2026, reviewed by the line supervisor on 27 July. Red for safety and red for a stopped booth both stop work and say who restarts it; amber is help at the station; blue is material. The responders agree with the escalation matrix, and the pulls with the rest of that week, from the booth door that stopped closing on 20 July and the near miss and runs on 21 July to the mixing room pump that failed on 22 July. Eleven pulls were logged, all timed, and 8 were answered within target. The checks find one gap in the definitions and show its cost: the blue material signal names a role, material handler, but no one to fill it, and both blue pulls, both on second shift, were over target, 40 and 25 minutes against 15, while the shift lead left the booth to fetch the material himself. One stopped-booth pull took 7 minutes against 5. The next step names a second-shift material handler by 31 July and asks maintenance why the technician was late.

## Common mistakes

- **A trigger nobody at the station can judge, like "a quality issue".** — If people have to decide whether it's serious enough, they hesitate and don't pull. Write the thing they can see: runs, dust, a test card that isn't clean.
- **Naming a role but no one to fill it on every shift.** — In the example the material signal had no one on second shift, and both pulls there waited far past the target. A pull nobody answers teaches people to stop pulling.
- **Asking the person who pulled to explain or justify it.** — Thank them and fix the problem. Every pull is a problem found early; a blamed pull is the last one you'll get.
- **Stopping the line with no agreed way to restart it.** — Without a named person and a check (the first part off against the limit sample) the restart is a guess, and the same fault runs again.
- **Not logging pulls, or not reading the log.** — The definition is a promise; the log is the only way to see whether it's kept, and where it isn't.

## What it connects to

Downstream:

- **Escalation matrix** (`escalation-matrix`) — When the first responder can't close the problem, the escalation matrix's ladder takes over; the andon's step-up time should match it.
- **Shift handover** (`shift-handover`) — Pulls answered but not closed during the shift are handed over as open problems.
- **Performance grid** (`performance-grid`) — The andon gives the signal in the moment; the grid shows the shift's results once it's over, and the log's pulls explain its reds.
- **Pareto chart** (`pareto-chart`) — Count a month of pulls by cause to see which few problems most of them come from.
- **Leader standard work** (`leader-standard-work`) — Reading the andon log is a natural weekly task in the supervisor's routine.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing the signals and what counts as a reason to pull
- Deciding who answers and how fast, and agreeing it with them
- Deciding when the line stops and who restarts it

Analysis — AI helps:

- Timing every pull against its target and finding the slow ones
- Spotting the pattern behind slow answers: a shift, a station, a missing name
- Counting pulls by signal and cause over weeks

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out response times from the log's clock times
- Counting each signal's pulls and misses
- Laying out the definitions and the log and exporting them as a workbook
