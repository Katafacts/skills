# True north statement

Write the direction you steer by as a few dimensions of an ideal state (customer, quality, flow, people, cost), each with a measure, where it stands today and the ideal, set at the limit so it can never be fully reached. Then check that this year's plan objectives each move one, and see which dimensions nothing this year moves.

Family: Hoshin Kanri · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A true north statement is the direction an organisation steers by over many years, written down so it can be checked. It starts with one sentence about what the organisation would be like at its best, then breaks that into a few dimensions that would all be true at the ideal: usually something for the customer, for quality, for how work flows, for people, and for cost. Each dimension gets the ideal in words, one measure, where that measure stands today, and the measure's value at the ideal. The ideal is set at the limit, such as every order on time, every part right first time, an order never waiting, so it can't be fully reached. That is the point: a target is met and forgotten, a true north always shows the next step. Long-term and annual objectives are milestones on the way, so the statement ends with a check. Each of this year's plan objectives should move at least one dimension, and any dimension nothing this year moves should be a choice someone made, not an accident.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're about to set or review a yearly plan and want its objectives anchored to something that doesn't change with each budget.
- Your plan's objectives are all about one thing (usually output or cost) and you suspect quality, people or the customer have dropped out of sight.
- A mission statement exists but nobody can say whether the organisation is getting closer to it. Measures and an ideal turn it into a direction you can check.
- A long-term objective has been hit and the team is asking "what now?". A true north shows how far there still is to go.

Not when:

- You need this year's objectives, targets and owners. That is the plan itself, for example a hoshin kanri X-matrix; the true north sits above it.
- You need to know whether a plan's objectives have reached the people who deliver them. That is a cascade map.
- Nobody with the authority to set long-term direction is in the room. A true north written by one team for an organisation it doesn't lead won't be used.

## How to fill it in

- **statement** — Write one sentence about what the organisation would be like at its best, in terms its customers would recognise: "the shop customers call first for time-sensitive custom orders", not "a world-class operation".
- **dimensions** — Name three to five dimensions that would all be true at that ideal, one or two words each. Customer, quality, flow, people and cost cover most organisations. Each should still matter in ten years; if one only matters this year, it's an objective.
- **ideal** — For each dimension, write the ideal in words, at the limit: every order arrives the day it's needed; every part right the first time; an order is only ever being worked on; nothing paid for is thrown away. If your plan could reach it in a few years, push it further.
- **numbers** — Pick one measure for each dimension, one you already record, and give today's value and the measure's value at the ideal (100%, zero, or the hands-on work time for a lead time). Say which way is better. If your three-to-five-year plan has a target for that measure, add it as the long-term target; leave it blank if not.
- **owner** — Name one person who reports each dimension when the plan is reviewed. They don't deliver it alone; they make sure it's looked at.
- **objectives** — Name the plan you're checking, then copy each of this year's objectives as written on it and say which dimension it moves, with one line on how. Use the dimension's name exactly; in the workbook it's a dropdown.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For each dimension the statement works out the gap from today to the ideal, the share of that gap the long-term target closes, and how many of this year's objectives move it. It flags a missing measure, value or owner, today already at the ideal, and a long-term target that reaches the ideal (so the ideal is really a target) or moves away from it. Each objective that moves no dimension is flagged, and the dimensions nothing this year moves are listed.
- **nextStep** — Write what was agreed: what happens to each dimension nothing this year moves (held on purpose, or a metric or objective added), any ideal that was pushed further, and when the statement is next reviewed, usually with next year's plan.

## What good looks like

The example below is Northwind Fabrication's true north, reviewed by the plant manager on 27 October 2026, the day after the 2027 X-matrix was agreed, with values from January to September 2026. Five dimensions: customer (86% of shipments on time, ideal 100%), quality (97% of custom orders pass inspection first time, ideal 100%), flow (an 18-day average lead time against an ideal of about 2 days, the hands-on work time on the order routings), people (56% of key operations, 5 of 9, have two trained people, ideal 100%) and cost (3.1% lost to scrap and rework, ideal zero). Only flow has a long-term target, 7 days by 2029; the gap to the ideal is 16 days and that target closes 69% of it. All three of the plan's objectives move a dimension: the lead-time objective moves flow, and the late-shipment and repeat-customer objectives both move customer. So the plan moves 2 of the 5 dimensions, and nothing this year moves quality, people or cost. No checks are flagged. The team agreed to hold quality and cost on purpose and review them monthly, but not people: the lead-time objective rests on hiring a second machinist and cross-training two fabricators, so key operations with two trained people goes onto the X-matrix as a metric.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing targets and calling them a true north.** — "Lead time of 7 days" is a goal for 2029. Once it's met, nothing points further. Set the ideal at the limit (no waiting at all) and make 7 days a milestone toward it; the workbook flags a long-term target that reaches the ideal.
- **Too many dimensions.** — Ten dimensions is a list of everything that matters, and nobody steers by a list. Three to five that everyone can name from memory do more.
- **A dimension with no measure.** — "Empowered people" can't tell anyone whether this year was better than last. Pick one measure you already record, even an imperfect one, and improve the measure later.
- **Letting the plan quietly drop a dimension.** — Plans pull toward output and cost. A year where nothing moves people or quality can be the right call, but decide it out loud; the statement lists the dimensions nothing moves so the choice is made on purpose.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis** (`swot-analysis`) — An evidenced look at where the organisation stands gives today's values, and often shows which dimension is furthest from its ideal.

Downstream:

- **Hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`hoshin-kanri`) — The plan's long-term and annual objectives are milestones toward the true north; its one-line true north is the statement's sentence.
- **Cascade map** (`cascade-map`) — Once the plan's objectives are checked against the true north, the cascade map deploys them to the units and teams that deliver them.
- **Key performance indicator (KPI) tree** (`kpi-tree`) — A dimension's measure can be broken into the measures that make it, so each team sees which number it moves.
- **Performance board** (`performance-board`) — Where each dimension's measure is tracked month by month, so the review sees whether the organisation is getting closer.
