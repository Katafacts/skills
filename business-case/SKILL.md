# Business case

The cost of the status quo, what the fix actually costs, and the quantified value it returns — with payback period and 3-year return on investment (ROI) computed from those numbers, not asserted, and every value driver's basis and confidence named plainly.

Family: Kaizen events · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A business case puts a real number on why an investment is worth making — the cost of the status quo, what the fix actually costs, and the quantified value it returns, with payback period and 3-year ROI computed from those numbers rather than asserted. Every value driver carries how the number was derived and how confident you actually are in it — a driver with no real basis is named as a gap, not hidden behind a confident-sounding total. It's the resourcing step that naturally comes before a kaizen event charter: before committing a team's time to an improvement, this is what earns that commitment.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're proposing a real investment — capital, a new system, dedicated time — and need more than 'this seems worth it' to get it approved.
- You want to know honestly which parts of the case are backed by real data and which are still an estimate worth flagging.
- The investment is heading toward a decision-maker who will ask how you got these numbers, not just what they add up to.

Not when:

- The investment is trivial enough that building a formal case costs more time than the decision itself is worth — use judgement, not process, for a small call.
- You don't have any real data yet, just a hunch that something would help — go gather evidence first (a go-see walk, a VOC pass), or every driver in this case will be a guess dressed up as a number.
- You're building the countermeasure itself, not funding it — that's an A3's job; a business case exists to justify committing resources, not to solve the problem directly.

## How to fill it in

- **costOfInaction** — Write what the current state keeps costing, in money, time or risk, and why acting now matters more than later. Use figures from the system of record or a count you made yourself, not an impression.
- **investment** — List what the fix costs, line by line, split into one-time and ongoing costs. Get the figures from quotes, finance or the people who'll do the work, and include internal staff time — it's the cost most often left out.
- **valueDrivers** — For each way the proposal pays back — time saved, cost removed, errors avoided — write the number, how you worked it out, and how confident you are in it. Base each one on a measurement or a record; if a driver rests on an estimate, mark it low confidence rather than leaving it out.
- **roiSummary** — Calculated from the investment and value drivers — don't type it; check the payback and return make sense against the totals above, and if they look too good, recheck your least-confident driver.
- **assumptions** — List what has to stay true for these numbers to hold — volumes, prices, adoption, timing. Write each one so someone could check it; whoever approves the case will test the weakest one first.
- **narrative** — Write a short summary of the case in plain words: what doing nothing costs, what you're asking for, and the two or three drivers that carry the value. Point to the numbers on the sheet rather than restating them, so the words and the figures never disagree.
- **nextSteps** — List what needs firming up before this case goes to whoever approves it — usually the least-confident drivers — ranked by impact and effort, each with an owner and a date.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a value driver with no real basis behind it.** — A number with nothing behind it gets challenged the moment someone asks how you got it — and once one number falls apart, the reader stops trusting the rest of the case too.
- **Padding the case with low-confidence drivers to inflate the total.** — The honest total, with its weak points named, survives scrutiny. A total padded with shaky numbers just gives a skeptical reviewer a reason to distrust the whole case.
- **Skipping the cost of inaction and only presenting the upside.** — A case that only shows what you gain, never what continuing to wait already costs, is missing half the argument for acting now instead of later.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis** (`swot-analysis`) — The SWOT's evidence and the action being funded are the starting point for the case.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — A countermeasure identified in an A3 that needs real resourcing is exactly what turns into a business case — the A3 finds the fix, this justifies committing to it.

Downstream:

- **Kaizen event charter** (`improvement-event-charter`) — Once a business case earns the investment, a kaizen event charter is where the actual work gets planned and resourced day to day.
