# Go-see interview protocol

Watch a customer do their real work in their own setting, then ask about what you saw. A short list of what to watch for, a timed observation record (what they did, what they used, workarounds, what surprised you), the questions asked after watching with answers in their words, quotes worth keeping, and follow-ups with an owner and a date. The checks are computed: minutes watched, watch-for items never noted, opinions written as facts, and questions about the future instead of the work.

Family: Voice of the customer · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A go-see interview is a visit to watch someone do their real work, where they normally do it, before you ask them anything. People describe their work the way it is meant to go; watching shows the way it goes, including the workarounds that have become so normal nobody mentions them. The protocol has four parts. A short list of what to watch for, decided before you go. An observation record written while you watch: the clock time, what they did, what they used, any workaround and anything that surprised you. Then, once the work is done, the questions you ask about what you saw, each tied to an observation, with the answer in their words. And the follow-ups, each with one owner and a date. The order matters: watch first, ask after. A question asked in the middle of the work changes the work, and a question asked without having watched gets you the tidy version.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You need to know how a customer really does a piece of work before you design for it, sell against it or count its cost.
- Someone has given you an estimate of how long their work takes or how often something goes wrong, and you want to see it rather than take it on trust.
- Interviews keep producing the same polite answers and you suspect the real friction is in workarounds nobody thinks to mention.
- You can be there while the work happens: at their desk, on their site, on a shared screen while they work for real.

Not when:

- You can't watch the work happen, or the work is a one-off decision rather than something they do. Plan a conversation with the customer interview guide instead.
- You want to know why a deal was won or lost. That's a decision already made; use the win-loss interview guide.
- You want to check your own team's work against a standard. Walk it with a go-see protocol; this protocol is for learning from a customer, not auditing them.
- Your presence would stop the real work happening (a sensitive call, a regulated step). Ask to watch a different instance, or note that the gap exists rather than pretending you saw it.

## How to fill it in

- **visit** — Write who you'll watch (their role and organisation), where (their own place of work, not a meeting room) and the one thing you want to learn, such as how long a weekly report really takes to build. Arrange to come when the work actually happens, and ask them to do it exactly as they would if you weren't there.
- **watchFor** — Three to five things to watch for, in plain words: where the time goes, how a problem gets noticed, anything kept or fixed outside the system, the checks they make before trusting a number. The list focuses your eyes; it doesn't script the visit. If something important happens that isn't on it, record it anyway.
- **observations** — Watch without interrupting. For each thing they do, write the clock time and what they did, as a fact: "pasted last week's notes back in, matching deal names by eye", not "struggled with the notes". Write what they used (the report, the spreadsheet, the chat, the folder). Where they work around the system or the official way, say how in the workaround column. Where something surprised you, write it down: surprises are where your picture of their work was wrong. Number each row with the watch-for item it speaks to.
- **questions** — Once the work is done, ask about what you saw, one observation at a time, and note its number: "At 09:50 you corrected the amount in the spreadsheet but not in the customer relationship management (CRM) system. Walk me through that." Ask about the last time something happened, not what they would do if things were different; people are poor at predicting their own behaviour. Write the answer in their words, in quotation marks.
- **quotes** — Keep the few things said in their own words that you'll want to repeat later, whether said while working or in an answer, with when and what about. Two good quotes are worth more than ten; a quote without its context can't be used honestly.
- **followUps** — Before you leave, agree what happens because of what you saw, each with one owner and a due date: correcting a number, restating an estimate, taking a workaround to the people who can remove it. A visit with workarounds and no follow-ups was only interesting.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. The record works out the minutes watched from the first and last times and counts the workarounds, surprises, answers and follow-ups. It flags a watch-for item with nothing noted, an observation with no time or a word that usually means an opinion ("seemed", "struggled", "confused"), a question not tied to an observation, with no answer, or about the future ("would you", "if we"), a quote with no context, and a follow-up with no owner or date.
- **takeaway** — Write in two or three sentences what you learned: what the work really takes, where the time and the workarounds go, and what that means for the decision in front of you. Then say what you'll watch next, usually someone else doing the same work.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' RevOps lead sitting beside Northline Freight's RevOps lead on Monday 2 March 2026 while the weekly pipeline review is built, to check the 4 hours a week the business case counts before the case goes to Northline's finance team. Eight observations from 08:30 to 11:35, 185 minutes, with all four watch-for items noted: three workarounds (last week's notes carried across by hand, stall reasons collected by chat, a deal amount corrected in the spreadsheet but not in the CRM) and three surprises, including the review going out without a reason for 12 of its 31 stalled deals. Four questions asked after watching, each tied to an observation and answered in the customer's words, confirm the estimate: about another hour of chasing after lunch makes four hours for one person. Two quotes kept, and three follow-ups, each with an owner and a date. The record has nothing for the checks to flag.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking questions while they work.** — The question stops the work and changes it: they start explaining how it should go and then do it that way. Note the time and what you saw, and ask once the work is done.
- **Writing how they seemed instead of what they did.** — "Struggled with the export" is your reading of it and can't be checked. "Exported 212 rows, deleted closed and duplicate rows by hand" can, and it shows where the time went. Ask afterwards how it felt.
- **Asking what they would do.** — "Would you use a tool that did this?" gets a polite yes. "Tell me about the last time a note didn't carry across" gets what really happens and what it cost.
- **Watching a demonstration instead of the work.** — A demo on a quiet afternoon skips the exceptions, the chasing and the corrections, which is where the workarounds live. Come when the real work is happening, with real data.
- **Leaving without follow-ups.** — A workaround seen and not acted on means the visit changed nothing. Agree who does what by when before you go, even if it's only to correct a number or ask a second person.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Customer interview guide** (`customer-interview-guide`) — When you can't watch the work, plan a conversation instead. Interviews often show which work is worth going to watch.
- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — The same habit inside your own operation: watch first, write facts, ask after. This protocol points it at a customer's work and records what they did rather than a standard.

Downstream:

- **Voice of the customer (VOC) evidence log** (`voc-evidence-log`) — Workarounds, surprises and quotes from each visit go into the evidence log, so a pattern across several customers can be counted rather than remembered.
- **Affinity diagram** (`affinity-diagram`) — After several visits, group the workarounds and surprises to see which problems are shared and which belong to one customer.
- **Customer journey map** (`customer-journey-map`) — Timed observations show what each step really involves and where the friction is, which gives the journey map evidence instead of assumptions.
- **Business case** (`business-case`) — Time watched is better evidence than time estimated: a visit turns one person's guess into a figure a finance team can trust.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing whom to watch, when, and what to watch for
- Deciding what a workaround or a surprise means for the customer and for you
- Agreeing follow-ups with the customer and your own team

Analysis — AI helps:

- Spotting patterns across several visits: the same workaround at different customers
- Rewriting an inference in the record as a question to ask next time
- Suggesting questions tied to the observations, about what happened rather than what would

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out the minutes watched and counting workarounds, surprises and answers
- Turning handwritten notes from the clipboard into a clean record
- Laying out the record and exporting it as a workbook
