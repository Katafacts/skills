# Daily management huddle

Answers one question each day: what stopped the work, what could stop it today, and who is doing what about it by when? The short standing meeting where a team reads its board, looks ahead at the day, and leaves with names against problems — plus the handful of numbers that tell you whether the huddle is still working or has quietly become a status meeting.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A short, standing, same-time-every-day meeting where a team reads its own board, says out loud what went wrong, and leaves with a named person and a date against each thing worth acting on. Ten to fifteen minutes, enforced. The idea that matters most: a huddle is a queue. Making problems visible raises the rate they arrive, and if nobody designs the capacity to close them, the list grows, raising stops feeling worthwhile, people go quiet, and the quiet is read as success. So the tool sets four things alongside the meeting: a cap on how many problems can be open at once, a promise about how fast someone responds, a three-way triage (fix now, watch, or needs problem solving), and a one-line record of each huddle. Two rules surprise people. More problems raised is good news, not bad. And the number raised must never become a target: it tells you whether people still trust the process, and once it is managed it stops telling you anything. The leader's job in the huddle is mostly questions: what did you see at the work, not who did it. A problem traced to a person gets hidden next time; one traced to the work gets raised again.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A team with a board or grid it already marks, that needs the meeting that makes it worth marking.
- Work with enough repetition that the same kinds of problems recur — that is what makes a queue worth managing.
- Someone in the room can act, or can reach the person who can, without writing a business case first.
- You suspect your existing stand-up has become a status round and want to know whether it still surfaces anything.

Not when:

- As a status report to a manager. If the purpose is telling someone what happened rather than deciding what to do, it is a different meeting and it will drive out honest problems.
- For individual accountability. Nothing in this record should ever reach an appraisal — attendance is counted rather than named for exactly that reason.
- At a group size where speaking up is expensive. Above roughly fifteen, or with several layers of leadership present, people stop raising the things worth hearing.
- As a reason to wait until things calm down. A team at full stretch needs to see its problems most; what it can't do is work all of them. Make problems visible anyway, keep the cap on open items low, and work only what fits under it — the rest waits, visibly, rather than being hidden or half-done.
- For project coordination or task allocation. Those are real meetings, but they are not this one, and merging them turns the huddle into a to-do list review.

## How to fill it in

- **setup** — Fix the boring things first, because they are what decay. Where — at the board, standing, not in a room with a screen. When — same time every period, and hold it when you are busy, because busy is when problems arrive. How long — a timebox you actually enforce, usually ten to fifteen minutes. How many — five to fifteen people; below five there is no team, above fifteen the cost of speaking up gets too high. Then the two limits that do most of the work: a cap on how many problems may be open at once, and a promise about how quickly someone responds to a raised item, meaning a named owner agrees a date for it. Both are commitments about capacity, not aspirations, and both should be uncomfortable to set.
- **agenda** — Run the same blocks, in the same order, every period, so nobody has to remember what happens next. A workable shape: read the board (name every red out loud before explaining any of them), reasons for the reds (one line of fact each, from whoever was closest to the work), triage (each item goes one of three ways — fix it now, watch it another period, or it needs a proper problem-solving cycle), yesterday's promises (anything due or overdue, closed or re-dated out loud with a reason), today ahead (the plan, who is in and who is short, and any risk that could stop the work before the next huddle), and a last call for anyone who needs help. Give each block minutes and add them up. The block teams skip is yesterday's promises, and skipping it is precisely how the list stops meaning anything. The block teams forget is today ahead: a huddle that only looks back finds problems after they have happened.
- **occurrences** — Write one line per huddle, straight afterwards, in about twenty seconds: the date, how many were there, how long it took, whether anyone actually read the board, and how many items were raised and closed. This line is what makes the health checks possible — a single huddle tells you nothing about whether the practice is alive, and by the time decay is obvious from inside the room it has usually been running for months.
- **items** — Write what was seen, which measure it came from when it came from one, how it was triaged, a named person, and the date that person agreed. Never a department: an item with no named responder is a complaint rather than a request for help. The date is not the team's response promise, which is a blanket default — it is what somebody actually said out loud about this item, and it is the difference between a list that gets worked and one that gets read out. Leaving it blank is allowed and is honest, because plenty of things are raised before anyone has agreed when they will be dealt with; it is only a problem when it stays blank, which is why items with no date are counted separately from items that are late. Write a commitment that can be answered yes or no. "Handle onboarding" is an area of ownership restated, and it gives nobody a clear answer on any given morning. When it closes, write what actually happened — not "done", but what changed and how you know. The three-way triage is the part that keeps this workable: without an explicit "watch", everything either escalates or gets quietly dropped, and a team that escalates everything drowns.
- **health** — Calculated from the huddle lines and the items — don't type it; check it against what you've seen in the room. It splits into two groups that often disagree. The meeting numbers — held against expected, average length, overruns, group size, whether the board was read — say whether the ritual is intact; the queue numbers — raised against closed, open against your cap, the oldest open item, items past the date their owner agreed, items still without a date past your response promise, and whether raising is going up or down — say whether it still does anything. When the two disagree, believe the queue numbers.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences on whether the huddle is still working: what the meeting numbers say, what the queue numbers say, and where they disagree. Use only what the record shows, and if there are too few huddles on record to see a trend, say so.

## What good looks like

The example below is a customer operations team over three weeks in September, and it looks healthy while it is quietly dying. Eleven of twelve huddles were held, averaging just under eleven minutes against a fifteen-minute timebox, with one overrun and the group always inside its size band. On those numbers alone, a well-run practice. The queue says otherwise: twelve things raised and seven closed, every closure a quick fix done within three days, and none of the four items needing problem solving has moved. Eight items are open against a cap of five: two are past the date their owner agreed, three have had no date agreed at all, long past the two-day response promise, and the oldest, a failed billing export, has been open fifty-seven days. Now read the raising column in date order: two, two, two, two, then one, one, one, zero, one, zero, zero. That is people giving up on the board, not running out of problems. From outside, shorter meetings and fewer problems raised would look like success, which is exactly why the queue numbers sit beside the meeting numbers.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating fewer problems raised as an improvement** — It is the main way huddles fail, and it always looks like success. Read the raising rate as a sign of whether people still trust the process, never as a performance number, and never set a target on it in either direction.
- **No limit on how many problems can be open at once** — A queue without a cap does not go faster, it stops finishing. How long each item waits is the open count divided by how many close each day, so the cap is the real lever on time to close. Without one the list grows until raising anything feels pointless.
- **Closing the easy items and never the hard ones** — Quick fixes keep the closed count moving while the problem-solving pile sits untouched, and a workaround logged as a fix leaves the cause where it was. If nothing that needs problem solving has closed in weeks, close it properly, hand it on, or drop it out loud.
- **Letting a date slide past instead of re-dating it out loud** — Once a few items are permanently late, nobody reads the dates at all. Re-dating with a reason at the huddle keeps the list honest; a quiet breach teaches the team that dates do not mean anything.
- **Turning it into a status round, or letting it grow** — Once each person reports what they did, or several layers of leadership are standing there, the meeting has an audience instead of a purpose, and nobody volunteers a problem to an audience. Read the board, not the people, and split a huddle that passes about fifteen.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Performance grid** (`performance-grid`) — What the team stands in front of. The grid produces the reds; the huddle is where a red turns into a named person with a date.
- **Leader standard work** (`leader-standard-work`) — What the leader does to make the huddle survive: being there, asking real questions, and following up on what they heard. A huddle delegated away signals it does not matter.

Downstream:

- **Tiered huddle board** (`tiered-huddle-board`) — What to do when an item is genuinely beyond this team: the route upward, what it takes with it, and what has to come back down.
- **Escalation matrix** (`escalation-matrix`) — Who an unclosable item goes to, how fast, and what returns. Without this, a huddle either escalates nothing or escalates everything.
- **Pareto chart** (`pareto-chart`) — Which of the things raised keep coming back. That, not volume or volume of complaint, is what earns the next problem-solving cycle.
- **A3 problem solving** (`a3`) — Where an item triaged as needing a proper cycle actually goes. If nothing on that pile ever closes, the triage has become a way of filing things rather than working them.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — The team's one list of what is being done about causes. The huddle's items say who does what by when; an item that needs a cause removed becomes a log entry, read out here.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Setting a cap on open problems that reflects the team's real capacity rather than its optimism
- Deciding whether an item should be fixed now, watched, or worked properly
- Judging whether the huddle has become a status round, which needs someone who was in the room
- Setting how many open items the team can really work at once, so visibility doesn't outrun capacity

Analysis — AI helps:

- Reading the meeting numbers against the queue numbers and saying plainly when they disagree
- Spotting that the raising rate is falling while the queue is not draining — the signature of a team that has stopped believing raising anything helps
- Noticing that everything closing is a same-day fix while the harder pile never moves
- Saying when there are too few huddles on record to claim a trend at all

Drudgery — automated:

- Totting up raised against closed, held against expected, and the age of the oldest open item
- Counting overruns, out-of-band group sizes, items past their agreed date, and undated items past the response promise
- Drafting a first standing agenda with timings that add up to the timebox
- Exporting the record to xlsx in the house format
