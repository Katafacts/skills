# Improvement Kata storyboard

Work toward a hard challenge one small, predicted step at a time: a challenge, the current condition seen first-hand, a near target condition 1 to 4 weeks out, one obstacle in focus, and experiment records where what you expect is written before each step. Use it with a coach when the path to a goal can't be planned in advance.

Family: Improvement kata · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

The Improvement Kata is a practice routine for reaching a goal when you can't see the path in advance. ("Kata" means a pattern you practise until it becomes habit.) Mike Rother described it in his book Toyota Kata, from studying how Toyota develops improvement thinking in its people. It has four steps: understand the challenge, grasp the current condition, set the next target condition, and experiment toward it one step at a time. The storyboard is the one sheet the learner keeps for all four, and the sheet a coach reads with them in a short, regular coaching cycle. It deliberately doesn't hold a plan of solutions. The route is found through small experiments, each with a prediction written down before the step, so the gap between what you expected and what happened shows exactly where your knowledge ran out, which is where the next step goes.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have a challenging goal and nobody can say in advance exactly how to get there, so you'll need to learn your way.
- You can go and see the process yourself, and try small steps whose results show up within days.
- You want to build improvement thinking in a person or a team, with a coach who meets them regularly, not only fix one problem.

Not when:

- The solution is already known and agreed. Just do it, and track it with an implementation plan.
- You have one bounded problem with a measurable gap that needs taking to root cause. An A3 fits better, and can sit alongside a storyboard as one obstacle's deep dive.
- Results only show up monthly or quarterly and you can't find a measure that moves within days. Find a faster process measure first, or the experiments will be too slow to learn from.
- As a way to judge or rank people. The storyboard only works if a wrong prediction is safe to write down.

## How to fill it in

- **challenge** — Write the direction you're working toward, usually six months to three years out, set by or agreed with the level above you. It should be beyond what you know how to do today: if you already know how to get there, it's a project plan, not a challenge. Describe what will be different for the customer or the process, with a measure and a date, and say why it matters. Don't write the solution into the challenge.
- **currentCondition** — Go and see the work where it happens before setting a target condition. Watch several cycles, time them, and talk to the people doing the work. Write down two things. The operating pattern: how the work actually runs today, including the sequence, who does what, where time goes and how much it varies. And the outcome: what the process produces today, with its spread, not just an average. Date each observation, and note where you were, what you saw and who does the work. Reports and dashboards can show you where to look, but they don't replace watching. Update the current condition after each step; it keeps moving.
- **targetCondition** — Describe how you want the process to be running on a date 1 to 4 weeks out. (Rother's range reaches further, up to about three months; this template keeps it short so you learn quickly.) Write it in two parts. First the operating pattern, as a state you could go and see: "every changeover runs from materials already staged at the booth, and both shifts clean in the same order." Then the outcome metric, with a number, a unit and a date: "35 minutes or less on 8 of the last 10 changeovers, by 15 May." It isn't a list of actions: "train the operators, buy a cart" is a to-do list, and the actions are what your experiments will discover. It should sit beyond what you know how to reach today. Once it's set, keep it until the date, and put what you learn into the next one.
- **obstacles** — List what you think stands between the current condition and the target condition. It's a parking lot, not a plan: you don't need to rank it carefully or solve everything on it. Pick one obstacle, mark it in focus, and work only on that one until it's cleared or you learn it doesn't matter. Expect the list to change as you learn; add new obstacles as they appear and strike out cleared ones. Obstacles are about the process (the method, materials, equipment or information), never about a person.
- **experiments** — One row per step, filled in as a Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle. Plan: write the step and what you expect will happen, before you take it. Do: take the step. Check: write what actually happened, as soon as you can go and see. Act: write what you learned, which shapes the next step. There are two kinds of step. An observe step goes and looks to learn more, and changes nothing. A change step changes one thing, so you can tell what caused the result. Give each step a date to go and see the result, ideally within a day or a few days; if you can't see the result within about a week, the step is too big, so break it down. Never fill in or edit the expectation once you know the result. A prediction that didn't come true is the most useful row on the sheet.
- **thresholdOfKnowledge** — The threshold of knowledge is the line between what you know and what you don't know yet. Before each step, write what you don't know yet that the step should find out. Your next step belongs right at that line: not so far beyond it that you can't interpret the result, and not behind it repeating what you already know. When a result surprises you, you've found the line. Not knowing is normal and useful; pretending to know is what stalls improvement.
- **metric** — Record the outcome metric on the day you measure it, from your own measurements, and plot it as a run chart: dates along the bottom, values up the side, with the target line drawn across. Look at the pattern over several points rather than reacting to a single one. Don't rebuild the numbers afterwards from memory.
- **coachingCycle** — A short conversation, often 20 minutes or less, held at the storyboard daily or a few times a week. The coach asks and the learner answers from the board, pointing at it. 1. What are you trying to reach right now? (the target condition) 2. Where are you actually now? (the current condition) Then look back at the last step: what did you plan to do, what did you expect, what actually happened, and what did you learn? 3. What do you think is in your way now, and which one thing are you working on? 4. What is your next step, and what do you expect will happen? 5. How soon can we go and see what we learned from that step? The coach doesn't hand over solutions. They check the learner's thinking follows the pattern, ask "how do you know?", and help the learner find the threshold of knowledge. These questions are a plain-language paraphrase of the five coaching questions described in Toyota Kata.

## Common mistakes

- **The target condition is a to-do list.** — "Buy a cart, train second shift, update the standard" decides the answers before anyone has learned anything, and leaves nothing to check except whether the tasks got done. Describe how the process will be running, plus a dated outcome, and let the experiments find the actions.
- **The prediction is written after the result.** — Once you know what happened, it's almost impossible not to "expect" it. The gap between expected and actual is what shows where your knowledge ran out, so write the expectation first and never edit it.
- **Several changes in one step.** — If you stage a cart, move the solvent store and change the cleaning order all at once, you can't tell which one made the difference, or which one made things worse. Change one thing per step. Observe steps, which change nothing, are how you learn before changing.
- **Skipping go and see.** — A current condition written from a report or from memory describes the process as people think it runs. The walks away from the booth, the drying wait and the locked cage only show up when you watch. Go again after every few steps, because the current condition keeps moving.
- **Blaming people.** — "Operator error", "retrain the team" and "be more careful" stop the learning at a person and leave the process that let it happen untouched. Ask what about the method, materials, equipment or information made the mistake possible, and work on that.
- **Steps too big to learn from.** — A step whose result you can't see for weeks gives you a few learning cycles a quarter instead of several a week. If the go-and-see date is more than about a week away, break the step down.
