# Customer journey map

See your service the way one kind of customer lives it, stage by stage: what they're trying to do, where they meet you, how it feels and what goes wrong, with the evidence for each. The feeling line, the low points, which pain points are proven and which are still assumptions, and where to start are worked out for you.

Family: Voice of the customer · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A customer journey map lays out, from the customer's side, the stages they go through to get what they need from you: finding you, buying, getting set up, using what they bought, getting help, paying, deciding whether to stay. For each stage it records what the customer is trying to get done, what they actually do, where they meet you (a person, a web page, an email, an invoice), how it feels to them and what goes wrong. Inside a business, work is organised by department, so nobody sees the whole experience: sales sees the deal, support sees the ticket, finance sees the invoice. The customer sees one journey, and a bad handoff between two departments is the part they remember. The map puts that journey on one page so the team can see where it breaks. Two things make a map worth trusting. First, it's built from evidence: interviews, support tickets, survey comments, watching customers, data. A map drawn in a workshop from what the team believes is a picture of the team's assumptions. This tool asks for the evidence behind every pain point and marks the ones without any as assumptions to check. Second, it focuses: a few moments decide whether a customer stays, pays or recommends you. Marking those, and seeing which of them feel bad, tells you where to start.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Customers complain, leave or go quiet and nobody can say where in the experience it goes wrong.
- Several teams each own a part of the customer's experience and nobody owns the whole.
- You've collected customer evidence (interviews, tickets, survey comments) and want to see it in the order customers live it.
- You're about to change a service, onboarding or a sales process and want to know which moments matter most before you start.

Not when:

- You haven't talked to customers or looked at what they've told you. Plan how to collect that evidence first; a map from opinions only spreads the opinions.
- You already know which step fails and want to know why. Map that process step by step with a Makigami process map, or find the cause with a 5-Whys.
- Your customers are too different to share one journey (a first-time buyer and a large account renewing). Map one kind of customer at a time.
- You need to specify what good looks like in measurable terms. Turn the moments that matter into measures with a critical-to-quality (CTQ) tree.

## How to fill it in

- **customer** — Name one kind of customer, described by what they're trying to do and their situation, not by a made-up persona with a name and a hobby. "The admin at a new business customer who sets the product up for their team" is enough. One map per kind of customer.
- **scope** — Say where the journey starts and ends, in the customer's terms: from noticing they need something to getting it, or from signing to the first invoice. A clear start and end keeps the map from growing to cover everything you do.
- **basis** — List the evidence the map is built from: how many interviews, which tickets, which survey, what you watched, what data. Anyone reading the map should be able to tell how much of it is known and how much is believed.
- **stages** — Write four to eight stages across the top, in the order the customer lives them, named in their words ("Bring data in"), not your department names ("Implementation phase 2"). For each stage write the customer's goal (what they're trying to get done), what they actually do, and where they meet you: people, pages, emails, documents. If a stage has no goal of its own, merge it with its neighbour.
- **feelings** — Rate how each stage feels to the customer from +2 (very good) to -2 (very bad), and write down something a real customer said about it, word for word. The rating comes from the evidence, not from what the team hopes; the quote is what keeps it honest. The tool draws the feeling line from your ratings and marks the low points.
- **painPoints** — Write up to three pain points per stage: the things that go wrong for the customer, stated as facts ("Import takes far longer than the demo suggests"), not solutions ("We need a better importer"). For each, pick where the evidence comes from: interview, support ticket, survey, observation or data. If there isn't any yet, pick 'None yet (assumption)'. That's allowed and useful: it turns a belief into a question to check. Note where the evidence lives so someone can go back to it.
- **moments** — Mark the moments that matter: the stages that decide whether the customer stays, pays, comes back or recommends you. Usually two to four, not all of them. For every stage, name who on your side owns it. A moment that matters with no owner is the first thing to fix.
- **summary** — Calculated from your ratings and evidence: the average and lowest feeling, the low points, how many pain points are backed by evidence and how many are still assumptions, and which moments that matter feel bad, worst first. Start with those, and check the assumptions before anyone builds a fix on them.
- **nextStep** — Write what the team will do, who will do it and by when: usually one or two fixes at the worst moments that matter, a measure to show the change worked, and the questions to ask customers to check the assumptions.

## What good looks like

The example below maps the first 30 days for the admin who sets up a business software product for their team, built from the same interviews, tickets and survey comments as the voice of the customer evidence log, with the customers' quotes word for word. Six stages from signing to the first invoice. The feeling line dips twice, to -2 at setting up access and at bringing data in. Eight of the nine pain points are backed by evidence and one is honestly marked as an assumption. Four stages are moments that matter and three of them feel bad, worst first: setting up access, bringing data in, the first invoice. The next step gives each an owner and a date. For bringing data in it does two things: it acts on the evidenced pain (import takes far longer than the demo suggests) with an import session in the first week and a measure of days to the first successful import, and, since failed imports have been seen in an interview and at a go-see, it checks the one unproven part (whether the error says which field failed) before anyone builds a fix.

## Common mistakes

- **Mapping from a workshop instead of from customers.** — A room of colleagues produces the journey the business thinks it runs, with the pain points it already knows about. Build the map from interviews, tickets, survey comments and watching customers, and mark anything else as an assumption.
- **Naming the stages after your departments or your process.** — "Onboarding phase 2" is how you organise the work, not how the customer experiences it. Stages named in the customer's words keep the map on their side of the counter, which is the whole point.
- **Writing solutions in the pain point row.** — "Needs a setup wizard" closes the question before anyone has looked at why setup hurts. Write what goes wrong for the customer; decide what to change after the map shows where it matters most.
- **Marking every stage as a moment that matters.** — If everything matters, the map can't tell you where to start. Pick the few stages that decide whether customers stay, pay or recommend you.
- **Mixing several kinds of customer in one map.** — A new small customer and a large account renewing live very different journeys. Averaging them hides both. One map per kind of customer.
- **Hanging the map on the wall and stopping there.** — A map changes nothing by itself. Give each moment that matters an owner, pick one or two to fix, measure them, and update the map when the evidence changes.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Voice of the customer (VOC) collection plan** (`voc-collection-plan`) — The collection plan sets up the interviews, ticket tagging and surveys that feed the map. Pain points marked as assumptions become goals for the next round of collection.
- **Customer interview guide** (`customer-interview-guide`) — Interviews that walk customers through their experience in order ("what happened first, then what?") give the stages, the feelings and the quotes the map needs.
- **Voice of the customer (VOC) evidence log** (`voc-evidence-log`) — The evidence log holds the quotes and their sources. The map puts them in the order customers live them, so the themes in the log show up at the stage where they happen.

Downstream:

- **Critical-to-quality (CTQ) tree** (`ctq-tree`) — Turn the worst moments that matter into measurable requirements: what the customer needs at that stage, what drives it and the measure that shows it's fixed.
- **Makigami process map** (`makigami`) — When a stage feels bad, map the work behind it step by step: the people, handoffs and systems inside your business that make the customer wait or repeat themselves.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Choosing which kind of customer to map and where the journey starts and ends
- Deciding which stages are moments that matter and what to fix first
- Judging whether the evidence really supports a rating or a pain point

Analysis — AI helps:

- Sorting interview notes, tickets and survey comments into the stage where each happened
- Spotting the same pain point told in different words by different customers
- Suggesting questions for the next interviews to test the assumptions

Drudgery — automated:

- Drawing the feeling line and finding the low points
- Counting pain points with and without evidence, per stage and in total
- Listing the moments that matter that feel bad, worst first
