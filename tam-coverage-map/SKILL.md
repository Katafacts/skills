# Total addressable market (TAM) coverage map

How far into your market you really reach, segment by segment: of the accounts in each segment, how many are in your customer relationship management (CRM) system, how many you worked this period, how many have an open opportunity and how many are customers. The percentages, the step where each segment loses the most accounts and the biggest gaps are computed.

Family: Problem definition · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A total addressable market (TAM) coverage map counts accounts, not dollars. For each segment of your market it puts five numbers side by side: the accounts that exist in the market, the ones in your customer relationship management (CRM) system, the ones your team actually worked in the period, the ones with an open opportunity, and the ones already paying you. Each number sits inside the one before, so reading across a row shows where accounts drop out: never loaded, loaded but nobody calls, called but nothing opens. Market size estimates tell you how big the prize is; this tells you how much of it your team can even see and touch. The usual surprise is that the gap is not where the effort goes: whole segments that fit well are barely in the CRM, while the team keeps working the accounts it already knows.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Planning next quarter's territories, outbound effort or list buying, and you want to put it where the gap is rather than where the habit is.
- Pipeline is short and you need to know whether the problem is reach (accounts nobody has loaded or called) or conversion (accounts called with nothing opening).
- You've written down your ideal customer and want to know how much of that market your team actually reaches.
- Reviewing whether a segment you chose to grow is really being worked.

Not when:

- You need the size of the market in money to make a business case. This counts accounts you reach; use a market-sizing estimate for revenue.
- You want to rank individual accounts to call next. That's a fit-criteria scorecard; this map tells you which segment to rank first.
- You want to know who you know inside one account. That's a stakeholder coverage map.
- You have no list of the market that is independent of your CRM. Get one first; counting the market from the CRM hides exactly the gap this map is for.

## How to fill it in

- **market** — Say what counts as an account in the market and where the list comes from: the industries, size bands and regions you sell to, from a bought list, a public register or a data provider. Count it from a source that doesn't depend on your CRM. Leave out accounts your own rules disqualify.
- **period** — Say which period "worked" covers (the last quarter or the last 90 days) and what counts as worked: at least one real two-way conversation, such as a meeting, a call or a reply. Emails nobody answered don't count; if they did, every account in the CRM would look worked.
- **segments** — List the segments down the side the way you plan and staff the team: industry and size band works for most. Four to eight is plenty. Put accounts outside your best-fit size band in their own segment, so you can see how much effort goes there.
- **counts** — Type five counts per segment, all as of the same date. In the market: accounts on the market list. In the CRM: of those, accounts with a record. Worked: of those, accounts with a real conversation in the period. Open opportunity: of those, accounts with an open deal. Customers: accounts in the segment already paying you, which are inside In the CRM. Count accounts, not contacts or deals.
- **winRate** — Optional, and worth the five minutes: for each segment, the share of last year's decided deals that were won. Won divided by won plus lost; leave out deals still open. It sits next to the gaps so you read them against where you win. A rate from two or three deals is a hint, not a verdict; say how many deals it rests on in the note.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here. For each segment and in total the map works out the share of the market in the CRM, the share worked, the share of worked accounts with an open opportunity and the customer share. It counts the accounts lost at each step, names the step where each segment loses the most (its biggest leak), and names the segment with the biggest gap at each step, with its win rate when you gave one. A segment with no market count, or a count bigger than the one it sits inside, is flagged and left out of the drop-outs until its counts are fixed.
- **nextStep** — Say where the effort goes next and what you'll stop, each with a person and a date. Read the gaps next to where you win: a big gap in a segment you rarely win can wait, a small one in your best segment can't. Count again next quarter from the same sources so the change is real.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' count on 27 March 2026 across six segments: four inside the profile's size band, plus companies below it (20 to 99 employees) and above it (enterprise), each kept separate so the effort spent outside the band shows. 310 accounts in the market, 192 in the CRM (61.9%), 82 worked in the last 90 days (26.5% of the market), 17 with an open opportunity (20.7% of those worked) and 15 customers (4.8%). Logistics and freight has the biggest gap at the first step: 28 of its 40 accounts aren't in the CRM, so only 30% are. Software and subscription has the most accounts in the CRM that nobody worked (47) and the most worked with no opportunity (32), and it is also where the team wins most often: 70% of last year's deals that reached a proposal. Professional services has 31 accounts in the CRM and 4 worked. Meanwhile the team worked 12 enterprise accounts and 10 smaller ones, each more than the 9 in logistics, in the two segments where it won least (13% and 25% of deals that reached a proposal last year). Read against the win rates, the next step gives the unworked software accounts owners first, loads the missing logistics accounts so they can be scored, and moves enterprise and the smaller companies back to inbound only.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the market from the CRM.** — If the market list is what's already in the CRM, coverage looks close to 100% and the biggest gap disappears. Count the market from an outside list, then match it to the CRM.
- **Counting any email as worked.** — A sequence can touch every record in a week without a single conversation. Count an account as worked only when someone there talked back.
- **Chasing the biggest number without asking about fit.** — The biggest gap is not always the best place to go. Read the gaps next to where you win: a large unworked segment you rarely win is cheaper to leave alone.
- **Mixing dates and sources.** — A market list from last year against this week's CRM makes counts run backwards. Take all five counts on the same date, and use the same sources each time you count.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Ideal customer profile (ICP) canvas** (`icp-canvas`) — The canvas defines the segments that win. This map counts how much of each of them your team actually reaches.
- **Anti-ideal customer profile (anti-ICP)** (`anti-icp`) — Its hard disqualifiers decide which accounts stay out of the market count, so the gaps aren't padded with accounts you'd never sell to.

Downstream:

- **Fit-criteria scorecard** (`fit-criteria-scorecard`) — Once this map shows which segment to go after, the scorecard ranks its accounts so the best ones get called first.
- **Stage conversion analysis** (`stage-conversion-analysis`) — This map stops at the open opportunity. Stage conversion follows those deals through the funnel to won or lost.
- **Stakeholder coverage map** (`stakeholder-coverage-map`) — This map shows which accounts you reach at all. The stakeholder map goes inside one account and shows who you reach there.
