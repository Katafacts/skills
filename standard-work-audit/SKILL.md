# Standard work audit

Watch the work and check it against the written standard, step by step: was each step done as written (yes, partly, no), what was seen and, where the standard gives minutes, how long it took. For every gap, find the cause (the standard is wrong, the person wasn't trained, something blocks them, or they chose another way) and the response that cause calls for, with an owner and a date. The % of steps to standard, gaps by cause and time against standard are computed.

Family: Standard work · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A written standard (a work instruction, a routine card, a procedure) is only useful if the work is actually done that way. A standard work audit checks that by watching. You take the standard, go to where the work happens, and watch someone do the job at its normal pace. For each step, as written, you mark whether it was done as written (yes, partly or no) and write what you actually saw. The audit's real value is in the gaps. When a step isn't done as written, ask the person why, without blame, and the answer nearly always falls into one of four causes, each with its own fix. The standard is wrong: it can't be followed or no longer fits the work, so you change the standard and then retrain. The person wasn't trained: they never learned the step, so you train them to it and watch them do it. Something blocks them: they know the step and can't do it (no time, the wrong tool, a system that won't load), so you find it and remove it. Or they chose another way: they know it, nothing stops them, and they do it differently. That last one is rarer than it looks, and usually means their way seems better to them, so ask why before coaching: sometimes it is better, and the standard should change. Getting the cause right matters more than the score, because retraining people to a wrong standard teaches the gap and blaming people for a blocked step teaches them to hide it. Where the standard gives minutes per step (a work instruction's minutes, a standard work chart's times), time each step too: a step much slower than standard usually has something in the way, and one much faster often skips a key point.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A standard has been in use for a few weeks and you want to know whether the work is really done that way.
- Results vary between people or shifts doing the same job, and you suspect they're doing it differently.
- A new starter has been trained on a standard and you want to confirm the training took.
- A sustainment audit found a practice drifting and you want to see exactly which steps slip, and why.

Not when:

- There's no written standard yet. Write the work instruction first; there's nothing to audit against.
- You want to know whether a whole change held, results included. Use a sustainment audit, which checks results and practices together.
- You can't watch the work, only ask about it. A standard work audit from memory or interviews records what people say they do.
- The aim is a disciplinary record. People will do the job by the book while you watch and their own way after, and you learn nothing.

## How to fill it in

- **standard** — Name the standard you're checking: its title, its version or date, and where the people doing the work find it. Audit against the version they actually have; if that isn't the latest, that is your first finding.
- **observation** — Say where you watched, who, and how many times. Watch real work at its normal pace, more than one cycle and, where you can, more than one person or shift, so one good or bad moment doesn't decide the audit. Tell people what you're doing and why: you're checking the standard, not marking them.
- **steps** — Copy each step and its key point from the standard, as written, in order. Don't reword them into what you think they mean. If the work has a step the standard doesn't mention, add it as a row: work that isn't in the standard is a finding too.
- **observed** — For each step, mark Yes if it was done as written every time you saw it, Partly if some of the time or only part of it, and No if not at all. Write what you saw: how many times out of how many, and what was different. "3 of 4 tickets; on the fourth the page timed out" is evidence; "mostly fine" is not. If the standard gives minutes per step, write them, time each step as you watch and write the average: the audit shows each step within 20% of standard, or slower or faster by how much.
- **cause** — For each Partly or No, ask the person why, then pick the cause. Standard wrong: the written way can't be followed or no longer fits, so change the standard, then retrain. Not trained: they never learned it, so train them and watch them do it. Blocked: they know it and something stops them (time, tools, a system), so find it and remove it. Chose not to: they know it, nothing stops them, and they do it another way, so ask what makes their way seem better, settle on one way (theirs, if it is better) and coach to it. Write the response, one owner and a due date. The workbook shows what each cause calls for next to it.
- **score** — Nothing to enter: the % of steps to standard (Yes out of the steps rated), counts of each cause, gaps with no action and, for timed steps, the standard and observed minutes and how many are more than 20% off are worked out for you, and each step's check flags anything missing. Read the causes before the score: a low score from one wrong standard is a quick fix; the same score with every gap put down to people choosing not to follow it needs a closer look at the standard and what gets in their way.
- **nextStep** — Write what happens next, in order (change the standard before retraining on it), and when you'll audit again. Share the audit with the people you watched and with the standard's owner.

## What good looks like

The example below audits the work instruction "Triage a renewal-risk support ticket" a month after it went into use, by watching two support specialists triage four live tickets and timing each step. One of four steps is done as written, 25% to standard: routing to the on-call responder. The other three are done partly, and each gap has a different cause. Logging the resolution path follows the instruction on only 1 of 4 tickets because the standard is wrong: the category list has no billing category, so the fix is to change the list and the instruction first. A placeholder left in a first response is a training gap for a new starter, so she is taken through the updated instruction and watched. A skipped health-score check is blocked, not chosen: the customer record page times out, so the fix removes the slow page from the step rather than reminding anyone. The timing says the same: the 12-minute triage takes 14, and the two steps more than 20% slow are the blocked one and the one with the wrong standard. Every gap has a response, an owner and a date.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting every gap down to people not following the standard.** — It feels like the obvious answer and is often wrong. Ask why first: if the standard can't be followed as written, or nobody taught it, a reminder fixes nothing.
- **Retraining people on a standard that's wrong.** — It teaches them the gap, and tells them the audit doesn't listen. Change the standard first, then train everyone on the new version.
- **Auditing from the standard on your desk, not the work.** — Without watching, you record what people say they do. The audit is only as good as what was seen.
- **Watching one cycle by one person.** — One cycle can be unusually good or bad. Watch a few, and more than one person or shift, so the finding is about the standard and not a moment.
- **Calling a blocked step a choice.** — "Didn't follow it" sounds like the person's fault, but most skipped steps are can't, not won't: the page timed out, the tool was missing, the queue was full. Ask what stopped them before writing chose not to.
- **Treating the audit as a test of the person.** — People then perform the standard while watched and stop when you leave. The audit checks the standard and the conditions around it; say so before you start.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Work instruction** (`work-instruction`) — The instruction's steps and key points are the rows of the audit, as written. When the cause is "standard wrong", the fix goes back into the instruction.
- **Sustainment audit** (`sustainment-audit`) — A sustainment audit checks whether a whole change held. When one of its practices is drifting, this audit shows which steps slip and why.
- **Standard work chart** (`standard-work-chart`) — The chart sets the sequence, takt and standard work in progress the audit checks against. Copy each element's time into the audit's standard minutes and time the work beside it, so the audit checks pace as well as sequence.
- **Error-proofing** (`error-proofing`) — Error-proofing devices and their scheduled tests become rows of the audit: is the device still in place, and is it really being tested?

Downstream:

- **Training matrix with I, L, U, O skill levels (ILUO)** (`iluo-training-matrix`) — A "not trained" gap shows someone isn't yet at the level the matrix says. Update it once they've been trained and watched.
- **Countermeasure log** (`countermeasure-log`) — Each gap's response, owner and date goes on the countermeasure log so it is followed up at the huddle.
- **5-Whys** (`five-whys`) — When a step keeps not being followed and the obvious reason doesn't explain it, ask why at the work until you reach something you can change.
