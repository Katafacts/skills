# Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)

A structured failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) that scores each way a process or product could fail on severity, occurrence, and detection, looks at severity first, and ranks the rest by a risk priority number so the vital-few risks get acted on first.

Family: Problem solving / root cause analysis · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) lists the specific ways a process or product could fail, then scores each one from 1 to 10 on severity (how bad the effect would be), occurrence (how likely the cause is) and detection (how likely today's controls are to miss it). This tool ranks in two steps: severity first, then the risk priority number. Any failure mode scored 9 or 10 on severity (someone could get hurt, or a legal requirement is broken) needs an action, or a written reason the current controls are enough, whatever its other scores. Everything else is ranked by the risk priority number (RPN), the three scores multiplied, from 1 to 1,000. Treat the RPN as a ranking aid, not a measure: very different risks can share the same number, so never use a cut-off as the only test of whether to act. When an action is done, score the three again; the new RPN shows whether the risk really came down. The tool doesn't calculate the high, medium or low action-priority rating some industry standards use; if your customer requires that rating, apply their table to the same three scores.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- A process or product change is about to go live, and you want to think through what could go wrong before it does, not after.
- You have several plausible failure modes and need a defensible way to rank which ones actually deserve attention first.
- You want severity, occurrence, and detection scored explicitly and separately, not blended into one vague 'risk level' that hides which dimension is actually driving the concern.

Not when:

- A failure has already happened and you're investigating why — that's root cause analysis (an A3's 5-Whys), not a forward-looking risk analysis.
- You're comparing a small set of options against multiple weighted criteria, not ranking failure modes — a weighted decision matrix is the right tool for that.
- The severity, occurrence, or detection scores would just be guesses with no real basis — an FMEA built on invented scores ranks risks in the wrong order just as confidently as one built on real judgement.

## How to fill it in

- **processOrProduct** — Name the process, product or system this covers, and where it starts and ends. Keep it narrow enough that the team can list its failure modes in one sitting.
- **items** — For each step or part, write what could go wrong, its effect, the likely cause and the controls in place today, then score severity, occurrence and detection from 1 to 10. Score with the people who do the work, using real defect or incident records where you have them. The risk priority number is calculated from your three scores — don't type it; check the highest ones match what the team worries about most.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences on the biggest risks: any item with a severity of 9 or 10 first, whatever its risk priority number, then the few with the highest numbers. Name the action that would bring each one down, and once actions are done, whether the re-scored numbers show the risk actually fell.

## Common mistakes

- **Ranking purely by risk priority number and ignoring a high-severity failure mode because its number is low.** — A failure that could hurt someone but rarely happens can score lower than a nuisance that happens every week. Severity 9 or 10 gets an action, or a written reason the controls are enough, regardless of the number — the same reason current FMEA practice weights severity most.
- **Setting a risk priority number cut-off, such as "act on anything over 100".** — The number isn't a true measure of risk — the scales aren't evenly spaced and many different score combinations give the same product. A fixed cut-off invites nudging a score down by one to get under the line instead of reducing the risk.
- **Scoring severity, occurrence, and detection from a rough overall impression instead of thinking through each dimension separately.** — A failure mode that's severe but well-detected needs a different fix than one that's mild but invisible until it's too late — collapsing the three dimensions into one gut-feel number erases exactly the distinction that makes the risk priority number useful.
- **Treating every item on the list as equally worth fixing.** — Same vital-few discipline as every other artifact in this catalogue — a long list of scored risks with no ranking just moves the prioritization problem downstream instead of solving it.
- **Writing a recommended action that doesn't actually change severity, occurrence, or detection.** — "Communicate more clearly" or "be more careful" doesn't move any of the three numbers — a real recommended action targets one of them specifically (a physical control, a gate the process can't skip, a check that catches the failure earlier).

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding the real severity, occurrence, and detection scores for each failure mode
- Deciding whether a recommended action actually targets the risk it's linked to

Analysis — AI helps:

- Tightening raw failure-mode, effect, cause, and controls notes into specific, scannable language
- Drafting the narrative naming the vital-few highest-risk items
- Drafting a recommended action grounded in the notes given, never inventing a fix with no basis in them

Drudgery — automated:

- Computing the risk priority number from severity, occurrence, and detection, every time a score changes
- Ranking items by risk priority number
- Exporting to xlsx in the house format
