# Lean maturity scorecard

Score how well a handful of improvement practices are really working in one area, each on a five-level scale you describe as what you'd see at the workplace, with the evidence for every score, a target for a date and the next step. Gaps, averages and the scores that don't hold up (no evidence, a level nobody described, a target too far for a year) are computed, not typed in.

Family: Foundations · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A maturity scorecard answers a plain question: how well are the improvement practices we brought in actually working, day to day, on every shift? You pick a handful of practice areas that matter for this area's results, such as standard work, visual management, problem solving, leader routines and flow. For each one you write a five-level scale in your own words, as what you would see at the workplace: level 1 is not there yet, level 3 is in use where it was introduced, level 5 is the team improving it without being asked. Then a small group goes and looks, on every shift, reads the records, and agrees where each area is now, writing down the evidence for the score. Each area gets a target for a date, usually a year out, and one next step with an owner and a date. The scale is the important part: because each level is described as something you can see, two people scoring the same area reach the same answer, and next year's score can be compared with this year's. Without it, a score is a mood. Used this way, the scorecard isn't an exam. It shows where the practices are thin, so the next year's effort goes to the few areas that hold the rest back.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Several improvement practices have been brought in over the past months and you want an honest view of which are holding and which have faded.
- You're planning next year's improvement work and want to put effort where the gap is, not where the noise is.
- Practices look fine on the shift managers see and you suspect they don't on the others.
- You want a baseline now so that in a year you can show, with evidence, what changed.
- A new leader is taking over an area and needs a quick, shared picture of how it works.

Not when:

- You want to check whether one new standard is still being followed. A sustainment audit or a standard work audit does that in more detail.
- You want to rank people or teams against each other. Scores used to compare or reward get inflated, and then they tell you nothing.
- Nobody can go and look at the work. A scorecard filled in from a meeting room is opinion with numbers on it.
- The area has one urgent problem. Solve it first (an A3 or a 5-Whys); the scorecard is for the slower question of how the whole practice is developing.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Say which area the scorecard covers (a line, a team, an office process) and which shifts, who scores it together, and the date the targets are for. Score as a small group that includes people who do the work and the leaders who run it, never one manager alone. Write how it was scored: which walk, which records.
- **areas** — Pick four to eight practice areas that matter for this area's results. Standard work, visual management, problem solving, leader routines, daily huddles, flow and how ideas are raised are common choices. Fewer, well described, beat a long list: every extra area is five more levels to write and one more thing to work on.
- **levels** — Before anyone scores, write each area's five levels as what you would see. Level 1: it isn't there. Level 2: it's started, or done by a few. Level 3: in use where it was introduced. Level 4: it holds on every shift and on bad days, and leaders check it. Level 5: the team improves it themselves. Make each step something you could point to ("followed by cover operators too"), not a feeling ("good discipline"). Reuse the same scale next year, so the scores can be compared.
- **scores** — Go and look, on every shift, and read the records. Then score Now for each area as a group: the highest level whose description is fully true of what you saw, not the one you're working towards. Set a Target for the date: one level up in a year is usual, two is a stretch, more is rarely real.
- **evidence** — For every score, write what you saw that puts it at that level: what, where and when ("20 July 21:00 changeover skipped the timed flush"). Include what stopped it scoring higher. If you can't write the evidence, you haven't checked the score yet: go and look, then score.
- **results** — For each area, name the safety, quality, delivery or cost result the practice should move, with its numbers and dates ("changeover 46 minutes in April, about 35 now"), and say whether it is getting better, flat or worse. A practice only matters for what it does to results. A level 4 standard work score next to flat defect numbers is a scoring error, or a practice aimed at the wrong thing, and the sheet flags it. If nobody measures the result yet, say so: that is a finding too.
- **next** — For each area below its target, write the one next step that moves it, with one owner and a due date. One step each is enough; the next one comes when it's done. Put the steps where they'll be followed up: a leader's routine, the huddle, the monthly review.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the sheet works it out. For each area it gives the gap (target minus now) and flags a missing score or target, a scored level nobody described, a score with no evidence, a score of 4 or 5 whose result isn't getting better (or isn't named), a target below the score or more than two levels up, and a gap with no next step, owner or date. It gives the average level now and the average target, how many areas are at target, and the area with the largest gap, and draws now against target as a bar chart.
- **summary** — Write in a few sentences what the scores say and the one or two areas to work on first, and why those. The largest gap isn't always the right start: the practice that keeps the others alive often is. Score again at the target date, with the same scale and the same kind of walk.

## What good looks like

The example below is Line 2's scorecard of 6 August 2026, six months into the paint booth improvement work, scored by the plant manager with the line supervisor, both shift leads and scheduling after walking the line on both shifts. Six practice areas, each with its own five levels: standard work, visual management and flow are at 3, problem solving and leader routines at 2, and everyone improving at 1. That averages 2.3 now against 3.8 targeted for July 2027, and no area is at its target yet. The evidence shows the same pattern in several areas: the practices hold on day shift and fade on second shift. Two checks fire. Everyone improving has the largest gap, three levels from 1 to 4, and that target is more than two levels up in a year; the plant manager decides at the September review whether to aim for 3. Visual management was scored in the room with no evidence from the walk, so it gets checked on second shift before the score stands. Each area names the result it should move, and where the practices hold the results agree: changeovers down from 46 to about 35 minutes, old-colour rejects from 31 to 2 in four weeks, on-time shipments from 81% to 94%. Nobody logs how long packing waits for painted parts, so visual management's result is a gap in itself. Every area below target has a next step with an owner and a date, and the team starts with leader routines, two levels to go, because a routine is what keeps the other practices alive.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoring from the meeting room.** — People score what they intended, or the shift they see. Walk the area on every shift and read the records first; a score you can't write evidence for isn't a score yet.
- **Levels described as feelings.** — "Good", "strong", "mature" mean different things to everyone. Describe each level as something you'd see, so two people scoring the same area agree, and next year's score means the same as this year's.
- **Targets that jump three levels.** — Practices become habits slowly. A level a year is usual, two is a stretch. A target nobody can reach makes the scorecard a list of failures by spring.
- **Scores that the results don't back up.** — A practice can look right on the walk and still not move anything: the routine is kept but aimed at the wrong problem, or kept only when someone is watching. Put each score next to the result it should move. A high score with flat results is the most useful thing the scorecard can show you.
- **Too many areas.** — Fifteen areas means fifteen half-done next steps. Pick the four to eight that matter for this area's results, and work on the one or two that hold the rest back.
- **Using scores to compare or reward teams.** — Once a score affects someone's standing, it goes up without the practice changing. Keep the scorecard for the team's own improvement.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Go-see protocol** (`go-see-protocol`) — A planned walk on every shift is how the scores get their evidence: what to look at in each practice area, and what was seen.
- **Standard work audit** (`standard-work-audit`) — An audit of one standard gives hard evidence for the standard work score: how many of the steps were followed, on which shifts.

Downstream:

- **Leader standard work** (`leader-standard-work`) — Next steps often become a check on a leader's routine, and the leader routines area is usually scored from those routine sheets.
- **Improvement kata storyboard** (`improvement-kata-storyboard`) — The area with the gap that matters most becomes a target condition the team works towards in small experiments.
- **Hoshin kanri X-matrix** (`hoshin-kanri`) — The few practice gaps that hold results back belong in next year's improvement priorities, with owners and measures.

Analysis — AI helps:
