# Value proposition map

Check how well what you offer fits what a group of buyers needs. On one side, the jobs they are trying to do, the pains in the way and the gains they want, each ranked and backed by evidence. On the other, what you offer and how each offer eases a named pain or creates a named gain, with proof. The checks are computed: top pains or gains nothing answers, answers to pains nobody has shown you, claims with no proof.

Family: Voice of the customer · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A value proposition map puts two lists side by side and draws lines between them. The customer's side describes one group of buyers: the jobs they are trying to get done, the pains that get in the way (time lost, money lost, risk, frustration) and the gains they want beyond the pain going away. Each one is ranked by how much it matters to them and carries its evidence: the call, the record or the number it came from. Our side lists what you actually offer today, then links each offer to the pain it eases or the gain it creates, saying how, with the proof that it works. The lines are the point. A top pain with no line to it is a gap in the offer or in the message. A line to a pain nobody has shown you is a feature looking for a problem. A line with no proof is a claim, however confident it sounds. Done honestly, the map tells you which parts of your offer you can stand behind with these buyers today, and which you are still hoping about.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You have real evidence from several buyers in one group (calls, a closed-lost review, a trial) and want to see how well the offer fits it before writing a standing message.
- Sales, marketing and product describe the value differently, and you want one picture of what buyers need and what actually answers it.
- A feature gets demoed a lot but nobody can say which buyer problem it solves.
- You are moving into a new group of buyers and want to test whether the jobs, pains and gains you know still hold for them.

Not when:

- You have one deal in front of you and need its numbers. Use a value hypothesis for that buyer; the map is for a group of buyers.
- You have no buyer evidence yet. Run customer interviews first; a map filled in from your own assumptions just restates the pitch.
- You need the words reps and the website will use. That is a message architecture, built from this map once the fit is proven.

## How to fill it in

- **segment** — Name one group of buyers who share the same jobs: a role in a kind of company, not 'everyone who might buy'. Two groups with different pains need two maps. Say where the evidence came from: which calls, records or trials.
- **jobs** — List what these buyers are trying to get done, in their words, not your product's: 'run a weekly pipeline review leadership believes', not 'use dashboards'. Rank each one High, Medium or Low, where High means it is one of the few things that decide whether they act. Write where you heard it.
- **pains** — List what gets in the way of those jobs or makes them costly. Be specific, and put the buyer's own number on it where they gave you one ('about 4 hours a week'). Rank each and write the evidence. If nobody has told you or shown you, leave the evidence blank: the map will flag it as an assumption rather than let it pass as fact.
- **gains** — List the outcomes they want, beyond the pain going away: 'hear about a quiet deal while there's still time to save it'. Same ranking, same evidence. Keep pains and gains separate; a gain is something they would pay for even if nothing hurt.
- **offers** — List the products, features and services you actually provide today, one per row. Not the roadmap: a promise can't ease a pain this quarter. A service counts, such as building the business case with the buyer.
- **eases** — One row per link: pick the offer (O#) and the pain (P#), and say how it eases that pain, specifically. Then write the proof that it does: a measured result, how many, from where. One customer's result is proof, but say it is one. If you only believe it works, leave proof blank.
- **creates** — The same for gains: which offer creates which gain (O# and G#), how, and the proof. A claim such as 'forecasts land within 5% of actual' needs a measured result behind it, or it stays blank and gets flagged.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the map is checked for you. Each pain and gain shows how many links answer it and how many of those are proven, and flags a high-ranked one nothing answers, one not ranked and one with no evidence. Each link shows the pain or gain it points at, and flags one with no offer from the list, one pointing at a need with no evidence, and one with no proof. An offer that answers nothing is flagged too. The fit counts top pains and gains answered with proof.
- **nextStep** — For each gap, decide: close it (build or test something), prove it (go and measure), or stop claiming it. Name who does what by when. The decisions matter more than the map.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' map for sales and revenue operations leaders at mid-market freight companies, made on 2 April 2026 from Northline Freight's discovery call, business case and 30-day trial and Harbor Line Freight's first calls. Three jobs, five pains, four gains, five offers and eight links (four easing pains, four creating gains). Two of the three top pains are eased with proof: deals flagged within 48 hours (41 of 44 in the trial) and the weekly review built from the customer relationship management (CRM) system (the RevOps lead's estimate of under 30 minutes a week, with a before-and-after time log due on 24 April). Both top gains are created with proof, the same estimate backs the review being ready in minutes, and 7 of the 8 links have some proof. The checks flag four rows. The third top pain, a team that won't act on a score it can't trace, has nothing easing it. 'Reps don't log calls' has no evidence, so the email logging that answers it is a feature looking for a problem. And the 5% forecast-accuracy claim has no proof. The next step deals with each: design and test a view of the activity behind each flag, take the forecast claim out of the deck, stop demoing email logging to these buyers.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling in the customer's side from the product.** — If every pain happens to match a feature, the map was written backwards. Start from what buyers said and did, in their words, before you look at what you sell. The pains with nothing easing them are the most useful thing on the page.
- **Treating every pain as equally important.** — A map with fifteen unranked pains can't tell you what to fix. Rank them, and look hardest at the few marked High: a top pain nothing eases matters far more than a minor one.
- **Writing a claim as if it were proof.** — 'Saves time' with nothing measured behind it is a hope. Leave the proof blank until you have a result, and say how many customers it comes from. The map is only useful if it shows which answers you can stand behind.
- **Drawing lines that are only loosely related.** — An offer 'eases' a pain only if it removes or reduces that specific pain. A link that needs a paragraph to justify it usually isn't one. Say how, in one sentence, or drop the link.
- **One map for every kind of buyer.** — Different buyers have different jobs and pains, and a map that averages them fits nobody. Make one per group, and keep the evidence for each separate.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Customer interview guide** (`customer-interview-guide`) — The jobs, pains and gains come from talking to buyers. Interviews fill the customer's side with evidence instead of assumptions, and a pain the map flags with no evidence becomes the next interview question.
- **Value hypothesis** (`value-hypothesis`) — A value hypothesis works one buyer's needs into numbers. Several of them, for buyers in the same group, are the best evidence for this map's customer side.

Downstream:

- **Message architecture** (`message-architecture`) — Once the fit is clear, the top pains and gains answered with proof become the message pillars. Gaps the map flags stay out of the message until they are closed.
- **Proof point inventory** (`proof-point-inventory`) — Each link's proof belongs in the inventory, where it is checked, cleared for outside use and refreshed as more customers add evidence.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding which buyers the map is for, and which pains really decide whether they buy
- Judging whether an offer genuinely eases a pain or only sounds related
- Deciding what to do about each gap: build, prove or stop claiming

Analysis — AI helps:

- Pulling candidate jobs, pains and gains, with quotes, out of call notes and transcripts
- Spotting links whose proof comes from a single customer or is out of date
- Suggesting the evidence still needed for each flagged row

Drudgery — automated:

- Counting links and proof for every pain and gain
- Keeping the ids and links straight as rows are added
- Laying the map out and exporting it as a workbook
