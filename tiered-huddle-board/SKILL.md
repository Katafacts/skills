# Tiered huddle board

Connect short standing huddles level by level: each tier reads its own board of a few measures against target, sends up only what it cannot fix, and logs the answer that comes back down.

Family: Daily management · Format: .xlsx · Domain: core

## What it is

A cascade of short standing huddles, from the team at the work up to site leadership, held in order each morning. Each tier has its own board: the few measures it can act on, a target, the latest number, and what is being done about anything off target. What a tier cannot fix goes up to the next tier on a written condition anyone can check, and the answer comes back down, logged with its date. The boards make each huddle worth holding; the answer coming back makes the cascade worth having. A problem that goes up and never returns teaches the floor to stop raising things.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- Problems regularly need decisions from above the team that sees them, and you want them to travel up and back the same day rather than wait for a weekly meeting.
- Each level needs its own few numbers: the floor this shift, the operation over the last 7 days, the site this month.
- You want to see whether escalations actually get answered, and how fast.

Not when:

- There is only one level of review. A single huddle is not tiered: run a daily management huddle instead.
- The tiers cannot meet in order on the same day. If the top tier meets weekly, write it as a review, not a tier, and do not route same-day problems through it.

## How to fill it in

- **tiers** — List each tier from the front line up: its name, when it meets and for how long, who attends, a named owner, what it reviews, and the condition that sends an issue up. Set the times so the tiers meet in order, each soon after the one below, and the top tier early enough to act the same day: an escalation that arrives after the cutoff it was about is history, not an escalation. Write the condition so anyone could apply it, such as "a pick error reaching a packed order, or the queue more than 45 minutes behind", not "serious issues".
- **board** — Give each tier three to five measures it can act on at its own altitude: close to the work means short horizons (this shift, today), higher up means trends (the last 7 days, month to date). For each, write the unit, which way is better and the target. Before each huddle, type the latest number and the status follows. Anything off target gets a line on what is being done and who has it; an off-target line with nothing against it is the first thing to own at the huddle. If a measure never moves anyone to act, take it off the board.
- **escalations** — Write one line each time a tier cannot fix something: the date, from which tier to which, and what went up, as a fact about the work. When the answer comes back, write it and the date it reached the tier that raised it, not the date someone upstairs decided. The log counts what is still waiting and the longest wait. Treat anything still waiting after the next meeting of the tier it went to as that tier's miss, and say so at its huddle.
- **narrative** — Write a few sentences: how the tiers meet in order, what the boards show off target, what went up and what came back, and the gaps to own: an off-target line with nothing against it, a tier with no owner, an escalation still waiting.

## What good looks like

The example below is a warehouse's outbound fulfillment cascade. Three tiers meet in order every morning: the pick/pack huddle at 6:45 (and at 6:45pm for nights), the fulfillment ops huddle at 7:15, and site leadership at 8:00, so anything raised on the floor can reach the site director long before the 4:00pm carrier cutoff. Every tier has a named owner and a board of four measures. Five of the twelve are off target, and zone C mis-picks show up at every tier with an action at each. It is Thursday 12 February, so the floor reads this shift, ops the last 7 days and site leadership month to date. Four things went up in the last ten days and three answers came back within a day, including a second carrier collection yesterday that got all 34 at-risk orders out. Two gaps stand out: overtime at the ops huddle is 74 hours against 60 with nothing written against it, and a request for a second night checker has waited six days for an answer from site leadership while zone C mis-picks continue.

## Common mistakes

- **Escalations that never get an answer back** — The floor judges the whole cascade by what comes back down. An escalation that disappears upstairs teaches people that raising things is a waste of breath, and the cascade goes quiet. Log every answer and its date, and read the waiting ones out at the tier they went to.
- **Tiers that meet out of order** — If the top tier meets before the one below it, or in the afternoon after the day's cutoffs, every escalation arrives a day late. Set the times bottom up, each tier soon after the one below, so a problem raised at 6:45 can be decided by mid-morning.
- **Every tier reading the same numbers, just in a bigger room** — Each tier up should see trends and patterns the tier below surfaced, over a longer horizon. If the board does not change between tiers, the extra meeting is overhead with no leverage.
- **An escalation condition like "when it becomes a problem"** — Nobody can check it, so nobody can tell when something should have gone up and did not. Write a threshold or an event anyone at the board could apply.
- **Naming a placeholder owner to make every tier look assigned** — A tier with a nominal owner is worse than an honestly empty one: it hides the gap, and the escalations sent to it go unanswered with nobody noticing.

## What it connects to

Upstream:

- **Daily management huddle** (`daily-huddle`) — The meeting each tier runs: its timebox, its triage, and the queue numbers that show whether it still works.
- **Escalation matrix** (`escalation-matrix`) — For a specific process, says what severity goes to whom and how fast; a tier's escalation condition often comes straight from it.
- **RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) chart** (`raci-chart`) — Says whose decision an escalated problem is, so it goes to the right tier first time.
- **Key performance indicator (KPI) tree** (`kpi-tree`) — Where each tier's board measures come from: the drivers of the top-level measure, scoped to each tier's altitude.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Deciding who is the right owner for each tier
- Deciding whether an escalation condition is realistic for how the place actually works
- Choosing the few measures each tier can genuinely act on

Analysis — AI helps:

- Drafting each tier's review categories and a checkable escalation condition from a rough description
- Spotting tiers whose meeting times put escalations past the cutoffs they are about
- Reading the escalation log for answers that never came back and tiers that are slow to reply

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out each board line's status against target
- Counting answered and waiting escalations, the longest wait and the average time to an answer
- Exporting to xlsx in the house format
