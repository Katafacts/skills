# Marketing maturity assessment

Score how well the main parts of a marketing team's work really function (demand generation, content and messaging, operations and data, measurement, the handoff to sales, customer insight), each on a five-level scale you describe as what you'd find in the plans, the CRM (customer relationship management system) and the reports, with the evidence, a target for a date and the next step. Gaps, averages and the scores that don't hold up are computed.

Family: Foundations · Format: .xlsx · Domain: commercial

## What it is

A marketing maturity assessment answers a plain question for a marketing leader: which parts of how we do marketing really work, and which only look as if they do? You pick the handful of areas that matter for your results, such as demand generation, content and messaging, marketing operations and data, measurement and attribution, the handoff to sales, and customer insight. For each one you write a five-level scale in your own words, as things you could point to: level 1 is not there yet, level 3 is in place and used, level 5 is the team testing and improving it. Then the team gathers the evidence (the plan, the CRM, the campaign and pipeline reports, a sit-in on the pipeline review) and agrees where each area is now, writing down what it found. Each area gets a target for a date, usually a year out, and one next step with an owner and a date. The scale is what makes it useful: because each level is something you can check, two people scoring the same area reach the same answer, and next year's score can be compared with this year's. Without it, a score is a mood. A team can hit its lead target while the handoff to sales leaks and channel credit rests on a rule nobody chose; the assessment is how those show up.

## When to use it

Use it when:

- You're planning next year's marketing priorities and want effort to go where the gap is, not where the noise is.
- Lead numbers look healthy and pipeline or revenue doesn't follow, and you want to see which part of the work is thin.
- A new marketing leader needs a quick, shared, evidenced picture of how the team works.
- You want a baseline now so that in a year you can show, with evidence, what changed.

Not when:

- One number is off and you want to know why. Work it as a problem (a key performance indicator (KPI) tree to find where, then a root cause method); the assessment is for the slower question of how the practices are developing.
- You want to rank people, agencies or teams against each other. Scores that affect standing get inflated, and then they tell you nothing.
- Nobody will look at the evidence. An assessment filled in from memory in a workshop is opinion with numbers on it.

## How to fill it in

- **scope** — Say which team the assessment covers and which handoffs (to sales, to customer success), who scored it together and the date the targets are for. The marketing leader owns it; score as a small group with the people who do the work and someone from sales, never the leader alone. Write what the scores were based on: which reports, which meetings.
- **areas** — Pick four to eight marketing areas that matter for your results. Demand generation, content and messaging, marketing operations and data, measurement and attribution, the handoff to sales and customer insight are common choices; brand, partner marketing or events belong only if they matter this year. Fewer, well described, beat a long list.
- **levels** — Before anyone scores, write each area's five levels as things you could find. Level 1: it isn't there. Level 2: started, or done by one person. Level 3: in place, written down and used. Level 4: measured and kept up on busy weeks too. Level 5: the team tests it and improves it. Make each step checkable ("a reason code on every rejected lead"), not a feeling ("strong alignment with sales"). Reuse the same scale next year.
- **scores** — Gather the evidence first, then score Now for each area as a group: the highest level whose description is fully true of what you found, not the one you're working towards. Set a Target for the date: one level up in a year is usual, two is a stretch, more is rarely real.
- **evidence** — For every score, write what you found that puts it at that level: what, where and when ("loss reason blank on 23 of 31 deals closed lost this half"). Include what stopped it scoring higher. If you can't write the evidence, you haven't checked the score yet.
- **next** — For each area below its target, write the one next step that moves it, with one owner and a due date. One step each is enough; the next comes when it's done. Put the steps where they'll be followed up: the weekly marketing meeting or the monthly review.
- **checks** — Nothing to enter here: the sheet works it out. For each area it gives the gap (target minus now) and flags a missing score or target, a scored level nobody described, a score with no evidence, a target below the score or more than two levels up, and a gap with no next step, owner or date. It gives the average level now and the average target, how many areas are at target, and the area with the largest gap, and draws now against target as a bar chart.
- **summary** — Write in a few sentences what the scores say and the one or two areas to work on first, and why those. The largest gap isn't always the right start: the area every lead passes through, such as the handoff to sales, often is. Score again at the target date, with the same scale and the same kind of evidence.

## What good looks like

The example below is Beacon Analytics' marketing maturity assessment of 22 July 2026, led by the head of marketing with marketing operations, demand generation, revenue operations and a sales manager, from the CRM, the campaign reports, the metric definition sheet, the June attribution check and the 8 July marketing KPI tree. Six marketing areas, each with its own five levels: demand generation and marketing operations and data are at 3, content and messaging, measurement and attribution, and the sales handoff at 2, and customer insight at 1. That averages 2.2 now against 3.8 targeted for June 2027, and no area is at its target yet. The team plans campaigns reasonably well; what happens to leads afterwards is weak. Two checks fire. Customer insight has the largest gap, three levels from 1 to 4, and that target is more than two levels up in a year; the head of marketing decides at the September review whether to aim for 3. Content and messaging was scored in the room with no evidence, so the assets get pulled before the 2 stands. Every area below target has a next step with an owner and a date, and the team starts with the sales handoff, two levels to go, because every lead passes through it and time to first contact is getting worse.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoring from the lead numbers.** — Healthy lead counts say nothing about whether sales follows them up or whether the credit between channels is right. Score each area from its own evidence: the handoff from contact times and rejected leads, measurement from how the attribution rule was chosen.
- **Levels described as feelings.** — "Data-driven", "strong brand" and "aligned with sales" mean different things to everyone. Describe each level as something you could find in a report or the CRM, so two people agree and next year's score means the same as this year's.
- **Scoring tools instead of practice.** — Buying a new platform doesn't move an area up a level; using it the same way every week does. Score what the team does with the tools, and keep tools out of the level descriptions.
- **Targets that jump three levels.** — Practices become habits slowly. A level a year is usual, two is a stretch; a target nobody can reach turns the assessment into a list of failures by spring.

## Where AI helps

Judgement — stays with you:

- Agreeing as a group which level matches what was found
- Choosing which one or two areas to work on first
- Deciding how far a target can realistically go in a year

Analysis — AI helps:

- Turning vague level descriptions into things you could find in a report or the CRM
- Spotting evidence that doesn't support the score beside it
- Comparing this year's scores with last year's, area by area

Drudgery — automated:

- Working out gaps, averages and the largest gap
- Flagging scores with no evidence and targets too far away
- Drawing the chart and exporting the assessment as a workbook
